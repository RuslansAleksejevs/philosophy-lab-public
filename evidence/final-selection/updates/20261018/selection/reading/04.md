# What Foreknowledge Can Take Away

A reader learns a novel’s ending before opening it. She subsequently enjoys the novel more than she would have enjoyed an uninformed reading: knowing the destination lets her appreciate the construction. Yet she regrets learning it early. This need not be an error about how much pleasure she received. She may have lost a valuable way of arriving at her understanding, even while gaining a better experience overall.

The distinction matters beyond spoiler etiquette. If aesthetic value resides partly in what appreciators do, the order in which information reaches them can determine which valuable activities remain possible. An artwork can remain intact while one of its aesthetic opportunities disappears for a particular person. Conversely, the disappearance of that opportunity need not diminish the value of the person’s subsequent encounter on balance.

I defend a limited claim: some works offer an aesthetically valuable opportunity to discover, through their unfolding form, how an explanation one reasonably favored turned out to be false. Foreknowledge can eliminate that opportunity. Its loss is distinct both from having information imposed without consent and from enjoying the work less. Recognizing it supports a defeasible reason to sequence explanations after initial engagement, but no general prohibition on advance knowledge.

## 1. Two questions about spoilers

Neil Levy distinguishes the effect of foreknowledge from our objection to having it thrust upon us. He proposes that much spoiler aversion concerns epistemic autonomy. He also expressly recognizes twist and puzzle stories as cases where foreknowledge can remove something enjoyable. These qualifications matter: merely announcing that spoilers interfere with discovery would repeat ground already covered. The question here is whether an aesthetically valuable opportunity can be lost independently of both an autonomy violation and a net hedonic loss. This is a question about grounds for regret, rather than a general explanation of the intensity of spoiler aversion. ([Levy, §§1, 3](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/29960126.2025.2527423).)

Consider three distinct judgments. “You had no right to tell me” concerns another person’s conduct. “I would have enjoyed this more without knowing” compares pleasures. “I no longer have the chance to arrive at this understanding in that way” concerns an opportunity. These judgments often coincide, but none simply entails either of the others. A reader can request a plot summary, rationally prefer the informed experience, and still acknowledge something forgone. Another reader can resent an unsolicited summary of a work she has no interest in experiencing. The latter resentment does not by itself establish an aesthetic loss.

To separate the questions, start with an accidental disclosure involving no objectionable agent. A reader opens an electronic book; a software fault momentarily displays the final page. Set aside inconvenience and suppose no one was negligent. Something may still have been lost. Now suppose she intentionally opened that page because, after considering the options, she reasonably wanted a guided reading. The same opportunity may disappear, though she has no complaint against anyone and no reason to reverse her decision. The value of a foregone option does not depend on the choice being wrong.

But this establishes little until we explain what the option is and why it is aesthetically valuable. Almost every event excludes unrepeatable alternatives. Irreversibility is not a source of value by itself.

## 2. A viewer implicated by the form

Consider an invented short film with two presentations of the same encounter. Initially, distant shots show a woman standing outside a building, looking at its entrance, checking her watch, and concealing something in her hand. The editing cuts away whenever she begins to move. There is no threatening act and no private glimpse of a criminal plan. Nevertheless, the sequence gives a viewer reasonable grounds to suspect that the film is establishing surveillance. Later, a sustained shot follows her interrupted movements. She holds a letter, repeatedly starts toward the door, and retreats. She is rehearsing how to approach an estranged family member.

The initial shots then return. This time their durations extend beyond the earlier cuts: the apparently decisive watch-check ends in an abandoned approach; the hidden object is the letter she cannot bring herself to deliver. The repeated composition invites the viewer to locate her changed understanding in the precise intervals that editing previously withheld. The film need not imply that the viewer was foolish or morally blameworthy. It shows how an economical sequence of apparently diagnostic details supported one explanation by concealing the continuation that would undermine it.

This construction does more than reveal a family relationship. It puts the viewer’s way of making sense of the images among the film’s subjects. The withheld continuations are not merely background facts now supplied. Their return makes an earlier economy of attention perceptible: a gesture had become evidence of menace when the viewer interpreted it within the interval the film supplied. The expressive movement from threat to hesitation is joined to a reconsideration of how the film elicited that estimate.

Three attitudes must be distinguished. A viewer can believe that the woman is a relative, imagine her as a possible threat within the sequence, and still find her posture menacing. Knowledge need not eliminate either the imaginative role or the felt aspect. Neither of those is the particular attitude at issue here. The relevant attitude is the viewer’s actual, revisable estimate of what explains the depicted conduct: a judgment about the fiction, not a belief that this woman exists outside it. It may be a confident belief or only a provisional preference among explanations.

Suppose a viewer reasonably assigns surveillance a probability of 0.6 on the initial evidence. The later sequence gives her reason to abandon that explanation. It does not show that 0.6 was too high when assigned. A reasonable estimate can favor an explanation that turns out false; learning which possibility occurred does not retrospectively make the earlier confidence irrational. Here, “correction” means replacing a previously favored account of the situation after its falsity becomes clear. It need not mean correcting a defect in the viewer’s reasoning. The example requires an actual interpretive commitment, but neither certainty nor overconfidence.

Now compare that viewer with one who reliably knows the explanation and continues to regard it as settled. Both may find the first sequence menacing, shift toward vulnerability, and appreciate the editing. Only the former can discover that the explanation she herself favored was false. The latter can reconstruct that revision without making it. This is a narrow exclusion. Foreknowledge may leave many other estimates unsettled, and if the viewer doubts the disclosure, the relevant correction can remain possible.

## 3. Why this particular correction can matter

A difference between activities is not yet a difference in value. The argument therefore needs an evaluative premise: when a work successfully makes a manner of interpretation part of its expressive subject, recognizing one’s own participation in that manner can be a worthwhile mode of appreciating the work. Its value lies in a particular integration. What the work shows about interpretation becomes perceptible through the appreciator’s own interpretive activity and its revision. That integration can give the work’s treatment of its subject a personal force without making it necessary, superior, or morally improving.

The invented film offers this possibility through duration, repetition, and gesture. The returned shots make the viewer’s earlier estimate available for comparison with what the extended intervals now disclose. She does not merely learn a general lesson that appearances can mislead. She recognizes how these cuts led her to read this stillness as surveillance, and how continuing the same gestures makes hesitation visible. The revelation concerns both the woman’s predicament and the viewer’s use of the film’s visual evidence. A mere announcement of the right answer would not achieve this relation.

A real literary case helps test the claim. In Austen’s *Emma*, Mr. Elton’s enthusiasm for a portrait of Harriet seems to Emma to express love for its subject. Yet his praise also repeatedly singles out Emma’s skill as its painter. After his proposal to Emma, the same behavior takes on a different explanation. The carriage scene briefly carries the defeated description into the disclosure itself: “Mr. Elton, the lover of Harriet, was professing himself her lover.” The phrase naming him as Harriet’s lover belongs to the understanding now collapsing. In the next chapter Emma returns to the picture and other signs that had seemed to support it. The narrative supplies both the earlier interpretation and the means of reconsidering it. ([Austen, volume I, chapters VI, XV–XVI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/158/158-h/158-h.htm).)

Consider a reader who provisionally favored Emma’s explanation. On reaching the proposal, she may find that a description she had accepted as a guide to the scene was Emma’s interested construction. The comedy of misinterpretation now includes the reader’s own uptake: she had helped turn the painter’s interpretation of an admirer into her account of his intentions. Reconsidering the portrait scene can make the earlier mixture of observation and inference newly audible. The reader experiences the narrative’s movement into and out of Emma’s perspective through a corresponding movement of her own judgment.

This is an interpretive possibility, not a report about every first reader. Austen gives warnings about Emma’s judgment and clues to Elton’s intentions. A reader can notice those clues early and enjoy the irony throughout. Nor must a reader who initially favors Harriet share Emma’s certainty or social prejudices; whether a particular degree of confidence was warranted depends on the evidence then noticed and available. The case shows how an actual revision could fit the work’s form. It does not certify the rationality of every mistaken reading or make being deceived a condition of reading well.

Why value that fit? Here the work gives the reader an opportunity to appreciate its handling of perspective by discovering what that handling has done in her own reading. The placement of praise, the temporary adoption of Emma’s descriptions, and their later exposure form a controlled relation between what the narrative depicts and how it is received. Recognizing that relation from within an actual change of judgment is a way of appreciating the narrative’s expressive success. Its object is not simply “I was wrong.” It is the way these sentences made an interpretation available, let it organize the scene, and then made its operation visible. That is a reason grounded in the work’s construction, rather than in the bare fact that an event happened to this reader.

The contrast cases matter. If the invented film initially displays fabricated evidence of a crime and then retracts it through an unexplained announcement, there may be surprise without any rewarding exposure of how its form guided interpretation. If a viewer suspects surveillance solely because she confused the actor with someone from another film, her correction need reveal nothing about the present construction. Likewise, correcting a misremembered name in *Emma* would not automatically realize the good just described. Actual error is neither sufficient nor intrinsically valuable. What matters is a successful expressive relation between interpretation, its revision, and the work’s treatment of that activity.

The strongest objection is that an informed reader can appreciate exactly those features. She can notice the double direction of Elton’s compliments, feel the pull of Emma’s outlook, and imaginatively undergo its collapse. She may understand the novel better and respond more richly than the reader who was surprised. All of this should be granted. It shows that actual correction is unnecessary for excellent appreciation. It does not show that appreciating the construction through a real revision is worthless. Two modes can answer to the same artistic achievement without being interchangeable activities.

Still, mere nonidentity would not suffice. The positive reason is the integration just identified: the reader recognizes the narrative’s handling of perspective in an interpretation she actually used and relinquished. The relevant value is not an extra prize added whenever the biography behind an experience is authentic. It is the value of this mode of responsive participation. An informed encounter may preserve much of it through imagination and offer other goods that outweigh what is unavailable. The claim is that actual correction can be one worthwhile realization, not that it adds a fixed amount of value to an otherwise identical experience. A skeptic who finds no aesthetic significance in that enacted relation has located the substantive disagreement; neither surprise nor irreversibility settles it.

This defense has close precedents. Fish treats the reader’s temporary projections and their failure as constituents of the reading event that final understanding does not erase. That is a precedent about meaning and experience, not a proof that every such event is valuable. Iser likewise describes interpretation through anticipation and retrospection, including the reader’s entanglement in an organization she has helped produce. Their accounts rule out presenting the general thought of temporally enacted interpretation as a new discovery here. ([Fish, reprint p.86](https://affecthistory.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/el-fish-literature-in-the-reader.pdf); [Iser, pp.287, 296](https://ctlsites.uga.edu/eberle/wp-content/uploads/sites/78/2017/08/iser_readingProcess.pdf).)

Nguyen’s engagement account supplies a further general precedent: it values active investigation and interpretation rather than simply possession of correct aesthetic judgments. The present argument applies that thought to a particular activity whose conditions settled knowledge can remove. It does not establish the larger engagement theory, claim priority for valuing interpretive activity, or infer that receiving help is always a loss. The distinction between help and replacement will matter below. ([Nguyen, author manuscript, abstract](https://philpapers.org/archive/NGUAAA.pdf).)

Greater epistemic caution is therefore not aesthetically disqualifying. A viewer who evenly suspends judgment may enjoy other valuable routes through the film. She need not manufacture error in order to deserve the artwork. Even a viewer whose suspicion is corrected need not experience shame or pride. The good is participation in an expressive reorientation of her own understanding, not earning a reward for being clever or blame for being wrong.

## 4. What informed imagination preserves

Chasid distinguishes knowledge of fictional facts from the imaginative role governed by what a fiction presents as credible at successive stages. His discussion allows informed re-engagement even while arguing that foreknowledge can interfere with its fluency. That distinction blocks an easy inference from knowing the ending to being unable to follow the earlier perspective. ([Chasid, penultimate manuscript, abstract and p.13](https://philarchive.org/archive/CHATSP-14).)

The informed viewer in the film can accordingly imagine the threatening interpretation, feel its force, and respond to its transformation. Nothing here establishes that she must experience less suspense, absorption, or pleasure. She may also recognize her own susceptibility to the framing: the scene still looks menacing despite what she knows. This is itself a kind of self-discovery. It would be a mistake to describe informed viewing as merely detached observation of someone else’s potential response.

What settled foreknowledge excludes is more specific: discovering that one’s actual estimate of the woman’s situation favored the wrong explanation. The informed viewer may undergo an aspectual change without that correction. If her interpretation nevertheless becomes genuinely unsettled, then she can realize the correction too; the proposed exclusion no longer applies. The thesis is about a condition that knowledge can sustain, not an exceptionless psychological law about what every spoiler does.

The result remains neutral between two accounts of aesthetic value. An experiential account can value the distinctive felt character of revising one’s own earlier interpretation, regardless of whether that recognition maximizes pleasure. A relational account can additionally value the actual history of responsiveness that the feeling registers. The argument does not establish what would happen if the two encounters were identical in every experiential respect. It requires only that a more pleasurable informed encounter can lack a valuable mode of engagement available without settled foreknowledge. Equal pleasure is not identical experience, and neither entails complete interchangeability.

Nor does the argument show that every attempt to reconstruct uncertainty is unsuccessful. Reconstruction can preserve important parts of the experience. It need not restore the exact activity it represents. Someone can vividly rehearse a decision she never actually faced; the rehearsal’s value does not make it the original act of deciding. Similarly, the informed viewer can appreciate a correction without it being a correction of that estimate of her own. If the first-person mode is valuable for the reasons just given, its absence is a loss of that opportunity, however good the reconstruction.

Forgetting can restore the opportunity. The temporal claim is therefore practical rather than absolute: replay alone normally cannot undo recently acquired knowledge. We ordinarily cannot choose to make a settled explanation genuinely open again. That asymmetry matters once a reason to value the alternative route has been established; it does not establish the reason by itself.

## 5. Realization, replacement, and foreclosure

The fate of an opportunity cannot be read directly from whether knowledge was self-generated. Three possibilities should be separated.

First, a viewer initially suspects surveillance, notices the poorly fitting details, and revises her estimate before the extended shots appear. She also recognizes how the earlier cuts encouraged her first reading. She has realized the relevant correction through attention to the work, even if she reaches it before the final disclosure. The extended shots may deepen her recognition of how that change occurred. Subsequent disclosure can confirm what she has discovered.

Second, a viewer infers estrangement immediately and never seriously favors surveillance. She realizes a different good: perceptive discovery of the explanation through the early presentation. Calling that a realization of the correction would be inconsistent, since she corrects no estimate. Her discovery may prevent that particular correction while supplying an equally good or better interpretive activity. The absence of regret is unsurprising. There is a real distinction between losing an option and making a worse choice.

Third, a confident answer occurs to a viewer through a lucky association unrelated to the film’s evidence. She treats it as settled. The correction may be foreclosed without either of those work-responsive activities being realized. This does not establish that her eventual encounter is worse. It shows only that self-generation is no guarantee that the relevant opportunity was used rather than closed.

External assistance cuts across the same categories. A companion who asks the viewer to reconsider where a shot ends can help her realize the correction. A requested explanation can replace it with a valuable informed encounter. An unsolicited answer can foreclose it while also violating the viewer’s wishes. These are different evaluations, not different names for a single wrong. The activity’s actual structure matters more than whether its ingredients arrived from outside.

There is consequently no automatic verdict about artistic defects. If a viewer correctly anticipates the situation, that need not make the film too predictable. If a disclosure removes one opportunity, the work can remain excellent and many other routes remain open. The thing lost is a possible relation between this viewer’s estimate and the unfolding presentation, not an intrinsic property scraped off the film.

## 6. The modest priority of an uncompleted route

What follows for criticism and presentation? Where both informed and uninformed engagement are attractive, an uninformed encounter can normally be followed by informed reconsideration. The reverse sequence cannot normally recover the initial live question. This gives an initial encounter a kind of option-preserving priority.

The qualification “where both are attractive” prevents a false dominance argument. Time and attention are scarce. An unsupported viewer may abandon a difficult work, become confused in an unrewarding way, or simply prefer the richer first encounter that explanation enables. Starting without help can also influence later appreciation adversely. It is false that uninformed-then-informed experience always contains everything obtainable through informed experience alone.

The defensible recommendation is conditional. When an appreciator has reason to value both routes, can realistically take both, and loses little by delaying decisive information, preserving the live interpretive question has an aesthetic justification. A critic can therefore give orientation before giving resolution: direct attention to relevant features, then offer a settled interpretation when the reader chooses it. This is not an instruction to maximize ignorance. Good orientation makes a valuable question tractable; withholding needed context can make it unintelligible.

The same distinction guards against paternalism. Preserving an option is not a mandate to impose it. A reader who knowingly chooses advance explanation may sacrifice a real good for a greater one. Respecting that decision is compatible with describing the sacrifice accurately. Calling every disclosure “spoiling” obscures both the worth of guided engagement and the specificity of the opportunity forgone.

The proposed reason for valuing self-correction concerns more than the truth of the viewer’s final judgment or how pleasant the encounter is. Some works make the viewer’s own revision an apt mode of appreciation, while leaving other excellent modes available. Once that relation becomes unavailable, a later encounter can still be excellent, perhaps better overall. A lost way of appreciating need not make what remains a lesser work—or a lesser appreciation.
