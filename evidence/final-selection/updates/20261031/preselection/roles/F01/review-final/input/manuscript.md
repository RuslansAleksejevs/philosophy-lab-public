# Answerability Without Surveillance

A service may have a good reason not to record what it does. That does not settle how it should handle complaints about its performance. Permission to remain ignorant and permission to make someone else bear the consequences of ignorance are different permissions.

I defend a limited positive duty. An institution that undertakes obligations and makes people practically dependent on its handling of claims against it owes them a usable and fair arrangement for presenting those claims and obtaining what is owed. When relief remains conditional on disputed performance, this requires a usable hearing and a defensible rule for cases that permissible inquiry cannot resolve. It need not include a payment to every unresolved claimant. A fair hearing followed by justified nonpayment can discharge the duty. But confidentiality alone cannot justify either an unusable hearing or the residual rule.

The duty arises before any service failure is established. It concerns how an institution exercises power over existing claims, not a general obligation to insure people against uncertainty. Its content also limits its remedies: denial of an owed process is not automatically denial of an owed refund.

## 1. A confidential service

Consider a fictional cooperative providing confidential advice. Users pay a fee. The cooperative promises to return it if the adviser uses the session mainly to sell an unrelated private product instead of providing the requested advice. The refund depends on what happened, not simply on dissatisfaction.

Sessions concern sensitive matters. Suppose that recording their contents would be wrong and that detailed institutional notes would recreate the same intrusion. Less intrusive records cannot reliably settle the relevant question. A timestamp shows that a conversation occurred, not whether it was a sales pitch. These are assumptions of the example, not findings about actual advice services. A user may nevertheless voluntarily describe the disputed conduct through a confidential complaint process.

The cooperative requires independent documentary proof for any refund. It categorically excludes the user's account. The adviser's denial defeats a complaint; there is no further review. Management defends this arrangement by invoking privacy. Suppose that confidential assessment of both accounts and a reviewer without a financial stake are practical at modest cost.

The problem is not that the cooperative lacks an impermissible recording. It demands a kind of proof its own legitimate service design normally excludes, then declines the permitted evidence it could consider. It gives its refund undertaking little practical effect in precisely the predictable disputes to which the undertaking applies.

Now change the case. The cooperative hears both accounts, permits relevant supporting information, and provides neutral review. Some complaints succeed; others fail; some remain balanced. The disclosed residual rule leaves balanced claims unpaid. A modest uncertainty benefit is affordable, but would increase fees and reward some false complaints.

The first arrangement is objectionable. The second is not objectionable merely because the additional benefit is affordable. Comparing them isolates two questions: whether people can genuinely contest performance, and whether the remaining risk is allocated fairly. A good answer to the first does not mechanically answer the second. Nor does a payment automatically provide either a hearing or an account of what occurred.

## 2. The narrower place of the argument

Several nearby theories already distinguish evidence, liability, and practical protection. My proposal is an application within that landscape, not a claim to have first discovered those distinctions.

Porat and Stein discuss special duties to protect or generate evidence, not merely its malicious destruction. They consider fault-independent cheapest-cost-avoider liability, ultimately favor negligence, and treat non-negligent evidential gaps as reciprocal. Their account also addresses proof burdens. I therefore do not infer a new duty simply by removing fault from evidential-damage liability. My question is what an institution owes in arranging claims when the decisive evidence was never owed. An extension of special relational duties might accommodate the answer. ([Porat and Stein, 1999 draft, Part IV(a), p.37 and n.92; V(d), pp.58–59; VI(b), pp.74–78](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2db3b43m/qt2db3b43m.pdf).)

Nissenbaum's contextual-integrity account distinguishes appropriate information from appropriate circulation. It also separates actual practice from justified practice. These distinctions explain why investigatory usefulness does not establish permission to collect information; they do not establish the particular prohibition stipulated here. ([Nissenbaum, 2004, III(A)–(C), pp.138–145](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/H.%20Nissenbaum,%20_Privacy%20as%20Contextual%20Integrity.pdf).)

Her earlier accountability essay connects producer control with responsibility, criticizes risk-shifting disclaimers, and warns against replacing answerability with compensation. Its discussion of strict liability removes the fault condition but retains causation. That is different from an uncertainty benefit paid without an established service failure. ([Nissenbaum, 1996, “Ownership without Liability” and “Restoring Accountability,” author-hosted PDF pp.11–15](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/accountability.pdf).) The distinction matters where an account of conduct is owed. It does not by itself establish such an entitlement after every fully paid financial claim.

The abstract of Stein's *On the Epistemic Authority of Courts* distinguishes epistemic assessment from morally and politically governed error allocation. I consulted that abstract, not the full article. It is sufficient to limit any claim that the general distinction is new, but not to establish a detailed agreement with his theory. ([Stein, 2008, institutional abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/on-the-epistemic-authority-of-courts/).)

Lambrecht's account addresses epistemic harms that remain after restoration and grounds reparations in a rights-violating wrong. The present design duty does not wait for wrongful noncollection or established service misconduct. A later remedy for violating that design duty does respond to a wrong, however; it is not reparation without wrongdoing. ([Lambrecht, 2026, §§3, 6–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02441-6).)

The proposed contribution is thus a constrained account of institutional claims under legitimate limits on evidence, together with limits on what follows from defective design. The selected passages consulted do not establish its historical novelty.

## 3. Why some arrangement is owed

An undertaking gives reasons to prepare for difficulty. That alone does not show that every useful preparation is obligatory. Gardner makes this distinction explicit: facilitative actions can be reason-supported without being individually obligatory. His necessary-means qualification does not settle which institutional arrangement is owed. ([Gardner, 2011, §5, author eprint typescript pp.37–38, n.53](https://johngardnerathome.info/pdfs/corrective.pdf).)

The missing premise concerns the institution's exercise of power:

**A party that undertakes an obligation and makes the other party practically dependent on its handling of claims owes a non-self-exempting exercise of that control. It must provide a usable way to present relevant claims and adopt decision rules for reasons that take the affected parties' legitimate interests seriously.**

“Non-self-exempting” means that control over recognition of a claim is not treated as permission to make one's own obligation ineffective for one's convenience. This is a relational moral premise, not a logical truth about promises. Three considerations support it.

First, a conditional obligation directs the institution's conduct whenever its condition is met. The right-holder's demand is therefore not a request for discretionary generosity. Uncertainty about whether the condition occurred does not convert the demand into such a request. The institution may reasonably withhold a contested benefit, but must distinguish withholding because the claim did not meet a defensible rule from withholding because management prefers not to be challenged.

Second, the institution's control makes that distinction its responsibility. It chooses admissible information, controls access to decision makers, and may make rejection practically final. In doing so it does more than decline to provide an optional service to strangers. It administers access to the performance it already owes in some cases. Unless its exercise of that power is constrained, the party owing the obligation can decide how little recognition the obligation receives. A declared conditional entitlement and a discretionary gift then become practically indistinguishable at the controller's option.

Third, reasonable safeguards constrain that power without turning a conditional refund into an unconditional one. Taking relevant testimony seriously, controlling conflicts, and defending a proof rule do not require deciding that every allegation is true. They recognize the standing of people entitled to demand performance if their claims are valid. The burden of providing such safeguards is attached to the power the institution organizes, rather than to proof of its employee's misconduct.

The alternative is revealing. Imagine a provider admitting that complaints could be assessed at little cost, yet declaring that it may withhold the disputed refunds while ignoring the complaints. It is not simply declining another useful precaution. It is asserting a unilateral exemption from having its own performance meaningfully questioned. An undertaking gives no such exemption. Where the provider makes people dependent on its judgment, it owes them constraints on that judgment.

This does not prove that every institution must create a tribunal. The duty is to secure some adequate route or limit its procedural control, not to select one particular mechanism. An accessible external process can provide the route. Small claims may be handled informally. Meaningful participant governance can help determine fair rules. These are alternative ways of meeting a requirement, not separately mandatory precautions.

Nor does practical dependence mean any inconvenience in seeking payment. It exists here when people cannot realistically secure the appropriate response without the institution's cooperation, including assessment where entitlement turns on disputed facts. A nominal right to pursue costly proceedings may offer little alternative for a small fee claim. An effective independent forum reduces that dependence. Membership in a cooperative does not itself remove it, but genuine influence over the rules can change its character.

The positive duty therefore has a different ground from a general duty to reduce uncertainty. It is a duty governing organized control over others' conditional claims. A dissenter may reject that relational constraint. Such a dissenter must explain why undertaking an obligation allows its bearer to make recognition of it entirely subject to unaccountable discretion, even when modest safeguards are available. Merely noting that preparation is not always obligatory does not answer this argument.

## 4. A completion condition

The relational premise would remain too vague if any explanation counted as compliance. Its requirements must follow the claims the institution still controls.

Consider a further version of the fictional service. It returns the entire fee promptly on a simple request from any verified participant. It corrects identification and payment errors, requires no waiver of other rights, and makes no finding about the adviser. Suppose that the only claim at issue is for return of the fee. The service need not assess session testimony to discharge this particular duty: it has removed disputed conduct as a barrier to receiving the full sum. A usable request channel and a way to correct mistakes remain necessary. Money has not explained what happened, but obtaining the fee no longer depends on settling that question. A further duty to explain conduct would need a ground beyond control of this fully satisfied refund claim.

For arrangements that continue to decide financial relief by assessing disputed service performance, I propose three requirements.

**A usable hearing.** Participants must have a practical way to identify the relationship and disputed performance, offer relevant information that can permissibly be disclosed, and receive a response capable of changing when that information changes. Testimony is not conclusive, but its lack of documentary corroboration cannot be a categorical reason to exclude it where the relationship properly rules out such documents. There can be proportionate eligibility checks and deadlines. Neither the number of claims nor dissatisfaction alone establishes misconduct.

**A check on interested judgment.** The arrangement must contain a proportionate way to challenge mistakes and manage the provider's interest in refusal. An independent reviewer or accessible external body can supply it. This need not mean a separate formal appeal for every tiny dispute. It does mean that the beneficiary of a rejection cannot make its own denial unchallengeable merely by announcing it. Advisers must also be able to answer allegations about their conduct.

**A justified residual rule.** The institution must specify what happens when permitted inquiry ends without resolution and choose that rule for reasons other than simply preserving its own advantage. Before introducing the arrangement, it should assess salient, feasible alternatives in light of the likely incidence and seriousness of false refusals, false payments, investigation costs, effects on other participants, and the rights limiting investigation. It must count truthful claimants' losses even when those losses never enter its accounts. Its relevant reasons must be available for challenge without disclosing confidential session contents.

This third requirement is partly substantive, not merely a duty to deliberate. An institution cannot discharge it by considering a guarantee and rejecting it because keeping fees is more profitable. It can reject a guarantee because the costs and incentives would substantially impair affordable access, or because another permissible arrangement supplies suitable protection. The interest in continued provision is not reducible to management's preference to keep each fee.

A useful exclusion test is this: a rule fails where it knowingly leaves serious, foreseeable claimant losses in place although a permissible alternative would materially reduce them at only minor burdens, with no comparable threat to rights or legitimate interests, and the reason for refusal is institutional convenience alone. People dependent on the institution's administration of their claims are entitled not to have their interests discounted in that way. Affordability by itself does not establish all these conditions.

Conversely, not every improvement is required. The duty is not to maximize claimants' expected recovery, equalize every error, or search endlessly for the best scheme. Different reasonable arrangements can trade some protection for substantially lower costs. A residual claimant burden can be defensible when protecting against false payment or preserving access gives adequate reasons for it. A reasoned choice must be implemented, not just discussed; it should also be reconsidered when experience undermines its factual basis.

Together these requirements supply a completion condition for arrangements that retain factual adjudication: a functioning route with an adequate check on conflicts, plus a disclosed and substantively defensible residual rule after a proportionate comparison of alternatives. A partial guarantee cannot by itself replace that route for a claim it leaves outstanding. A route alone cannot vindicate an otherwise indefensible default. No additional benefit is invariably required once all three conditions are met.

This is not a numerical test. It identifies operational minima and some unjustifiable choices, while leaving difficult comparisons unsettled. In particular, it does not supply a universal refund percentage or identify a unique acceptable scheme. Its verdict is correspondingly limited where costs and competing interests are closely balanced.

## 5. The fair-hearing case and reciprocal risk

Return to the second cooperative. Everyone receives a genuine hearing, neutral review assesses permissible information, confidentiality benefits both sides, and members knowingly adopt a low-price service with balanced claims left unpaid. A modest guarantee is financially possible but would appreciably burden low-income members and change incentives.

That arrangement can meet the duty. Its residual rule is not unfair merely because the institution currently holds the money. Members have an interest in affordability, mistaken payment has a real cost, and governance can give participants meaningful authority over the tradeoff. A factual refund remains owed whenever the sales-pitch condition actually occurred; the procedure is fallible recognition of that entitlement, not a redefinition of its truth conditions. No misconduct finding follows from an unresolved case.

If the variant tells us only that a guarantee is affordable, its moral status remains underdetermined. We need to know the importance of refusing valid claims, the burdens of mistaken payments, and the quality of risk acceptance. A hearing and disclosure do not make these questions disappear. But their incompleteness also gives no warrant to declare the guarantee obligatory.

The reciprocal-risk objection is consequently a genuine limit. A jointly beneficial decision not to record need not make the provider uniquely responsible for the evidence gap. My argument does not redescribe that permissible choice as negligence or insist that every reciprocal gap should trigger payment. The further question is whether an institution exercises its distinct procedural power fairly. Symmetry in the reason for missing evidence does not establish symmetry in power over the subsequent decision. Equally, unequal procedural power does not by itself establish unequal monetary risk-bearing.

Contrast a provider that knowingly leaves clients facing severe fee losses from recurring unresolved disputes. Suppose that it could provide a bounded benefit, with safeguards against repeat claims, at a minor cost that threatens no comparable interest; its only reason not to do so is that clients cannot challenge its default. Even after an honest hearing, that residual arrangement fails the exclusion test. The objection is not that confidentiality was wrongful. It is that the provider's rule disregards serious avoidable losses in exercising power over its own obligations. Here additional protection may be owed. Nothing determines its exact form or amount merely from the absence of a recording.

Consent matters, but it must concern the allocated risk. Agreement not to record a conversation is not agreement to lose every unresolved refund claim. A freely adopted low-cost arrangement differs from terms imposed on people without meaningful alternatives. Conversely, genuinely informed and fair acceptance of a residual burden is not nullified just because a more protective service could be designed. Disclosure is evidence relevant to the arrangement's fairness, not a substitute for assessing it.

## 6. What follows from defective design?

The design duty is owed to participants before the alleged service misconduct occurs. It can therefore be breached even if all advisers have behaved properly. Its condition is the organized relationship and the way claims are handled, not the occurrence of a sales pitch.

That permits a finding of defective design without a finding of service misconduct. It does not, on its own, establish a particular payment.

Suppose the cooperative wrongly excluded an account and denied review. It should correct the arrangement. If the refund claim remains outstanding and review is still possible, it should provide the affected participant a proper hearing without the defective evidential barrier. The review may find strong contrary evidence and reject the complaint without treating the complainant as dishonest. The participant was denied an owed process, but was not thereby owed the fee that a different guarantee would have returned. No new payment follows from the design defect alone. Conversely, later payment does not erase the earlier procedural wrong; any further remedy for that wrong needs its own justification.

Where a definite assurance was separately owed, the position differs. A service may have committed to a defined benefit whenever a verified participant's eligible complaint remains unresolved after review. If that condition is met, the benefit is owed without proving the alleged sales pitch. The assurance's independent trigger does the work. Its payment should be described accordingly.

An assurance might also be owed without an explicit promise if further reasons establish it as a necessary component of any acceptable arrangement for that particular relationship. But an obligation to choose among acceptable designs does not authorize a claimant to pick the most generous design retrospectively. A monetary minimum would require grounds showing that every permissible design had to provide at least that protection, or grounds fixing a particular scheme. This argument establishes no general minimum of that kind.

Sometimes belated review is impossible: testimony has faded, a confidential channel has vanished, or the opportunity for timely challenge has been lost. Acknowledging the design wrong and changing future practice does not recreate that opportunity. There may be additional reparative obligations. I have not established how to value the lost opportunity or which compensation is owed. That unresolved issue prevents deriving a fee refund simply by imagining a favorable counterfactual hearing.

Two further limits are essential. First, an uncertainty payment is not itself a finding or admission that an individual adviser committed misconduct. It can nevertheless carry information. If only complaints surviving a screening process receive a benefit, learning that a payment was made can reveal that the complaint survived. In a hypothetical set of 100 sessions, suppose misconduct occurred in ten, and nine true and nine mistaken complaints remained unresolved after permissible review. If all and only those eighteen complaints received the benefit, learning that a randomly selected session generated a payment would raise an observer's probability of misconduct from 10 percent to 50 percent, assuming the observer knows these proportions. These numbers are invented, not empirical estimates.

By contrast, if a reviewer already knows the facts determining payment and knows that the announced rule is followed, the payment adds no further information about misconduct. We must distinguish information, an official finding, and sufficient grounds for a sanction. Thomson treats individualized evidence as fallible and allows some statistical evidence to count as individualized; her discussion provides no basis for treating every uncertainty payment as evidentially inert. ([Thomson, 1986, §III, pp.202–206](https://www.marcellodibello.com/mathtrial/resources/ThomsonIndEvidence1986.pdf).) A disciplinary judgment requires a justified assessment of the relevant evidence and a fair opportunity for the adviser to respond. Payment under the assurance rule cannot substitute for that assessment. Nor should it be counted as an additional independent item of evidence when it merely restates information already considered.

Second, no duplicate recovery follows merely because the payment's basis later becomes clearer. A benefit returning the full fee should be credited against a subsequently established obligation to return that fee. A smaller benefit need not extinguish distinct established claims. The terms must distinguish what has been supplied, what remains disputed, and what has been released.

## 7. Limits and objections

**Is this just a familiar duty to handle complaints fairly?**

Much of it is, deliberately. The argument specifies how that duty operates when justified limits on evidence cause predictable impasses. It reaches the design of the route and the residual default, rather than stopping once management has offered a hearing. Its modest contribution is to connect those requirements while separating their remedial consequences. It need not establish a historically new general category of obligation to be useful.

**Does protection reward deception?**

Some uncertainty schemes will pay false or mistaken claims. Others will leave truthful claims unpaid. Eligibility checks, limits on repeat demands, and proportionate investigation can reduce these risks. Their feasibility must be assessed, not assumed. A complaint cannot be used simultaneously as insufficient evidence for a misconduct finding and as sufficient evidence for punishment of the claimant merely because it remains unresolved.

Possible abuse can justify declining a particular guarantee. It cannot alone justify comparing the worst imaginable guarantee with an unrealistically infallible proof system. The institution must compare actual permissible alternatives, including who bears their errors. In the fictional example, manageable costs are stipulated; no prediction about real fraud rates follows.

**Are advisers protected merely by labeling payments as agnostic?**

No. A label cannot prevent either reasonable inferences from selection into the payment scheme or mistaken inferences that a payment is a finding of fault. Repeated complaints can affect employment or reputation. The arrangement must explain what payment does and does not establish, limit unnecessary disclosure, and preserve a separate route for disciplinary findings. If a proposed guarantee cannot avoid comparably serious injustice to advisers, the case for that guarantee fails. Protecting users does not authorize manufacturing findings against someone else.

**Must a valuable service close if no satisfactory arrangement is possible?**

Not whenever uncertainty remains. Valuable services can operate with limited, fairly allocated protection. An effective external route or fair collective funding may help. But a provider should not market a highly consequential undertaking that it knows its claims arrangement will render practically useless. It may have to narrow the undertaking or change the activity rather than invoke its value as authority to disregard the undertaking's beneficiaries.

The duty concerns foreseeable problems within an institution's reasonable advance capacity. An emergency provider unable to prepare a claims process falls outside that argument. Further duties of assistance may exist, but need a different ground.

Finally, the fee example does not establish adequate arrangements for coercion, bodily injury, or equal standing. Those interests may require protection that a refund cannot supply. Nor does the argument establish that recording is wrong in any actual service. Temporary records, participant-held information, or carefully limited review might be permissible and valuable there. Privacy constrains inquiry; it does not automatically forbid it.

## 8. Conclusion

The strongest conclusion is neither “record everything” nor “pay whenever proof fails.” It is that an institution administering claims against itself must constrain its own power through a usable claims route and, where permissible inquiry leaves claims unresolved, a justified residual rule. An unconditional full refund can remove the need to adjudicate the refund's factual trigger. Where a claim remains disputed, sometimes additional assurance is owed; sometimes fair consideration and justified nonpayment suffice. The difference requires reasons about the relationship and its burdens, not repetition of the reason for confidentiality.

This duty can be violated without wrongful noncollection or established service misconduct. Its violation still does not identify whichever refund a hypothetical protective scheme might have offered. Keeping those points together preserves a demand for answerability without turning uncertainty into proof, compensation into confession, or confidentiality into immunity.
