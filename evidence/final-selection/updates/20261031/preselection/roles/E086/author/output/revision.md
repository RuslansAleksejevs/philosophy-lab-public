# The Inquiry Inside an Invitation to Faith

Confidence can help make itself true. Believing that a new association will succeed can lead me to join it, encouraging others whose cooperation makes success possible. William James uses cases of this kind to challenge the assumption that responsible belief must always wait upon facts settled independently of the believer. But an appeal to this power of confidence needs to specify what participation involves. Joining a project, checking its work, and urging people to stop checking are different contributions, even when they express confidence in the same outcome.

This essay offers a diagnostic for such appeals. When an advocate recommends participation because confidence will help produce truth, ask whether the proposed participation preserves the work and the grounds on which that particular rationale depends. Removing work can obstruct success. Removing grounds can instead leave success possible while making an assurance unwarranted. The advocate must address the relevant dependence, arrange a substitute, or qualify the invitation. Saying only that the challenge weakens confidence answers neither objection.

The requirement is familiar: a reason must apply to the proposal actually defended. The contribution here is to show how it changes the assessment of a difficult public dispute. In the 2009 American bank stress tests, disclosure could support confidence by making an assessment credible, yet could also threaten confidence by exposing weaknesses. The proper response was not determined by choosing between trust and inquiry. It depended on distinguishing examination, action on its findings, and the grounds available to those invited to rely on the result. These distinctions can justify some limits on disclosure while ruling out some assurances made under those limits.

I do not expose a contradiction in James, establish a new general principle of belief, or resolve the economic debate over optimal transparency. I ask what an advocate owes participants when invoking confidence's contribution to success. That question remains important even when people disagree about whether confidence ahead of evidence is epistemically justified.

## 1. Faith with differentiated work

James's permission concerns options that are live, forced and momentous and cannot be settled intellectually as he understands them. He allows suspended judgment when postponement does not lose an opportunity. His challenge is that refusing to participate can close a possibility that participation would help realize. His cooperative examples give this a causal form: approaching someone trustingly can encourage friendship; trusting partners can help sustain cooperation (1897/1912, §§I–IV, VIII–IX).

James also distinguishes audiences needing caution from those needing encouragement. He connects useful investigation with both interest in a hypothesis and vigilance against deception, and describes cooperation as people doing their respective duties while trusting others to do theirs (Preface, x–xi; §§VIII–IX, 21–24). Inquiry and different practical roles are already available within his account. For present purposes, grant that confidence's contribution to success can supply a reason for it. I leave open whether that reason is epistemic, practical, or both. [James, *The Will to Believe*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659-images.html).

Max Khan Hayward's defence of collectively self-fulfilling optimism supplies a contemporary version. Team reasoning identifies what people can best achieve together and each person's part in achieving it. Optimism can stabilize cooperation when individuals move between collective and individual perspectives. But his defence requires evidence about feasibility, potential partners and their willingness to cooperate without exploiting others. Investigation remains necessary (§§3.2–3.3, 25–29). He also distinguishes beliefs that help produce their own truth from beliefs that merely generate further apparent support for themselves (§3.1, 19–20). My question applies these qualifications to invitations rather than supplying a constraint his theory lacks. [Hayward, manuscript](https://maxkhanhayward.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/yes-we-can.pdf).

Clifford supplies a different resource. He permits action on probabilities where belief is not yet warranted; action and observation can help obtain evidence. He also permits reliance on another person's investigations. His chemist's authority depends on competent work open to testing by others, not on every recipient personally repeating the experiments (§II). This does not reconcile him with James. Clifford argues that conviction itself can impair inquiry, despite a convinced person's continuing duty to investigate (§I). I use the dependence on inquiry elsewhere without assuming that confident investigators always perform it adequately. [Clifford, “The Ethics of Belief”](https://ditext.com/clifford/belief.html).

Hardwig likewise explains how justified reliance can rest on another person's expertise without the recipient possessing or understanding all the underlying reasons (1985, 335–338). This matters institutionally: preserving inquiry need not mean imposing the same inquiry on everyone. [Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence”](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523).

## 2. Two ways an invitation can lose its basis

Two deliberately simple hypothetical cases separate the relevant failures. They establish distinctions; they do not establish that those distinctions settle a difficult controversy.

Consider a community reference work. Ada and Ben contribute entries; Chen checks uncertain entries against records. Each has time for one substantial role. Two contributors and one competent checker can complete an accurate work. Three contributors without checking leave a known class of errors undetected. One contributor and two checkers cannot finish in time. Ben will contribute if Ada joins, and Chen is already committed to checking. Ada needs confidence in success to undertake her contribution. Grant that her confidence helps secure the participation that produces the accurate work she expects.

Ada then invites Chen to express the same faith by writing entries instead. Chen agrees. The work grows, but the stipulated errors remain. Ada has offered a reason for confidence within one arrangement as a reason to participate in another. Keep the participants' beliefs fixed: all three can confidently expect success while two write and one checks, or while all three write and nobody checks. The difference is in the work. Replacing the missing checker with a missing server technician would preserve this instrumental structure. The example establishes neither a defect in spreading bare belief nor a need for someone always to doubt.

The narrower point concerns a recommendation combining belief with a specified expression of it. Confidence's benefits do not justify removing the activities through which those benefits arise. Ada could invite shared confidence while preserving different roles. Nothing requires a personal permission to remain beneficial under universal identical exercise. The objection arises because Ada recommends changing the arrangement; an invitation addressed only to contributors could leave checking intact. A competent substitute, effective automatic check, or different design could preserve the relevant function. Nor does its necessity give Ada an entitlement to Chen's indefinite unpaid labour.

The second case concerns grounds without a corresponding productive contribution. A choir needs a hall and enough singers for a weekly performance. The hall is allocated independently each week by an equal-chance draw between two groups; no other venue is available. The result is posted before rehearsals. Reading it does not affect allocation. Assume that obtaining the hall and sufficient attendance together suffice for a performance.

In week one, the organizer reads that the choir has won. She invites confidence on a particular basis: the hall is secured, and coordinated attendance will supply what remains. Suppose confidence helps the singers attend, and they perform. Before week two, she urges everyone to regard checking as distrust and transfers the notice-reader to recruitment. After the new draw, with no fresh information, she repeats the assurance that the venue is secured.

The choir might win again and perform. Holding the allocation and attendance fixed, reading the notice changes the organizer's grounds, not the supply of a hall or singers. The old notice remains good evidence about week one; it does not answer the question about week two. The defect is carrying a specified assurance across a known reset without the information it needs. Merely stopping inquiry would not establish such a defect: completed inquiries often remain informative.

A weaker invitation remains available. Since the choir may get the hall, confidence that secures attendance can still improve its prospects. The organizer could candidly invite the singers to take that chance. This would change the rationale from completing an already secured route to pursuing an uncertain one. Defeating the stronger assurance does not defeat all optimism, establish that the performance will fail, or make every participant irrational.

The shared inference is therefore limited. Suppose confidence expressed through certain activities helps produce an outcome under specified conditions, and grant that this supplies a reason for confidence there. Offering that connection as a reason for new activities requires checking whether those conditions survive. Even a positive answer preserves only that reason; it does not show that participation is required all things considered. Costs, competing responsibilities and the project's moral worth remain separate.

## 3. A documented dispute: bank stress tests

The banking case is harder because information can change the outcome people are trying to assess. Diamond and Dybvig's model makes this possibility precise: deposit contracts that provide liquidity can admit both a normal outcome and a damaging run. A change in expectations can help produce failure rather than merely predict it. This is a model of a possible mechanism, not a claim that every bank failure is caused by unwarranted fear. [Diamond and Dybvig 1983, abstract and introduction](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261155).

The historical dispute concerns the Supervisory Capital Assessment Program, or SCAP. In May 2009, American supervisors released assessments of nineteen large bank holding companies. They examined losses under an adverse economic scenario, the resources available to absorb them, and the resulting capital buffers required. The official announcement paired this assessment with Treasury support intended to bridge institutions toward private capital. [Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC, May 7, 2009](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20090507a.htm).

Bernanke presented the findings as grounds for reassurance, while explaining that some firms needed stronger capital buffers and that Treasury stood ready to supply additional capital. The claim concerned a route through a prospective downturn, not a finding that no further work was necessary. [Bernanke, May 7, 2009](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/press/bcreg/bernankescap20090507.htm).

The disclosure decision was disputed. Tarullo, a participant, later reported disagreement within the Federal Reserve over whether publishing individual results would harm weaker banks. He defended the decision by citing credible methods, reduced uncertainty and the availability of Treasury capital. But he warned that publication might be destabilizing under different conditions, particularly without a government capital backstop. He considered alternatives including publication of methods with aggregate results instead of bank-specific results. [Tarullo 2010, “Origins and Execution” and “The Question of Transparency”](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/tarullo20100326a.htm).

This account documents an actual disagreement about how information should support confidence. It does not establish that the dissenters opposed examination or that the successful policy conclusively proved them wrong. Nor is bank recapitalization an instance of Hayward's theory in every respect: its participants can be self-interested, and its rationale need not require belief beyond evidence. It tests the narrower feature shared by these invitations: confidence is offered partly as a contribution to the favourable outcome, not merely as a prediction of it.

The relevant invitation can be reconstructed as follows: a credible assessment identifies the scale of the problem; a credible arrangement addresses capital needs; private participation can help complete that arrangement. This is my reconstruction of the policy's rationale, not a quotation or a claim that all officials expressed precisely the same argument. The combination contains both inquiry and action upon its results. It also explains why disclosing bad news need not amount to abandoning the project.

## 4. What the distinction changes

Start with the productive function. Examination can guide the provision of capital by identifying where additional resources are needed. This is inquiry contributing to a remedial process, rather than inquiry merely announcing a verdict on something already complete. Bernanke described a timetable for capital plans and their implementation, private means of raising capital, and Treasury's commitment to supply a fallback. He also described supervisors' challenges to banks' assumptions and resulting modifications. [Bernanke, May 11, 2009, “Objectives” and “Process and Methodology”](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/bernanke20090511a.htm).

A confidence campaign that preserved the assessments but abandoned the means of responding to them would not automatically preserve the same route to success. A favourable reaction to the earlier package would be no adequate answer: that package included a response to adverse findings. This is not a prediction that removing a backstop must cause failure. Private financing might suffice, or a different support arrangement might work. The point is that the old rationale cannot establish that they will suffice merely because it once supported confidence under different conditions.

Now consider the evidential function. An assessment can support a claim about estimated capital needs even before those needs are met. It cannot, just by identifying a repair, establish that the repair has happened. Conversely, the later appearance of sufficient capital need not show that the original estimate was well grounded. Here success, the quality of the investigation, and the timing of a justified assurance can come apart. Calling the whole process a confidence exercise hides these differences.

This yields a substantive distinction between two invitations. One asks people to contribute to a specified plan whose remaining needs have been assessed. Another asks them to rely on a claim that the assessed needs are already satisfied. Participation might help make the first plan succeed without making the second claim warranted at the time it is offered. The same investment could therefore be defensible under the first description while the second assurance remains defective. No proof of eventual failure is required to challenge that assurance.

The harder question concerns disclosure. Does preserving the evidential function require publishing each bank's result? Not necessarily. Supervisors could conduct competent examinations privately. Participants might reasonably rely on their testimony without inspecting confidential portfolios. A demand that every participant reproduce the assessment confuses justified reliance with possession of all the underlying evidence. Clifford's chemist and Hardwig's dependence already show why that demand is too strong.

But the opposite move is also invalid: keeping evidence somewhere inside the institution does not by itself establish that every public assurance is adequately supported for its recipients. The content of the assurance matters. An aggregate assessment of the system cannot, by itself, identify the position of a particular bank. Tarullo acknowledges that aggregation can prevent outsiders from distinguishing individual institutions, while regarding it as potentially useful for understanding the system. He also treats scrutiny of methods as useful both for improving them and for assessing their quality. [Tarullo 2010, “The Question of Transparency”](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/tarullo20100326a.htm).

Thus an advocate could defend restricted disclosure and a correspondingly limited invitation: participants may rely on a system-level assessment, or on trustworthy testimony that specified institution-level conditions have been met. If the testimony has adequate grounds and is reasonably trusted, withholding raw data need not defeat the invitation. If neither institution-specific evidence nor adequately grounded testimony is available to support the stronger assurance, pointing to an aggregate result does not fill the gap. Another evidential route is needed, or a narrower assurance. This conclusion neither condemns confidentiality nor lets its practical benefits supply whatever evidence is missing.

The concern about panic makes the choice genuinely difficult. Publication might make it harder to secure the cooperation required for recovery. That is a reason to assess the timing, form and scope of disclosure; it is not evidence that the unpublished claim is true. A credible fallback can also change the consequences of releasing an adverse finding. Instead of communicating only a weakness, the combined announcement can communicate a weakness with an available response. But the fallback's adequacy must itself be assessed. Describing it as a confidence measure cannot settle its capacity to meet needs.

Consider three proposals within this dispute. First, retain private examination and the response to identified needs, but publish less detailed results. The diagnostic does not reject this merely because some information remains confidential. It asks whether the particular invitation has a sound testimonial or other basis. Second, publish detailed findings while removing an element of support on which the earlier invitation depended. Transparency alone does not preserve that invitation's productive rationale; the advocate needs grounds for a replacement route. Third, suppress effective examination in order to avoid alarming findings, while continuing to invoke assessed and manageable needs. This removes the basis of that assurance and may also impair the repair process. The same concern for confidence gives different answers because the proposals change different dependencies.

These are analytical comparisons, not claims that all three policies were adopted or advocated in 2009. Their relevance is that Tarullo's documented alternatives require precisely this separation. An indiscriminate injunction to preserve public checking would reject potentially defensible confidentiality. An indiscriminate injunction to protect confidence would permit an assurance to survive after its grounds or practical support had been removed. The diagnostic excludes both inferences. It asks the proponent of restricted disclosure to specify what recipients may reasonably rely on, and the proponent of broader disclosure to identify how the response to adverse findings will remain viable.

It also changes what a challenge can demand. Someone may support the recapitalization effort while refusing to accept a stronger assurance than the assessment supports. They need not argue that every bank is unsafe or that no cooperation could succeed. Equally, someone who defends postponing publication need not reject inquiry: they may be defending continued private examination and a different means of warranted reliance. The disagreement is partly about how to arrange and communicate inquiry, rather than whether trust is preferable to knowledge.

This is a conditional assessment of competing proposals, not an independent causal estimate of the 2009 policy. The cited official accounts do not isolate the effect of disclosure from capital support and other interventions. A subsequent recovery would therefore not by itself establish that disclosure was necessary, sufficient or optimal. The philosophical result is the discrimination between claims and proposals; the empirical choice still requires evidence about their likely effects.

## 5. Commitment without a universal ban on checking

This result fits, rather than overturns, nearby work on faith and inquiry. Buchak makes faith relative to committing to a risky act without first seeking further evidence for the purpose of deciding whether to perform it. She expressly allows inquiry for other purposes and distinguishes the proposition in which someone has faith from background propositions on which the act also depends. Her example permits further investigation of a charity while acting on a religious commitment (author manuscript, 6–8). A call to commitment therefore cannot simply be read as a demand to end every connected investigation. [Buchak, “Rational Faith and Justified Belief”](https://www.larabuchak.net/s/A06-Rational-Faith-and-Justified-Belief.pdf).

The institutional question adds an allocation problem: whose inquiry, into which question, must remain effective for this invitation? Participants can commit to financing a recovery while supervisors investigate what capital is needed. Whether an individual thereby has faith in Buchak's precise sense depends on the act, proposition and purpose of further evidence-seeking. Nothing in the banking case shows that her conditions are satisfied by every investor, or that her account entails my particular policy assessment.

Friedman's norm against simultaneously believing a complete answer and inquiring into that same question addresses another level. Her account distinguishes complete from partial answers and focuses on an individual inquirer (2019; author manuscript, §§2–3). My argument needs no general verdict against that norm. Confidence that a recovery plan can succeed is not a complete answer to how much additional capital each institution needs; different people can also occupy the different roles. [Friedman, “Inquiry and Belief”](https://jfriedmanphilo.github.io/IB.pdf).

Nor does the argument vindicate a confident examiner simply by naming two questions. If conviction about success prevents serious attention to adverse findings, the required work has not been preserved. Independence, access to records or different personnel may be needed. Dividing roles is a proposal for making inquiry effective, not proof that it is effective.

These connections restrict the originality claim. The dependence of reliance on others' inquiries, the difference between committing and investigating, and the importance of background conditions are established resources. The application contributes a way of assessing a public rationale across changes in institutional arrangements. It does not demonstrate that no earlier author has offered the same combination or that financial policymakers needed philosophy to discover the relevant distinctions.

## 6. What the advocate still owes

The diagnostic imposes no standing demand to investigate everything again. A completed assessment can continue to support an assurance. A new check can be redundant, unreliable, irrelevant or too costly. The challenger must identify what the proposed inquiry contributes and why the existing grounds or arrangements no longer suffice. Only then must an advocate address the dependence to keep offering the same rationale unchanged.

Nor does it require the strongest possible assurance. Participants can knowingly accept uncertainty. They may have other reasons to act, or weaker reasons for optimism. What matters is that an appeal to risk should not masquerade as a report that a necessary condition has already been established. Failure of one justification leaves those alternatives open, but does not silently turn it into one of them.

Sometimes effective checking and the required cooperation cannot coexist. Investigation may consume an urgent opportunity; publication may expose a vulnerability that others exploit. Then an advocate must revise the proposal or its rationale. An argument that depends on both a check and its suppression cannot retain both benefits by changing the subject to the value of trust. A candid, less secure invitation might remain defensible, but the loss is real and should be reflected in what participants are told.

Finally, preserving a route to success does not make the enterprise good. Confidence can support harmful projects; reliable public assurances can distribute risks unfairly. Neither the bank application nor the hypothetical cases establish an entitlement to other people's resources. They identify conditions of a specified argument, leaving a fuller assessment of participation to include costs, rights and competing responsibilities.

James directs attention to truths that participation can help create. Taking that possibility seriously also means taking seriously the arrangement through which participation works. In the banking dispute, the useful question is not simply whether checking builds or destroys trust. It is whether this examination, this response to its findings and this public assurance together support the invitation being made. One can answer that question by preserving inquiry, changing its disclosure, replacing a missing practical condition, or weakening an assurance. Confidence alone does not tell us which answer is needed.
