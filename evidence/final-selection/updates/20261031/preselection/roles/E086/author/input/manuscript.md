# The Inquiry Inside an Invitation to Faith

Confidence can help make itself true. Believing that a new association will succeed can lead me to join it, encouraging others whose cooperation makes success possible. William James uses cases of this kind to challenge the assumption that responsible belief must always wait upon facts settled independently of the believer. But a public appeal to this power of confidence needs to specify what participation involves. Joining a project, checking its work, and urging everyone to stop checking are different contributions, even when all three express confidence in the same outcome.

This essay offers a diagnostic for such appeals. When an advocate recommends a form of participation because confidence will help produce truth, ask whether the proposed participation preserves the activities and grounds on which that particular rationale depends. If it removes them, the advocate must arrange a substitute, qualify the invitation, or change its justification. The original benefit of confidence cannot simply be carried across the change.

The underlying requirement is familiar: a reason must apply to the proposal actually defended. I do not present it as a new principle of rational belief. Its value here is to distinguish two things an inquiry objection may establish. It may show that the invitation removes work needed for success. Alternatively, it may show that the advocate has lost grounds for presenting confidence as capable of securing success, although the project can still succeed. In either case, answering only that the objection discourages people leaves the relevant dependence unanswered.

This is an application of resources within the debate about faith, not the exposure of a contradiction in James. Both James and a contemporary defender of collective optimism, Max Khan Hayward, already make room for different contributions and continued inquiry. Taking those qualifications seriously helps locate the proper target of criticism: sometimes the problem is neither the number of believers nor their shared confidence, but the account of participation being offered in confidence's name.

## 1. Faith with differentiated work

James's permission is restricted. His central argument concerns options that are live, forced and momentous and cannot be settled intellectually as he understands them. He recognizes limits on voluntary belief and the value of suspended judgment where postponement does not lose an opportunity. His challenge is that waiting cannot always preserve every prospect of truth. A refusal to participate may close a possibility that participation would help realize (James 1897/1912, §§I–IV, VII–IX).

The cooperative examples in §IX give this argument a causal form. Trusting another person's friendliness may encourage an approach that helps bring friendliness about. Confidence in partners can support the actions through which cooperation succeeds. Here the believer participates in producing what the belief represents. For this essay, grant that such a connection can supply a reason for confidence before ordinary evidence settles the outcome. The argument below does not decide whether that reason is epistemic, practical, or both. It assesses an advocate who offers the connection as a reason.

James does not infer that one message or one activity suits everybody. In the Preface, pp. x–xi, he distinguishes audiences needing criticism and caution from an academic audience whose inhibition calls for encouragement. In §VIII, p. 21, he describes useful investigation as combining interest in a hypothesis with alertness to deception, and identifies verification as an organized practice. In §IX, p. 24, his description of cooperation depends on each person doing their own duty while trusting others to do theirs. Audience sensitivity, inquiry and differentiated work are already part of his account.

Hayward's defence of collectively self-fulfilling optimism is equally relevant. Team reasoning starts with what people can best achieve together and identifies each person's part in achieving it (§3.2, p. 25). Optimism can stabilize cooperation when people move between individual and collective ways of considering their options. Yet Hayward requires evidence concerning collective feasibility, willing partners and their altruism, and expressly makes his proposed faith answerable to investigation and deliberation (§3.3, pp. 27–29). His framework can accommodate different practical roles from the start.

My maintenance question therefore adds no missing universal constraint to these accounts. It asks how their acknowledged qualifications bear on a particular invitation: does what people are being asked to do sustain the rationale for asking them? The following examples make that question precise. They are compatible developments of the sources, rather than counterexamples to an inference either author is shown to make.

Clifford contributes a different resource. In §II of “The Ethics of Belief,” he permits action on probabilities when the evidence does not yet warrant belief. Action and observation can help obtain evidence. He also permits reliance on others; a recipient need not personally repeat every experiment. His chemist's authority depends on practices of questioning and testing, so another person's justified reliance can rest on inquiry conducted elsewhere (Clifford 1877, §II). Hardwig develops the importance of such epistemic dependence without requiring that everyone possess the expert's inquiry (1985, pp. 335–338).

This does not reconcile Clifford with James. Clifford can insist on acting probabilistically rather than believing ahead of evidence. Moreover, in §I he grants that an already convinced person still has a duty to investigate, while arguing that conviction impairs the fairness and completeness of inquiry. His allowance for an inquiry duty is not an unqualified endorsement of confident investigators. I use the social dependence identified in his account without adopting that stronger psychological claim. Whether any particular confident checker can investigate adequately remains a condition to assess, not something established by giving them the title of checker.

## 2. A successful believer and an unsuccessful invitation

Consider a hypothetical community reference work. Ada and Ben contribute entries; Chen checks uncertain entries against records. Each has time for one substantial role. Two contributors and one competent checker can produce an accurate completed work. Three contributors without checking will leave a known class of copying errors undetected. One contributor and two checkers cannot finish in time. These are stipulations, not empirical claims about actual reference projects.

Ben will contribute if Ada joins; Chen is already committed to checking. Ada finds confidence in the project's success necessary to undertake her contribution. Grant that her confidence has the truth-producing role invoked in a Jamesian defence: it secures her participation, helps bring Ben into the project, and thereby helps create the accurate work she expects.

Ada then invites Chen to show the same faith by spending all available time producing entries instead of checking them. She points to progress as evidence that confidence works. Chen follows her advice. The work gets larger, but the stipulated errors remain. Ada has offered a reason for confidence within one arrangement as a reason for participating in a different arrangement.

The diagnostic is deliberately elementary. Keep everyone's belief fixed. Ada, Ben and Chen may all confidently expect success while two contribute and one checks. With those same beliefs, all three can instead contribute and nobody check. The difference in outcome is explained by the allocation of work. Replacing the missing checker with a missing server technician would preserve that basic instrumental structure. The example therefore does not establish a new limitation on the spread of bare belief, or a requirement that someone always doubt what others believe.

It establishes something narrower. A recommendation that combines belief with a specified expression of belief needs a reason applying to that combination. Someone relying on the belief's consequences cannot omit the activities through which those consequences arise. Ada could invite everybody to share her confidence while retaining the division of work. What fails is her particular invitation to express it uniformly.

Nor does the case impose a requirement that every personal permission remain beneficial under universal identical exercise. Ada need not turn her permission into a general command. She has chosen to recommend a change, and that change is what calls for justification. If an invitation addresses only potential contributors and leaves checking intact, this objection does not arise.

The inference at issue can be stated with consistent normative strength:

1. Confidence expressed through specified activities helps produce the believed outcome under certain conditions.
2. Grant that this connection supplies a reason for that confidence in those circumstances.
3. The same connection is offered as a reason for the confidence and activities now being recommended.

The third claim requires an additional assessment. The new activities may remove a condition in the first claim. Matching the conditions would at most preserve this particular reason; it would not establish that participation is required all things considered. Costs, competing responsibilities and the morality of the project would remain separate questions.

In the reference-work case, Chen's checking is part of producing accuracy. Preserving its function does not necessarily mean retaining Chen in that role. A competent substitute, an effective automatic check, or a different project design might work. Nor does necessity authorize conscripting Chen into indefinite unpaid labour. The argument identifies a condition of Ada's proposed rationale, not an entitlement to another person's service.

## 3. Losing the grounds without losing the outcome

Inquiry can play a second role. It can establish that the proposed route to success is available without itself creating that availability. Here stopping inquiry need not make success less likely through the loss of a productive task. It can instead deprive the advocate of grounds for continuing to offer a particular rationale.

The distinction needs care. Evidence does not disappear merely because nobody keeps collecting it. A completed survey can remain informative. A settled fact may require no further check. Even when a condition can change, a warning that the grounds may lapse is not yet proof that they have lapsed. A useful example must identify what changes and why previous evidence no longer answers the current question.

Imagine a small choir hoping to stage a performance each week. A venue is required, and enough singers must attend. The community hall is allocated separately each week by an independent draw between two groups, with equal chances. Last week's allocation has no bearing on this week's. The result is posted before rehearsals begin. Reading the notice does not affect the draw. Assume no other venue is available and that, once the choir has the hall, attendance by the necessary singers will suffice for a successful performance.

In the first week, the organizer checks the notice: the choir has the hall. She can now invite the singers to have confidence in their joint success on a specific basis. The venue is secured; their coordinated participation can supply what remains. Suppose confidence in one another's attendance helps them turn up. The performance occurs, and the invitation's claimed causal role is realized.

Before the following week, the organizer urges everyone to treat checking as an expression of distrust and assigns the person who reads the notice to recruitment instead. The next allocation occurs, but nobody reads it or obtains equivalent evidence. The organizer repeats the same assurance: the venue is secured, and participation will complete the route to success.

That assurance has lost its stated basis. The old notice remains evidence about the first week. It does not establish the allocation after the independent new draw. Before the draw, the organizer had reason to anticipate a need for new information; after the draw without checking, she lacks the evidence her renewed assurance purports to have. It is the attempt to carry the particular rationale across the known reset, not the mere cessation of inquiry, that creates the problem.

Suppose the choir happens to win again, everybody attends, and the second performance succeeds. Nothing in the argument denies this possibility. Holding the actual allocation and attendance fixed, reading the notice changes what the organizer has grounds to claim, rather than supplying the hall or the singers. The inquiry's relevant role here concerns grounds for the appeal, unlike Chen's error correction in the reference work.

There can also remain a different reason for optimism. Since the choir has a chance of receiving the hall, confidence that secures attendance may still raise the probability of a performance. The organizer might candidly propose taking that chance. The example does not refute every self-fulfilment argument available to her. It defeats reuse of the stronger argument that an already secured venue makes coordinated attendance sufficient. Changing that argument is a legitimate response, but a change must not be disguised as its uninterrupted survival.

The example's independent draw makes the evidential break unusually clear. It is a limiting case, not a general model of changing social conditions. Where conditions persist, earlier evidence may retain substantial force. Where there is reliable new testimony, personal checking may be unnecessary. A diagnostic should ask what grounds the actual rationale needs and whether they survive; it should not turn uncertainty into a standing demand to investigate everything again.

## 4. What a confidence objection leaves unanswered

The two cases support a limited consequence for argument between an advocate and a participant. A participant can identify an inquiry on which the advocated route to truth, or the grounds offered for that route, depends. The advocate can answer that the claimed dependence is mistaken, that an adequate substitute exists, or that a different, more qualified rationale supports participation. Merely replying that the inquiry reduces confidence does not establish any of these things.

This is the epistemic point of the diagnostic, despite its familiar instrumental basis. Inquiry can be part of what makes the advocate's account warranted. In that case, a challenge to the invitation need not deny the hoped-for outcome or reject cooperation. It can ask whether the organizer is entitled to give the kind of reason she is giving. The object of the challenge is the adequacy of a public rationale, not necessarily the participants' optimism.

The choir makes the consequence especially clear. A singer can support the second performance, believe that it will happen, and still object to an assurance presented as based on a checked allocation. That objection does not require proving that the performance will fail. Nor must the singer first establish that every weaker reason for participation is insufficient. Showing that the specified basis is absent is enough to challenge the assurance in its present form. Whether a different appeal would be acceptable is a further issue.

This also limits how much can be claimed for the objection. Identifying lost grounds for one rationale does not license a general accusation of irrationality. The organizer may have other reasons to believe, or reasons to act under uncertainty without belief. Equally, the organizer's success by luck does not retrospectively show that the stated basis was present. Assessment of the invitation, assessment of confidence itself, and assessment of the eventual outcome can come apart.

Why spend philosophical effort on something a competent advocate can accept? Because these distinctions determine what a disagreement is about and what would answer it. The reference case calls for preserving or replacing productive work. The choir case may instead call for obtaining information or weakening an assurance. A generic dispute over whether faith is beneficial does not distinguish those responses. The diagnostic supplies a way to identify a relevant defect without taking a position against all belief ahead of evidence.

Its significance is therefore practical and interpretive. It clarifies an implication of existing views and gives worked conditions for applying it. I have not shown that James or Hayward commits the mistaken inference, that people commonly commit it, or that no earlier author has drawn these distinctions. The argument should stand or fall by the accuracy and usefulness of the application, not by an unsupported priority claim.

## 5. The limits of preserving inquiry

Several limits follow from the same reasoning. First, not every questioner performs a needed function. A check can be irrelevant, redundant, badly executed or too costly. Calling an activity inquiry does not establish its value. The challenger must identify what it contributes to the particular route or rationale. Once that dependence is established, the advocate must address it if she continues to offer that rationale unchanged.

Second, continued checking can coexist with confidence in the examples, but its psychological adequacy cannot always be assumed. Clifford's warning matters: a confident checker may ignore unwelcome evidence. If that happens, appointing one on paper does not preserve the required function. Different personnel, independent access to records or a different method may be necessary. The diagnostic requires effective inquiry where the rationale depends on it; it does not settle which arrangements make inquiry effective.

Third, an inquiry might itself damage cooperation. Repeated demands for reassurance can be intrusive, and an urgent venture might lose its opportunity while people investigate. These possibilities defeat any blanket instruction to keep checking. They do not show that incompatible conditions can jointly support one appeal. If the inquiry required for the stated rationale cannot coexist with the proposed participation, the advocate must alter the rationale or the proposal. Sometimes a candid appeal to risk is available where an assurance is not.

Finally, preserving inquiry does not make an enterprise good. Success can itself be harmful, and an effective form of confidence can have unacceptable costs. This essay grants an initial self-fulfilment reason and follows its conditions; it supplies no complete ethics of belief or collective action. Even a sound causal story is only part of what a participant may need to consider.

James directs attention to truths that participation can help create. His own account, and Hayward's, already permits participation to take different forms. Clifford shows why responsible reliance can depend on inquiry elsewhere. Together these resources support a precise question for the advocate of confidence: what work and what grounds does this invitation need to remain the invitation you claim it is? Sometimes the answer is more contribution. Sometimes it is a check. Sometimes it is a less confident assurance about what confidence can do.
