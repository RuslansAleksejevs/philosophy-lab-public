# What the Verdict Does Not Tell Us

A judge hears a consideration, rules it inadmissible, and reaches the verdict she would have reached without hearing it. Has she successfully excluded that consideration? Perhaps. But perhaps she still counted it, while the remaining evidence settled the case. A verdict can stay fixed while the reasons entering its determination change. Conversely, a rule can alter a verdict without removing every contribution the forbidden consideration makes to deliberation. These possibilities matter when judicial experiments are used to establish our ability to control which reasons we act on.

I examine what one such experiment can show. Its reported conviction rates fit both a population in which some judges completely remove a consideration from the basis of their decision and one in which everybody continues to count it. That result survives even if the rates are treated as exact population probabilities. But it concerns a particular achievement: complete rule-guided removal on the observed occasion. A broader achievement, such as refraining from an action for a forbidden reason because of a rule, need not require that complete removal. The distinction limits both the empirical inference and the criticism we may make of it. My argument challenges a strong reading of an empirical claim in Kenta Sekine's defence of second-order reasons; it does not refute his broader defence of our capacity to be guided by them.

Second-order reasons concern the reasons for which we act. An instruction to decide a hiring question solely on professional qualifications, for example, addresses more than the choice between applicants: it restricts the considerations on which that choice should depend. Following such an instruction is different from selecting the same applicant for an excluded reason. It is also different from being accidentally unmoved by that reason. Successful responsiveness requires a connection between the restriction and the agent's way of deciding.

In §3 of “In Defence of Second-Order Reasons,” Sekine discusses an experiment by Wistrich, Guthrie and Rachlinski. From its conviction rates he infers that most judges cannot exclude the consideration, but some can. Yet his qualifications matter. Footnote 39 treats preventing deliberation on a reason as one sufficient way of avoiding action for that reason. It does not make this the only possible way. Footnote 43 explicitly leaves room for guidance by exclusionary reasons beyond excluding the target reasons. His response also distinguishes difficulty in tested conditions from psychological impossibility. [Sekine 2026, §3, nn.39–43](https://doi.org/10.1017/cjlj.2026.10062)

The charitable reading therefore separates two claims. The experiment is offered as an illustration of some successful control over acting for reasons. Its description also invites a stronger interpretation: some subjects entirely removed the forbidden consideration from their practical weighing of the case. The latter is a natural reading of exclusion in deliberation, but it is not a requirement we may simply impose on the former. The construction below establishes a limit on the stronger interpretation. It also shows why that limit cannot automatically be promoted into a challenge to all successful rule-guided action.

There is already a careful discussion of these figures. Maggie Wittlin notes the statistical weakness of the difference between the two exposed subgroups and distinguishes discounting evidence from discarding it. She also explains why residual influence can remain objectionable when the concern is an improper basis for deciding. These are substantive insights, not merely a warning about sample size. I take them as starting points. The further question is whether even perfectly established rates would identify any complete excluder. [Wittlin 2023, pp. 1759–60, n.143](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-wordpress-0/wp-content/uploads/sites/278/2023/11/27125213/Binding-Hercules-A-Proposal-for-Bench-Trials.pdf)

The original report helps locate the issue. Judges received a hypothetical sexual-assault case, with or without additional sexual-history information. Exposed judges first ruled on its admissibility; all then answered a guilt question requiring reliance only on admitted evidence. The conviction counts were 27/55 without exposure, 7/35 among those ruling the information inadmissible, and 1/13 among those admitting it: the reported 49.1%, 20%, and 7.7%. The last two groups were formed by the judges' own rulings, and their difference was not statistically significant (Fisher's exact p = .42). The researchers also note that judges admitting the information may have regarded it as more probative. [Wistrich, Guthrie and Rachlinski 2005, pp.1283–84, 1300–03, 1336–37](https://repository.law.upenn.edu/Documents/Detail/can-judges-ignore-inadmissible-information-the-difficulty-of-deliberately-disregarding/153906)

So the difference between the exposed groups is not an observed before-and-after change in the same judges. Judges who independently find information less persuasive might also be more likely to reject its admissibility. That possibility complicates even an inference to rule-caused discounting. It is a problem about attributing a change to the rule; the problem I now isolate remains after granting that attribution.

For the construction, I treat the three rounded rates as exact probabilities of a hypothetical population. I grant comparable baseline distributions and let the 7.7% rate represent full reliance on the information. I also stipulate that the intermediate rate reflects a response to the rule. These are deliberately generous assumptions, not findings that the experimental design independently establishes. Even with them, complete exclusion does not follow.

By complete exclusion I mean that, because of the rule, the agent gives the target consideration no role as a reason in weighing the case. This does not require forgetting it, ceasing to recognize its apparent force, or eliminating every emotional or other causal trace of exposure. The model concerns active reliance on a consideration in deciding, not an empty mind.

Imagine a population of judges. Each has a baseline deliberative score, x, representing how strongly the admissible case supports conviction relative to the judge's threshold. A positive final score produces conviction; a negative score produces acquittal. Let baseline scores be uniformly distributed from −0.509 to 0.491. The interval has length one, and its positive part has length 0.491. Without the extra consideration, 49.1% therefore convict.

When counted at full strength, suppose the extra consideration subtracts 0.414 from each judge's score. A judge then convicts only if x exceeds 0.414. The relevant portion of the interval has length 0.077, producing the second rate of 7.7%.

These scores are not proposed measurements of actual judges. They describe a possible pattern of deliberation. In particular, the subtraction represents the agent counting the consideration against conviction. It does not represent a stomach ache, an unnoticed distraction, or any other influence that might cause behaviour without functioning as a reason. The model stipulates deliberative reliance because that is what the philosophical inference is meant to concern. Nor need real deliberation be literally numerical. The scores provide a convenient representation of differences in how close a judge is to a decision threshold.

Now consider two different responses to the exclusionary rule.

In the first model, some judges completely exclude the consideration because they accept the rule as a reason not to rely on it. They recognize its apparent force, but remove it from the basis of their decision. The rest continue to count it at full strength. Let the proportion of complete excluders be q, independently of their baseline scores. The overall conviction rate is then:

q × 0.491 + (1 − q) × 0.077.

Set q to 0.123/0.414, or approximately 0.2971. The rate becomes exactly 0.200. Thus a population containing about 29.7% complete excluders reproduces the reported intermediate rate. By stipulation, the successful judges exclude because of the rule; their zero reliance is not merely accidental.

In the second model, every judge responds to the rule by reducing the weight assigned to the consideration, but nobody eliminates that weight. Each now subtracts 0.291 rather than 0.414. Each still treats the information as counting against conviction. A judge convicts when x exceeds 0.291. The relevant portion of the interval has length 0.200. Again, exactly 20% convict.

The contrast can be displayed compactly:

| Condition | Model A: complete exclusion by some | Model B: partial reliance by everyone |
|---|---:|---:|
| No exposure | 49.1% convict | 49.1% convict |
| Stipulated full reliance on the information | 7.7% convict | 7.7% convict |
| Response to the exclusionary rule | 20% convict | 20% convict |
| Complete exclusion because of the rule on this occasion | About 29.7% | 0% |

The same three outcome probabilities fit opposite answers to the question whether anyone completely removes the consideration from the deliberative basis. Knowing those probabilities exactly would not choose between the models. More precise estimation of those same quantities would therefore leave this particular problem unresolved.

One could obtain the first model by assuming in advance that judges either exclude completely or continue to rely at full strength. This is my illustrative reconstruction of one sufficient additional restriction, not a quantitative model or explicit premise stated by Sekine. Under that restriction and the other assumptions just granted, the intermediate rate identifies a mixture of complete exclusion and full reliance. But the restriction does essential work. It rules out the second model rather than deriving its impossibility from the verdicts. Partial discounting is especially relevant here because Wittlin already identifies it as a live possibility in discussing this experiment. The alternative is not an elaborate mechanism invented solely to evade the result.

There is an important limitation to what “complete exclusion” means in this argument. A consideration against conviction need not be a positive motivating reason for a verdict of conviction. A judge might consider it, weigh it against stronger considerations, and convict despite it. It would be misleading to say that she convicted *for* the reason that pointed toward acquittal. Model B instead says that everyone retains the excluded consideration in the deliberative basis from which the verdict is reached. The consideration remains part of what the judge takes the case for and against conviction to be.

This distinction makes the correction more exact. The construction challenges the inference to complete removal from the deliberative basis. It does not establish that no one succeeds under every action-based conception of exclusion. Consider the judges whose baseline scores lie between 0.291 and 0.414. In Model B, they acquit when the consideration is counted at full strength but convict after the rule reduces its weight. This interval contains 12.3% of the illustrative population. Their changed action is explained by their response to the rule. A defender might count this as successfully refraining from an acquittal for the prohibited reason, even though the consideration remains in deliberation.

That interpretation cannot be dismissed by saying that the consideration merely lost to stronger reasons: the rule itself explains why it lost. If such rule-caused defeat counts as the relevant achievement, Model B contains successes. The paired models then cease to contrast success with failure under that conception. This is a reason to narrow the objection. In light of Sekine's qualifications, complete removal should not be treated as a necessary condition of the success his general defence requires. What the models establish is that the three probabilities do not identify complete removal, even after rule-guided discounting is granted.

We should therefore distinguish accepting the rule, reducing reliance in response to it, and wholly excluding the target consideration. Model B allows the first two. It does not portray agents as wholly unresponsive to reasons about reasons. Whether its action-changing cases also satisfy an account of refraining from acting for a forbidden reason needs an account of that achievement. A mere verdict switch is not enough to settle the account. But neither is residual deliberative weight enough to rule the achievement out. An advocate of the broader conception can accept the construction without abandoning the possibility of successful guidance.

The problem also extends beyond the familiar warning against moving from groups to individuals. The underlying action-theoretic distinction is already explicit in Keeling: her §3.2.4 considers a decision that stays fixed while its basis changes following a reminder of a promise. The following examples apply that inherited distinction to the interpretation of verdict observations; they do not introduce a new theory of basing. [Keeling 2024, §3.2.4](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.13069) Suppose we somehow learned that a particular judge's verdict would be the same with and without exposure. In the second model, a judge with a baseline score of 0.4 convicts both before and after subtracting 0.291. She nevertheless counts the extra consideration. A judge with a baseline score of −0.1 acquits in both conditions while treating the consideration as additional support for acquittal. Outcome stability identifies neither the absence of reliance nor the explanation of its absence.

Now compare either person with a judge who would count the information if it were admissible, but excludes it in response to the rule. That judge might also give the same verdict in every observed condition because the remaining case is decisive. Here there is successful exclusion without a verdict change. Stable decisions can accordingly conceal either successful restriction or continued reliance. What matters is not simply which verdict occurs, but how the consideration enters the agent's response and why its role changes or stays fixed.

This is compatible with different philosophical accounts of acting for reasons. Keeling's account of second-order basing explicitly combines a causal role with an appraisal of the lower-order reason's appropriateness. One need not accept all its details to see the measurement issue: an outcome does not by itself reveal that relationship. The present argument supplies no replacement theory of basing. [Keeling 2024, §§3.1–3.3](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.13069)

What follows for the debate? First, the two models establish compatibility, not equal plausibility. Independent psychological evidence might make the mixture model more credible than universal partial discounting. With such evidence, the observed frequencies might probabilistically support the existence of complete excluders. My argument does not show that the experiment has no evidential value. It shows which claim the frequencies fail to establish on their own and why a stronger inference needs additional assumptions about the mechanism.

Second, no finding about these particular judges' actual capacities follows from the second model. It is a counterexample to an inference to complete removal, not a diagnosis. Even if nobody in the experiment achieved that removal, it would remain possible that they could do so with more time, better conditions, or a different form of control. A fortiori, the result would not establish a logical impossibility. The inference from demonstrated successes to capacity needs the relevant successes to be established; capacity defended on other grounds does not depend on this route. Nothing in the construction overturns Sekine's distinction between difficulty and impossibility.

Third, the distinction does not render empirical research into reason-responsiveness hopeless. But extra observations must actually separate proposed explanations. Merely varying the apparent strength of the consideration need not do so. Extend the models by letting d denote its full weight, keeping q fixed in Model A, and letting everyone in Model B count the fraction k = 1 − q of that weight. Throughout 0 ≤ d ≤ 0.491, their conviction probabilities remain identical:

Model A: q(0.491) + (1 − q)(0.491 − d) = 0.491 − kd.

Model B: P(x > kd) = 0.491 − kd.

Thus a whole range of further measurements could reproduce the original ambiguity. This is a useful constraint on the suggestion that more varied cases would automatically help.

The same specified extensions diverge beyond that range. At d = 0.6, nobody who counts the consideration fully can convict. Model A retains convictions among complete excluders and predicts q × 0.491, approximately 0.1459. Model B predicts 0.491 − 0.6k, approximately 0.0693. This is an illustration of a discriminating prediction under added assumptions, not a validated experimental design. It requires stable baseline distributions and response parameters, and a justified mapping between an experimental manipulation and the score d. Other plausible extensions might differ. Process observations and reports of reasoning could also constrain the explanation, without providing transparent access to motives. The lesson is to state and test the relevant mechanism assumptions rather than simply request more outcomes.

The original investigators already measured confidence in an earlier version of the case. They found a confidence difference where verdicts did not differ. That is useful additional evidence of influence, although confidence is not a direct readout of an action's motivating basis. [Wistrich, Guthrie and Rachlinski 2005, pp.1301–02](https://repository.law.upenn.edu/Documents/Detail/can-judges-ignore-inadmissible-information-the-difficulty-of-deliberately-disregarding/153906)

There is also research that looks beyond compliance alone. Hoeft, Kurschilgen and Mill study how authoritative requests affect information acquisition in a modified dictator game; their discussion considers alternative explanations for the observed behaviour. That work should prevent any claim that process-sensitive investigation is a new recommendation here. It also illustrates a relevant distinction: declining to acquire information and excluding a consideration already acquired are different routes by which a norm can shape deliberation. Success on one task does not automatically measure success on the other. [Hoeft, Kurschilgen and Mill 2024, §§3, 6; study published 2025](https://www.crctr224.de/research/discussion-papers/archive/dp610/@@download/file/CRCTR224_2024_610.pdf)

The useful conclusion is consequently restrained. The general method of comparing models with the same observable implications is inherited, as are the distinctions between discounting and discarding, and between a verdict and its basis. Here they establish that an intermediate conviction rate need not contain any complete excluder, even when the rule really changes everyone's deliberation. They do not establish that it contains no successful response to an exclusionary reason. If the empirical claim is complete removal, an additional argument about the mechanism is needed. If the claim is a broader form of guided nonperformance, the paired models do not refute it, and the appropriate evidence must be assessed against that different target. The lesson is to match the philosophical achievement to the empirical quantity before drawing a conclusion about capacity. Verdicts tell us what agents decide. How they decide, and which kind of control that displays, remain further questions.
