# Who Chooses the Last Citizens’ Assembly?

## 1. An honest lottery, a selected answer

Imagine a mayor who wants a congestion charge. A citizens’ assembly rejects it. A second assembly considers the same proposal with the same information and also rejects it. A third approves. Consultation ends. Every panel was fairly recruited, adequately informed, and free to deliberate. All reports remain public.

Those facts alone establish no misconduct. Perhaps the task was to discover whether at least one of three panels could endorse the charge. The last report might simply record that a specified threshold was reached. Ending a procedure does not necessarily make its last report more authoritative than its predecessors.

My target requires further facts. Suppose the council commissioned an advisory citizen appraisal of the proposal, favorable or unfavorable, to which officials would publicly respond. It did not authorize a search for at least one endorsement. The mayor administers the consultation and controls recommissioning. His policy allows at most three panels in all, repeating an unchanged inquiry after rejection but stopping after any approval. He now presents the approving report as the completed consultation’s citizen appraisal, rather than merely one authentic endorsement within a record of disagreement. Asked what entitles this report to that role, he invokes its fair lottery and independent deliberation. The contrary reports retain publicity but not that institutional standing.

The charge might be excellent policy, and the third report might contain excellent reasons. What has not yet been justified is the particular attribution: why this report should supply the consultation’s claimed citizen warrant. That question remains even if officials also give reasons for their policy choices.

I defend a limited thesis: **fair recruitment and free deliberation do not by themselves justify the institutional standing of a report selected for its favorable answer. A claim to independent citizen appraisal requires an account of how completed judgments acquire that role.** The account must include continuation, stopping, and designation, not just membership selection.

This is an insufficiency claim, not an illegitimacy theorem. A separate mandate, a substantive comparison of arguments, or a defensible aggregate test might supply the missing justification. I grant no permanent veto to a first panel and impose no general ban on repetition. The issue is a transfer of warrant: from the independence of the people forming a judgment to the justification of making that judgment count as the institution’s appraisal.

## 2. Three claims and their predecessors

A report can establish three different things.

First, it can be an **authentic judgment**: these participants sincerely reached this conclusion after deliberation. Favorable-answer selection does not make them insincere or turn them into the mayor’s agents.

Second, it can be **evidence about judgments beyond this group**. A modest claim concerns how other comparably recruited panels might respond. This is not the same as estimating the proportion of individual citizens who support the policy. Either generalization needs an appropriate account of selection.

Third, a report can have **institutional standing**: it completes a consultation, triggers a response, satisfies a review requirement, or receives special weight in a decision. Standing need not include a power to enact or veto policy. An advisory report can still occupy an officially important role.

These claims do not entail one another. Authenticity does not establish typicality. Informative evidence does not establish authority. A justified institutional threshold need not measure typical opinion.

Lafont offers a nearby argument, not an opponent to be corrected. Against automatic deference to a mini-public’s majority, she observes that minority participants also received the information and deliberated, yet disagreed (2017, pp. 91–92). She also allows non-authoritative evidential uses that stimulate wider contestation rather than settle it (pp. 96–97). The shared-credentials challenge below extends across panels and adds a report-designation rule; I do not attribute that extension to her. [Lafont, “Can Democracy be Deliberative & Participatory?”](https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/publication/downloads/09_lafont.pdf)

The broader problem of control after deliberation is already well developed. Setälä examines strategic commissioning, selective uptake, public explanations for accepting or rejecting recommendations, and the limits of publicity. Her proposed opportunities for later scrutiny and corrective exchanges show why repetition can support, rather than undermine, accountability (2017, sections on connecting mini-publics to representative decision making). [Setälä, “Connecting deliberative mini-publics to representative decision making”](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6765.12207)

Fernández-Martínez, Font, and Smith directly ask whether selective listening can be legitimate. Their answer makes public justification central without requiring implementation of every recommendation; they apply this concern to citizens’ assemblies as well (2023, pp. 233–234, 238–239, 249–250). Thus neither post-deliberation selection nor its justificatory burden is introduced here. [“The Sin of Omission?”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377487747_The_Sin_of_Omission_The_Public_Justification_of_Cherry-Picking)

The narrower question concerns selection among reports for the role of completed appraisal, not only selection among recommendations for implementation. That distinction matters because three responses can come apart: officials can explain their policy decision, correctly interpret the whole record, and still fail to explain why one report occupies the consultation’s designated role. Conversely, a justified role need not make the designated report an estimate of typical support. The proposed contribution is this clarification and its transparent stopping example, not a separate theory of democratic legitimacy. Its priority and additional significance remain open.

Other comparisons reinforce the boundary. Bagg distinguishes representation from resistance to capture and makes institutional design central to citizen oversight (2024, “Two Models of Sortition”; “The Structure of Citizen Oversight”). Random recruitment is not a cure for every form of institutional control. [Bagg, “Sortition as Anti-Corruption”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12704)

Membership research addresses a different question. Flanigan and colleagues evaluate selection probabilities for members of an input pool, subject to representation constraints—not unconditional equality for every resident (2021, “Algorithmic framework”). [“Fair algorithms for selecting citizens’ assemblies”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03788-6) Kalaycı and Micha also study sequences: their temporal model takes panel numbers and sizes as inputs and evaluates individual, panel, global, and prefix representation (2026, §2). Fairness over time has not been ignored. Report standing is a different object of assessment, not a failure of those models. [“Temporal Panel Selection in Ongoing Citizens’ Assemblies”](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16194v1)

## 3. What must justify a report’s role?

The three reports in the example share the credentials offered for the third: fair recruitment, suitable information, free deliberation, and authentic authorship. Those shared credentials cannot alone explain why the approving report receives the special role.

The underlying principle is modest: **a feature shared by conflicting reports cannot, without an additional premise, establish the comparative priority of one report.** This does not require equal report weights. A later report might correct an error, answer an objection, contain better reasoning, or hold a separately justified review role. But repeating that its authors were randomly selected does not identify such a difference.

This insufficiency is not peculiar to approval stopping. A fixed-last rule or a lottery among reports also needs a defense of why order or chance should allocate standing. The statistical difference between procedures cannot supply the missing normative premise.

The further premise comes from the claimed task. An institution that invokes outside appraisal as a check on its sponsor must explain how its procedure performs that task. If the sponsor’s satisfaction with the answer is the sole reason for making it the appraisal that counts, the appeal to an outside assessment has not supplied that explanation. The sponsor’s preferred conclusion is doing work at the point where the outside judgment is assigned its role.

Why should this matter? The institution is claiming more than a favorable argument. It invokes an assessment formed outside the sponsor’s own judgment as an additional reason for public confidence. Yet in the specified case, the sponsor decides which of several equally credentialed assessments receives that status by asking whether it agrees. The very agreement to be supported is being used to allocate the supporting role. An additional rationale could break this circle; fair recruitment alone cannot.

This does not mean that an outside check must distribute identical powers to approval and rejection. To avoid that mistake, distinguish three questions:

1. **Outcome dependence:** does the rule select reports according to their verdicts?
2. **Sponsor control:** can the sponsor choose or alter that rule, or its application, to favor the desired answer?
3. **Role justification:** is there an adequate reason for the output to have its assigned standing?

These are different relations. An external body might fix an approval threshold that would remain unchanged if the mayor changed sides. It would be outcome-dependent but insulated from this sponsor’s discretion. It could also be justified. In the target example, sponsor control is a stipulated fact about recommissioning and designation, not something inferred from outcome dependence alone.

Nor does sponsor involvement automatically defeat independence. Officials must select questions, provide resources, and arrange procedures. The objection is functional: their control of report designation must be compatible with the outside-appraisal role being invoked. Not every political choice is disqualifying, and no complete account of that compatibility is supplied here.

With a finite cap, repeated rejection can still end the procedure. Even without a cap, published rejections can influence voters, journalists, and officials. The process therefore need not lose every checking function. My concern is narrower: what licenses treating the selected approval as the completed appraisal, rather than acknowledging the conflicting judgments? Political influence through publicity does not automatically answer that question about institutional standing.

Suppose the third report genuinely offers better reasons. Officials can explain how those reasons answer the earlier objections. Their comparison may justify greater weight. But the rationale is then substantive and comparative, not merely recruitment-based. Likewise, good policy does not automatically vindicate every description of its citizen support. Officials may adopt a sound policy against advice while accurately admitting that the consultation remained divided.

## 4. What stopping changes—and what it does not

A simple model isolates the evidential issue. Suppose potential panels independently approve the same proposal with probability *p*. Hold the question and information fixed. Allow at most *n* panels and compare:

- **Fixed last:** obtain *n* reports and designate the nth, whatever it says.
- **First approval:** stop at the first approval; if all *n* reject, designate the last rejection.

Fixed last produces an approving designated report with probability *p*. First approval produces one with probability

**1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.**

It rejects only if every potential panel rejects. With *p* = 1/4 and *n* = 3, approval has probability 37/64, about 0.578, rather than 0.25. If all-rejection histories instead produce no designated report, approval is certain conditional on such a report existing. Unlimited independent attempts with constant *p* greater than zero eventually produce approval with probability one.

These are elementary calculations, not empirical estimates. Here *p* concerns panel verdicts, not individual citizen support or policy quality. Real panels may influence one another, share shocks, or encounter changing evidence. If all verdicts are perfectly correlated, first approval and fixed last can have the same outcome distribution. Independence and constant *p* are modeling assumptions, not findings about assemblies.

The model nevertheless defeats a sufficiency inference: fairness in recruiting each panel does not guarantee an unselected-panel distribution for the designated answer. Unequal terminal membership chances are not essential to this counterexample. In a stipulated symmetric population where panel identities are independent of verdict variation, equal chances of belonging to the terminal panel can coexist with outcome selection.

The general mechanism is familiar. Stern and colleagues describe an organizer repeating an honest randomization and releasing only the recording best suited to its aims (2020, §4). Their discarded draws are concealed. Here every report is public, so concealment is removed; the separate question is what institutional use follows from the disclosed selection. This is not a new discovery about randomization. [Stern et al., “Randomization and Fair Judgment in Law and Science”](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06709v4)

It would also be wrong to say that a selected approval is necessarily worthless evidence. Compare hypotheses *p* = 1/4 and *p* = 3/4. A likelihood is the probability of the specified observation under a hypothesis. The last column divides the higher-*p* likelihood by the lower-*p* likelihood.

| Specified observation | p = 1/4 | p = 3/4 | Higher/lower likelihood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One unselected panel approves | 1/4 | 3/4 | 3 |
| Approval occurs within three first-approval attempts; the stopping index is unreported | 37/64 | 63/64 | 63/37 |
| The known first-approval procedure stops with approval at attempt three | 9/64 | 3/64 | 1/3 |

The second observation favors the higher approval propensity, though less strongly than the first. The third favors the lower propensity in this comparison. No priors are assumed, and these ratios do not determine final beliefs.

The information sets are crucial. Under the known binary first-approval rule, approval at attempt three already reveals reject–reject–approve. Full report texts are unnecessary to recover that binary history. The second row omits the attempt index; it is not the evidential meaning of every terminal report.

For the reject–reject–approve history, the likelihood is (1 − *p*)²*p* under both fixed-last with three panels and first-approval with cap three. Conditional on the two rejections, the third approval contributes a higher/lower likelihood factor of 3 under either procedure. Stopping adds no automatic penalty to that update.

This qualification accords with existing stopping-rule independence: Hendriksen, de Heide, and Grünwald show, under the stated model conditions, why posterior odds for the same compatible observed data can be independent of which stopping rule produced them (2020, §2.1). That result should not be confused with every other statistical property of optional stopping. [“Optional Stopping with Bayes Factors”](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.09077v3)

The lesson is therefore not universal contamination. It is that evidential meaning depends on the observation and selection procedure. Even when the whole history supports valid inference, a different question remains: why does the approving report occupy the institution’s designated role? Statistical usability cannot itself confer that standing.

## 5. Reopening without making agreement the answer

A first panel can be mistaken, and public inquiry must remain open to correction. Nothing in the preceding argument freezes its report.

Consider a rejection identifying missing evidence about who would bear the congestion charge. An audit addresses the gap, and a further panel assesses the proposal on that improved basis. Either answer ends the specified follow-up; earlier objections remain on record and receive a response. The later report’s place is justified by what the new inquiry addresses, not merely by its approval.

But a demonstrated defect is not necessary for every legitimate repetition. An institution might authorize one replication of a narrowly divided verdict to test stability, whether the narrow majority approved or rejected. Uncertainty can justify further inquiry even when no particular error is known. Another deliberation may also develop arguments from unchanged materials. These are possibilities for justification, not endorsements of every replication scheme.

What needs explanation is why the further inquiry is appropriate and how its results will be used. New information is not sufficient by itself: officials can always commission another fact sheet. Independent administration is not sufficient either: an independent administrator can faithfully execute an approval-seeking rule. Conversely, a defensible rule can give the two verdicts different effects. The issue is not mechanical symmetry but a rationale compatible with the claimed task.

Sincere motives cannot replace that rationale. A mayor might believe every rejection demonstrates misunderstanding. If this belief alone warrants unchanged reruns while approval immediately counts as adequate understanding, it merely restates the preference. A claim about a particular defect needs support. A replication or error-cost justification needs its own defense; neither must pretend to have found a defect.

This raises a boundary question. Could the mayor simply call every rerun a new consultation? Labels cannot determine which obligations have been discharged. Relevant continuity concerns the proposal and question, the outstanding review obligation, the authorizing mandate, and the continued bearing of earlier reports on the citizen warrant now claimed. If officials invoke later approval to settle that same outstanding task, a new name or budget line cannot alone sever the connection.

A materially revised proposal, changed circumstances, or a genuinely new authorization can justify a fresh inquiry. But none automatically makes all earlier objections irrelevant. Those claiming a fresh start should explain which previous task was fulfilled or validly replaced and why the new inquiry has its assigned role. Borderline cases remain contestable. This is a continuity test for the warrant being claimed, not an exhaustive classification of consultation episodes.

Commissioning and policy uptake may also belong to different actors. Then the relevant account must follow their respective powers and commitments rather than attribute all control to one mayor. The principle concerns the institutional chain, not a necessary identity between commissioner and decision-maker.

## 6. A legitimate search and a genuinely asymmetric check

Suppose officials openly seek one considered defense of a policy. They do not claim to measure typical support or discharge an incompatible appraisal obligation. Earlier objections retain their force. Finding an approving panel may reveal arguments that experts missed, and random recruitment still differs from hand-picking known supporters. The present argument does not forbid this search. An authentic endorsement can contribute to debate without receiving a stronger role.

A harder counterexample is an externally authorized gate: a proposal may proceed if at least one of three fairly recruited panels finds it defensible. If that threshold has an adequate justification, the first approval can fulfill it. The approving report witnesses satisfaction of the test; it need not be more authoritative than the contrary reports or defeat their arguments.

Indeed, first-approval stopping and collecting all three reports under an any-approval rule give the same binary result for every possible sequence. Early stopping can simply avoid reports that cannot change that specified result. It does not necessarily replace an independent check with the sponsor’s discretion.

There can even be a substantive rationale for this asymmetry. As a toy example, suppose good and bad policies are initially equally likely. Conditional on policy type, independent panels approve with probabilities 3/4 and 1/4 respectively. Suppose mistakenly rejecting a good policy has loss 10, while mistakenly approving a bad policy has loss 1. The expected loss of any approval in three is 47/128, compared with 11/8 for one panel and 55/64 for majority-of-three. Under these stipulated costs, asymmetric error allocation has a reason beyond the sponsor’s liking for approval.

This example adds assumptions about quality that the earlier model did not make. It is not evidence about real assemblies, and expected loss does not settle democratic legitimacy. The values, reliability assumptions, costs, distribution of opportunities, and mandate all require defense. Mere enactment or advance announcement is not enough.

Nevertheless, the gate is a genuine limit on the argument. Outcome-dependent standing can be both independent of a particular sponsor’s preferences and justified. Its warrant comes from the defended task together with appropriate recruitment and deliberation, not from recruitment alone. A procedure may legitimately be asymmetric; it may not simply borrow the warrant of a different task without explaining the difference.

## 7. Two burdens, not a universal stopping rule

Institutions claiming citizen warrant face two distinct burdens.

The first is **evidential calibration**: matching a claim to what the observation supports, not a further statistical guarantee. What does the observation show, given the selection procedure and the observer’s information? An indexed approval may already reveal the binary history; accessible report texts can additionally reveal the arguments and disagreements. When panel probabilities are unknown, numerical adjustment may be unavailable. Accurate qualitative claims remain possible. Finding an endorsement is not the same as measuring typical considered support.

The second is **justification of standing**: why does this report complete this task, and what becomes of earlier judgments? Valid inference from the public history cannot replace that explanation. Conversely, a justified gate can confer standing without producing a typical-panel verdict. An explanation for adopting policy also need not explain why a selected report should be presented as the consultation’s appraisal.

These burdens fit existing whole-process standards. The OECD already calls for public process design, a commitment to respond or act, participant control over recommendation wording, and arm’s-length process decisions (2020, ch. 5, “Accountability,” “Transparency,” “Integrity”). These are prescriptions, not proof of universal compliance or an explicit ban on the exact stopping procedure. [OECD, *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2020/06/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_11aa2baf/339306da-en.pdf)

A duty to respond also differs from a duty to obey. Ireland’s Citizens’ Assembly describes its recommendations as advisory while stating advance commitments by government and parliament to detailed responses. That is evidence of the institution’s stated arrangement, not independent verification of compliance or report shopping. [Citizens’ Assembly, “FAQ,” completion and implementation questions](https://citizensassembly.ie/about/faq/)

For the mayor’s case, the practical questions are these:

- What appraisal or threshold was authorized, and what rules connect reports to that role?
- Who controls continuation and designation? What explains a later report’s standing: better arguments, justified replication, a material change, an aggregate test, or simply agreement?
- What exactly is claimed about citizen judgment, and how are earlier contrary reports represented and answered?

The immediate remedy need not be another assembly. Officials can correct an inflated attribution, acknowledge the disagreement, answer adverse arguments, and accept responsibility for their policy choice. Where an outside appraisal was promised, they must also explain whether and how that task has been fulfilled. Which remedy suffices depends on the mandate, not on the lottery alone.

## 8. The limited conclusion

No general theory of legitimate commissioning has been established. Nor has a verified real case of unchanged-question first-approval recommissioning, an empirical estimate of panel variation, or a complete criterion for separating consultation episodes. The statistical results are elementary, and the general concerns about selective uptake and institutional control have clear predecessors. Whether this application adds enough beyond them remains a substantive question; its precise priority is unresolved.

What survives is a distinction between authenticity, calibrated evidence, and institutional standing. Fair recruitment, together with the separately stipulated conditions of free and informed deliberation, can leave every judgment authentic while failing to justify the role assigned to one of them. Recruitment alone does not even guarantee those deliberative conditions. Publicity can preserve usable information without justifying every official use of it.

A lottery chooses who deliberates. It does not, merely by choosing them fairly, explain which of their completed answers should count as the institution’s appraisal.

The bibliography lists sources used, not works read exhaustively.
