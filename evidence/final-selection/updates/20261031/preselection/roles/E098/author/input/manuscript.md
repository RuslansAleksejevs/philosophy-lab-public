# Being Able to Stop

A carpenter sands a panel while thinking about a conversation. She can stop the moment someone asks. But can she keep the sanding within the marked limit if she will not notice when she reaches it?

Her skill with the wood may be unchanged. She can still keep the surface smooth, adjust the pressure, and stop her hands. What is missing is a way for the approaching limit to affect her work. If nobody warns her, her ability to stop will remain unused.

Now suppose a colleague is watching and will warn her in time. Does this restore her control? It is tempting to say yes only if she asked for the help, or at least knows about it. I will argue that this is too demanding. A reliable warning can support her control through her ordinary ability to understand and assess warnings, even if she does not know who is watching. What matters is whether the help reaches her own guidance of the task, rather than simply ensuring the desired result around her.

This gives us two conclusions. The ability to stop after a warning does not establish control unless a suitable warning can actually reach the performer. Yet the performer need not have arranged, or even anticipated, every source that makes her control possible. Control of a task in the present setting and the ability to secure that control for oneself are different achievements.

## What the stopping ability leaves open

A habit is a way of acting that tends to recur in familiar circumstances. A skill is an ability to perform an activity well. The carpenter habitually starts sanding from one end, and she has the skill to keep the surface even. Habits need not be skilled, and skills need not be habitual. My subject is their familiar overlap: practiced work that needs little deliberate attention to its details.

We should distinguish three things. The carpenter has learned how to sand. She may currently have the means to keep this job within its limit. And she may succeed in doing so on this attempt. A practiced ability can remain when its use is blocked. A usable ability can also be exercised badly. Success alone does not settle either question.

The opening example concerns the second question: what the carpenter can currently do in the conditions she faces. Suppose her absorption is deep enough that the mark cannot draw her attention while she works, although speech can. This is a temporary blockage of one route to stopping. It is not merely the prediction that a generally attentive carpenter will happen to overlook the mark once. One failure would not establish that she lacks control. Conversely, a blockage can exist without causing failure, if the sanding happens to end early for another reason.

There are also different aspects of the job to control. Can she adjust the sanding pressure? Can she keep sanding only while doing so serves her plan for the panel? She can retain the first ability while losing the second. Calling her simply controlled or uncontrolled hides the difference.

Fridland's account of skill separates control of strategy, attention, and movement. Attention can respond to a performer's aims without being consciously directed at each feature. Practice includes learning what to notice, as well as learning how to move (Fridland 2014, §§4.1–4.3). Nothing here requires silent instructions about each stroke of the sandpaper.

The problem arises when an explanation appeals only to what would happen if she noticed a reason to stop. She might stop if she noticed the mark, yet have no working way to notice it while she sands. A disconnected fire alarm could make someone leave if it sounded. Its ability to produce that response does not show that it currently provides protection from fire. Similarly, a stopping response does not establish control over when a routine ends.

This analogy identifies a missing part of the explanation. A claim about responding to a warning leaves unanswered how the warning can arrive. If we want to explain control of sanding to the marked depth, we need both parts.

No person must detect every possible reason for stopping. The carpenter need not sense an invisible flaw. The ordinary variations that matter here include reaching the mark at different speeds as the wood changes. We identify those variations from the job, before knowing whether she succeeds. I offer no universal numerical threshold for reliability. The comparison concerns a clear contrast between a route that usually works through such variations and one that is blocked throughout the task.

## The colleague she did not ask for

Consider two workshops. In the first, the carpenter asks a colleague to watch the mark and call out when it is reached. If the plan changes, she updates a work order that the colleague reads. She is ready to hear and assess the warning. If it is accurate, she stops; if the colleague has misunderstood the work order, she checks and corrects the mistake.

In the second workshop, the colleague undertakes exactly the same monitoring without telling her. The colleague reads the same public work order, notices every relevant update, and issues equally timely warnings. The carpenter is equally ready to assess clear warnings from anyone nearby. She can stop, check the mark, or reject a warning that does not fit the revised job. For the duration of this job, the colleague's presence is equally dependable in both workshops.

Does the second carpenter have less control of the sanding limit? There is a difference in preparation: she did not arrange the protection and cannot be credited with doing so. There may also be a difference in what she is justified in expecting. But neither difference establishes less ability to control this job now. The warning can reach her; she can understand it; and what she does with it depends on her plan. All of those facts hold before the warning arrives.

This last point matters because control is partly a readiness to act. The first carpenter need not actually receive a warning before possessing control. She has it because the arrangement will work if the relevant condition occurs. We should apply the same test to the second carpenter. Her ordinary openness to warnings can connect the reliable outside help to her guidance of the task. Requiring an additional prior decision to rely on this particular colleague would exclude help without identifying a missing practical ability.

There is a familiar parallel. A person may have an unobstructed view through a clean window without knowing who cleaned it. The setting enables her perception; credit for establishing that setting belongs elsewhere. Outside monitoring does more than clean a window, since another person selects the information. But in the stipulated case that selection remains responsive to the carpenter's actual task. Its social origin alone does not make her ability to use it less real.

The conclusion is limited. An accidental shout that happens to arrive at the right instant does not establish a dependable ability over the interval. Nor does the carpenter gain a portable monitoring skill that she will carry to the next workshop. She has control in this setting because she has a suitable way to receive and use information here. If the unknown colleague leaves, that control can disappear without the loss of anything she has learned.

The strongest objection is that an unknown arrangement cannot be something she controls *through*. This objection treats “through” as requiring advance reliance. But that would settle the matter by choosing a word. The substantive question is whether she can guide the task when its conditions change. In the paired cases she can do so equally well. To deny this on the ground that she did not expect the help would turn a difference in her evidence about control into a difference in control itself.

This is not a reason to call her decision to work unattended sensible. She may be taking an unreasonable chance as far as she knows. A person can possess more control than she reasonably believes she has, just as she can reasonably believe she has control when an unknown malfunction has removed it.

## Protection that bypasses her

Now change the hidden help. Instead of warning the carpenter, the colleague silently moves the panel away whenever its limit is reached. Suppose the carpenter cannot prevent or alter this intervention. The panel is equally well protected, but the protection now bypasses her ability to understand, judge, and stop.

She still controls her sanding movements. What she lacks is the additional control supplied by receiving a warning. Her readiness to assess information plays no role in the new protection. If the same intervention would work with an unresponsive performer, that helps explain why it cannot, by itself, establish the carpenter's control of the limit.

The contrast is not between internal and external processes. Both warnings and physical removal come from outside. It is between assistance that enables the performer's guidance and assistance that replaces it. A protector can ensure a good outcome without improving what the protected person can do.

This gives a better test than asking whether the carpenter knows the helper. First, does the help provide timely information about conditions that matter for this job? Second, can her understanding of that information guide the response? Third, does she retain a way to change her use of the help when the job changes? These are checks on whether the arrangement supplies the kind of ongoing control at issue, not a complete definition of agency.

The third check does not require power over the alarm or the colleague. A fixed alarm can be entirely useful even if she cannot reprogram it. She may use it as a reason to pause and inspect, rather than as a command to end the job. What must remain flexible is her use of the signal. A warning about an old limit need not defeat her control if she can recognize that it no longer applies.

This also explains why merely serving her earlier plan is insufficient. Suppose she voluntarily installs a device that physically prevents any further sanding after a preset point, even if she later decides that the panel needs more work. The device may be a wise commitment and an effective exercise of earlier control. While locked, however, it reduces her present ability to revise this aspect of the work. Describing that trade-off does not condemn commitment devices. It distinguishes execution of an earlier decision from current freedom to redirect the task.

There are mixed cases. A device might stop the sanding automatically but allow her to inspect and restart. It then protects the panel and preserves a further route for her guidance. The question is what the whole arrangement lets her do, not whether every intervention waits for her permission.

## Control need not make the helper part of the mind

Clark and Chalmers argue that external resources can participate in cognition. Their notebook example emphasizes dependable access and habitual use; their discussion of social cases stresses trust, reliance, and accessibility. They do not present every feature as an exceptionless necessary condition (Clark and Chalmers 1998, §§3–5).

That discussion raises an important boundary here. To claim that the hidden colleague supports the carpenter's current control is not to claim that the colleague's information already constitutes her standing beliefs. She need not believe that the limit has been reached before being told. A source can enable her to acquire and use information without already being part of what she believes. Conditions for attributing a lasting belief therefore cannot simply be imposed on control of a particular task.

The argument also differs from an account of precautions. Schwind holds that obligations can concern arrangements that keep existing knowledge available, including external safeguards (Schwind 2026, §§4.1–4.3). My claim concerns what an arrangement enables, not whether anyone fulfilled a duty to establish it. Unrequested help can increase a person's current control without showing that she took reasonable precautions. Conversely, a reasonable precaution can fail and leave her without control through no fault of her own.

The general importance of attention, reliable support, and responsiveness to goals is established territory. The narrower point defended here is that prior reliance on a particular external source is not required for that source to support control of a present task. The paired workshops isolate this point by holding the warning route, its reliability, and its responsiveness to changed plans fixed. The difference in preparation remains real, but it does not track the difference in current control.

## No endless chain of watchers

Must someone watch the colleague, and someone else watch that watcher? Only if control requires guaranteed success. The account requires no such guarantee. A trained capacity can operate without deliberate supervision, and a dependable colleague can do the same. Their possible failure does not entail a missing second observer.

A further check may be needed where failures are frequent or the task unusually demanding. That is a question about the conditions of this job, not a logical demand for an endless chain. If no available arrangement is dependable enough, the person lacks that degree of control. We should not invent a perfect final observer to avoid the conclusion.

Ordinary habits need not have carefully planned stopping points. Someone can intentionally hum without aiming to keep the humming within a time limit. Failing to watch the clock then shows no loss of control over such a limit. If she undertakes to hum until a recording ends, the situation changes: ending at that point is now part of what she is doing.

Nor can we protect every claim of control by shrinking the task after a failure. The carpenter undertook to preserve the marked thickness. If she loses every way of noticing the mark, we cannot redescribe her job as moving sandpaper and say that nothing was lost. We can credit the control she retains without erasing the part she lacks.

## What skill does not settle

One observer says that the carpenter is still skilled; another says she cannot control this job. Both can be right. The ability to sand smoothly can remain while the information needed to end the task becomes unavailable. Restoring a warning route can restore her control without improving her technique.

Equally, a person can have the means to control a job without having earned credit for securing them. The hidden colleague illustrates this possibility. Denying her current control because the help was unrequested would make control depend too heavily on its preparation. Crediting her with the helper's monitoring skill would go too far in the other direction.

We should therefore ask separate questions: what has the person learned, what can she currently receive and use, and what can she reliably arrange for herself? Habit and skill contribute to all three, but do not make them identical. Being able to stop answers only part of the question about control. The rest concerns whether the world can reach the person's guidance of the task—and that connection can be supplied by help she did not know she had.
