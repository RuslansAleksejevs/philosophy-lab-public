# Being Able to Stop

A carpenter sands a panel while thinking about a conversation. She can stop the moment someone asks. But can she keep the sanding within the marked limit if she will not notice when she reaches it?

Her skill with the wood may be unchanged. She can still keep the surface smooth, adjust the pressure, and stop her hands. What is missing is a way for the approaching limit to affect her work. If nobody warns her, her ability to stop will remain unused.

Now suppose a colleague is watching and will warn her in time. The warning can make a difference to what she can do. Yet this leaves an important question unanswered: does the carpenter control the whole process, or does one person monitor while another controls the response?

I will defend a qualified answer. Reliable help can support the carpenter's own guidance even when she did not arrange or anticipate it. But support does not transfer the helper's monitoring to her. We must distinguish the ability to guide a task using available information from control over the activity that makes that information available. These can belong to different people within one successful performance.

The distinction matters in both directions. Denying the carpenter any present control overlooks her ability to assess a warning and direct her work. Attributing the entire arrangement to her overlooks the independent agency that keeps her informed. An ability to respond, a working connection to the relevant facts, and responsibility for maintaining that connection are different things.

## What the stopping ability leaves open

A habit is a way of acting that tends to recur in familiar circumstances. A skill is an ability to perform an activity well. The carpenter habitually starts sanding from one end, and she has the skill to keep the surface even. Habits need not be skilled, and skills need not be habitual. My subject is their familiar overlap: practiced work that needs little deliberate attention to its details.

We should distinguish three questions. Has the carpenter learned how to sand? Does she currently have the means to keep this job within its limit? Does she succeed on this attempt? A practiced ability can remain when its use is blocked. A usable ability can also be exercised badly. Success alone does not settle either question.

The opening example concerns the second question. Suppose her absorption is deep enough that the mark cannot draw her attention while she works, although speech can. After an intelligible warning she can pause, inspect the mark, and reconsider. This is a temporary blockage of the ordinary visual route while sanding, not merely a prediction that an attentive carpenter will happen to overlook the mark once. One failure would not establish that she lacks control. Conversely, the blockage can exist without causing failure if the job happens to end early.

There are also different aspects of the job to control. Can she adjust the pressure? Can she keep sanding only while it serves her plan for the panel? She can retain the first ability while losing the means needed for the second. Calling her simply controlled or uncontrolled hides the difference.

Fridland distinguishes strategic control, selective attention, and motor control. On her account, attention can be responsive to the performer's aims without being consciously directed at each feature; practice includes learning what to notice, as well as how to move. Nothing in the present argument requires silent instructions about each stroke of the sandpaper. ([Fridland 2014, §§4.1–4.3](https://www.ellenfridland.com/uploads/8/0/1/2/80126914/theyve_lost_control_doi_pdf.pdf))

The problem arises when an explanation appeals only to what would happen if the carpenter noticed a reason to stop. She might stop if she noticed the mark, yet have no working way to notice it while sanding. A disconnected fire alarm could make someone leave if it sounded. Its capacity to elicit that response does not show that it currently provides protection from fire. Similarly, a stopping response does not establish control over when a routine ends.

We must identify the relevant variations from the task before knowing its outcome. For sanding to a marked thickness, they include reaching the mark sooner because the wood is softer. They need not include an invisible defect that no available method could detect. No universal numerical threshold of reliability is needed for the present comparison. The contrast is between a route that works dependably across the specified variations and a route that is blocked throughout the job.

## When information supports someone's guidance

A collection of useful capacities need not form a usable ability. Mayr and Vetter make this point central to their account of adaptive abilities: powers to perform an activity must be integrated with powers to monitor, reconsider, and interrupt it. They distinguish flexibility in how one proceeds from flexibility in whether and when one proceeds. Their account leaves the particular implementation of these powers open. It does not, by that fact alone, assign a second person's monitoring to the performer. ([Mayr and Vetter 2023, §§2, 4–6](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/phis.12249))

For the carpenter, three checks help identify an effective connection between information and her guidance.

First, **access**: changes relevant to the job must have a dependable route to her before adjustment becomes too late. This is a claim about a standing arrangement, not a lucky message on one occasion.

Second, **assessment**: the information must enter her understanding of the task, and her assessment must help determine what she does. A different warranted assessment should be capable of making a difference to the response. She can stop at the old limit, inspect an ambiguous mark, or continue under a changed instruction. An irresistible startle that always freezes her hands does not meet this condition merely because she can also understand what was shouted.

Third, **revision**: her use of the information must remain responsive to changes in her purposes. She can abandon the job, change its target, or change what a warning is a reason to do. This need not involve deliberation at every stroke. A practiced, flexible response can express her understanding without a fresh explicit decision each time.

The checks do more than add together a capacity to hear and a capacity to stop. They concern how those capacities are connected. If her understanding changes but her response cannot change with it, the warning does not support the guidance at issue. If she can judge correctly but no warning can reach her, the connection is incomplete in another way.

The checks also have independent grounds. Access distinguishes dependable assistance from coincidence. Assessment distinguishes enabling her action from moving her body or protecting the panel around her. Revision distinguishes guidance of an ongoing task from the irreversible execution of an earlier instruction. These are reasons to look for the connections, rather than simply declaring that helpful information is hers.

They are not a complete theory of agency. They presuppose an ordinary competent performer whose judgments and movements are her own; they do not settle every problem about manipulation or ownership of psychological mechanisms. More immediately, they identify how an arrangement can support her guidance without yet settling who controls every activity within that arrangement. That further question requires us to follow the contributions separately.

## Three equally reliable workshops

Consider three hypothetical workshops. In each, the carpenter undertakes the same job, possesses the same sanding skill, and can assess the relevant information equally well once it reaches her. Hold fixed the accuracy and timing of that information across the ordinary variations just described. Hold fixed, too, her ability to pause, inspect, correct a misunderstanding, and revise the job. These are stipulated comparisons, not reports of measured human performance.

In **the first workshop**, her attention is available to the mark. Her practiced observation registers its approach while she sands. She adjusts her observation to the current target and stops or inspects when appropriate. She both monitors the limit and guides the response.

In **the second workshop**, her visual attention while sanding is blocked as in the opening case. She has asked a colleague to watch and warn her. The colleague reads her work order and its updates. When warned, she can pause to inspect, accept an accurate report, or reject a report based on a misunderstanding. The colleague supplies information; she decides how it bears on continuing.

In **the third workshop**, the same assistance is undertaken without telling her. A colleague reads the same public work order, notices the same updates, and issues equally timely warnings. The carpenter is ordinarily receptive to intelligible warnings from people nearby. She need not know who is watching in order to understand a warning about the panel. The colleague's presence and monitoring are equally dependable for this job.

All three meet the access, assessment, and revision checks. In each, a relevant change can reach the carpenter's understanding and affect her conduct in a way sensitive to her present task. Thus the third case gives her more than an idle ability to stop if some unspecified event occurred. There is an existing route by which the event that matters will prompt her own response.

But the cases do not establish equality in every kind of control. In the first workshop, the carpenter performs the monitoring. In the other two, the colleague performs it. The carpenter's ability to use a warning does not explain why the colleague looks at the mark, notices a new instruction, or continues watching. Those events require another person's contribution.

This is not a negligible difference in the history of an otherwise identical ability. During the job itself, different people exercise the powers that keep the relevant facts available. The arrangement is distributed in the second and third workshops even if its reliability matches the first.

What, then, does prior agreement add? At a minimum, it changes the carpenter's information about the support and may give her credit for arranging it. It can also add a present practical power: she may be able to ask the colleague to change position, check a second mark, or hand over the monitoring at a chosen time. Such powers matter. They are powers to organize the help, not simply better grounds for expecting it.

However, an agreement does not automatically supply those powers. If the colleague has agreed only to the stipulated warning policy, the carpenter may have no additional effective way to direct the monitoring. Conversely, a previously unknown helper may become readily available for coordination after the first warning. We must examine what each person can actually do. Prior knowledge is neither a necessary condition of useful assistance nor a sufficient condition of control over its organization.

The controlled comparison therefore yields a limited equality: equal access to information for guiding the response within the specified task. It does not erase differences in who monitors, who can reorganize the monitoring, or who was justified in relying on it.

## Support is not a transfer of control

To locate control, ask two different questions. Whose understanding guides whether the carpenter stops, inspects, or continues? And whose activity keeps changes at the mark available for that understanding? In the first workshop both answers are the carpenter. In the other workshops the answers diverge.

This way of assigning contributions does not require every physical cause of a performance to be under the performer's direction. A carpenter need not control daylight in order to see. The relevant contrast is that, in the assisted cases, an identifiable part of the task's monitoring is performed through another person's attention and judgment. The colleague does not become a passive feature of the environment just because the assistance is reliable.

Nor does the colleague's role make the carpenter a passive endpoint. Imagine holding the warning fixed while changing the carpenter's assessment in a task-relevant way. If the work order has legitimately changed, she can inspect and continue. If it has not, she can stop. The helper supplies a reason for attention; the carpenter's understanding helps settle what follows. Remove that capacity and substitute an unthinking compulsory response, and something in the carpenter's control is lost even if the panel remains equally safe.

Now make the complementary change. Leave the carpenter's responsive capacities intact but remove the colleague's monitoring, with no replacement. The reliable route to those capacities disappears. This locates a second contribution. It does not show that the first contribution was unreal.

The resulting attribution is layered. The carpenter has personal guidance supported by the colleague. The monitoring-and-response arrangement depends on both. Neither claim entails that the whole arrangement is under the carpenter's sole direction. The second and third workshops involve a division of control; only the second necessarily includes an agreement about it. Whether the third counts as a joint action in a stricter sense is a further question. Nothing here requires a shared intention or a new collective agent.

A natural objection remains: before the first warning, perhaps the carpenter has only the possibility of regaining control later. This objection correctly denies that she is personally exercising the colleague's monitoring in the meantime. It does not establish that the supported ability begins only when the message arrives. The colleague is already watching, the route to her is already available, and she is already able to use it. The arrangement is in place throughout the interval in a way that an accidental rescuing shout is not.

We should therefore distinguish a presently available supported capacity from its exercise at a particular moment. She need not have received a warning to possess the former. But her possession of it does not mean that she has already exercised the judgment she will exercise on receiving the message. Nor does it license the stronger claim that she has been personally supervising the limit all along.

Someone may reserve the unqualified phrase “control of the whole task” for a performer who also directs its monitoring. On that usage, the unknown helper does not restore such control. The important point survives: the helper can restore an existing route for the carpenter's own guidance while leaving control of surveillance elsewhere. The distinction states what is gained and what remains missing without settling the issue by a choice of words.

## Protection that bypasses her

Now change the hidden help. Instead of warning the carpenter, the colleague silently moves the panel away at its limit. Suppose the carpenter cannot prevent or alter this intervention. The panel is equally well protected, but its protection bypasses her understanding of the limit.

She still controls her sanding movements. What is absent is the contribution supported by a usable warning: her assessment does not determine how the limit is handled. The intervention would protect the panel even if she were entirely unresponsive to information about it. This explains why protection by itself cannot establish her guidance of that aspect of the job.

The contrast is not between internal and external processes. Both warnings and physical removal come from outside. It is between enabling the performer's guidance and replacing a contribution that guidance could otherwise make. An outside process can improve what she can do, improve what happens to the panel, or do both. These improvements should not be conflated.

The distinction also applies to devices. A fixed alarm can support guidance even if the carpenter cannot reprogram it. She may treat its signal as a reason to pause and inspect rather than as a command to finish. What must remain flexible is her practical use of it.

There is a limit to this flexibility. If she moves the target beyond the alarm's setting, and the alarm sounds only once, simply ignoring it will not preserve monitoring of the new target. She needs to inspect while proceeding or obtain another route to the relevant information. An ability to reject a signal is not enough when rejection leaves the task informationally unattended.

Suppose instead that she voluntarily installs a device that prevents further sanding after a preset point even if she later decides that more work is needed. It may be a wise commitment and an effective exercise of earlier control. While locked, however, it removes one present way of redirecting the work. Describing that trade-off does not condemn commitment devices. It distinguishes fulfillment of an earlier decision from current scope for revision.

There are mixed cases. A device might stop the sanding automatically but permit inspection and restart. It then protects the panel while preserving a further route for her guidance. We must ask what the arrangement lets her assess and change, rather than treating every unrequested physical intervention as a complete loss of control.

## Belief, preparation, and credit

The claim about supported guidance does not require making the helper part of the carpenter's mind. Clark and Chalmers' account of extended belief emphasizes the role of stable access and established use in its notebook example, and trust, reliance, and accessibility in its social examples. Their discussion leaves room for borderline cases. ([Clark and Chalmers 1998, §§4–5](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html))

Here the carpenter need not have a standing belief that the limit has been reached before anyone tells her. The helper enables her to acquire information and use it in action. That is different from the helper's information already constituting her belief. Conditions for the latter cannot simply decide whether the former supports her guidance.

The same distinction separates having support from having prepared responsibly. Schwind's precautionist account concerns duties to maintain the conditions through which existing knowledge becomes available for action. Its subject is a normative demand on the agent's preparations and routines. ([Schwind 2026, §4.1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05785-6))

Unrequested help can improve the carpenter's present means without showing that she took reasonable precautions. Conversely, a reasonable precaution can fail and leave the relevant route unavailable through no fault of hers. Nothing in the three workshops allocates moral blame merely by identifying who supplies a part of the control.

The distinction also prevents an inflation of credit. The carpenter has not acquired the colleague's monitoring skill. She cannot carry it away to another workshop, and she deserves no credit for secretly arranging what she did not arrange. But the colleague's achievement does not consume every achievement in the performance. Understanding a warning, recognizing its bearing on a changed task, and adjusting one's conduct can remain the carpenter's contributions.

A person can thus have more support for present guidance than she reasonably believes she has. That does not mean her decision to begin work was sensible. It also does not mean that all differences between arranged and unarranged help concern sensible expectation. Effective authority to reorganize help is a genuine further capacity when an agreement supplies it.

## No endless chain of watchers

Must someone watch the colleague, and someone else watch that watcher? Only if control requires guaranteed success. Nothing here requires that. Practiced attention can operate without deliberate supervision, and dependable help can occasionally fail. Possible failure does not by itself entail a missing second observer.

A further check may be needed when failures are common or the task is unusually demanding. That is a question about what this job requires, not a logical demand for an endless chain. If no available arrangement is dependable enough, the performer lacks the corresponding degree of supported control.

Ordinary habits need not have carefully planned stopping points. Someone can intentionally hum without aiming to keep the humming within a time limit. Failing to watch the clock then shows no loss of control over such a limit. If she undertakes to hum until a recording ends, ending there becomes part of the task and requires an appropriate route to adjustment.

Nor can we protect a claim of control by shrinking the task after a failure. The carpenter undertook to preserve the marked thickness. Losing every way of registering the mark removes something relevant to that undertaking. We can credit her remaining control of pressure without pretending that nothing was lost. Equally, restoring a warning route restores something relevant without making every part of the undertaking hers to manage.

## What being able to stop establishes

One observer says that the carpenter is still skilled; another says that she cannot monitor this job. Both can be right. Technique can remain when the information needed to end the task becomes unavailable. An outside warning route can change what she is able to guide without improving that technique.

The further question is whose control the route involves. A reliable warning can be integrated with the carpenter's judgment even though another person controls its provision. Advance agreement can add knowledge, credit, and sometimes a power to organize the help. It does not automatically turn another person's monitoring into her own.

The conclusion is therefore neither that the carpenter must arrange every source of help nor that reliable responsiveness gives her sole control of the task. Being able to stop is one contribution. Having a dependable route from the changing task to one's judgment is another. Who sustains that route is a third question, which success and reliability cannot answer alone.
