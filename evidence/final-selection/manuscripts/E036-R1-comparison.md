# No Penalty for Silence

## Privacy, fair lotteries, and corrective evidence

A training programme has one fellowship and three qualified applicants: Ada, Ben, and Cy. Its work sample and interview establish that each can benefit from the training. Under its published rules, the programme allocates the place by an equal lottery among qualified applicants. It also assures applicants that withholding intimate records will not reduce their chances.

Ada volunteers her diary. It provides convincing evidence of perseverance. She understands the risks and wants the selectors to use it. They award her the fellowship, explaining that they have drawn no negative inference about Ben or Cy. Neither applicant has lost points; Ada has supplied additional positive evidence.

The explanation misses the relevant loss. Ben and Cy each had a one-third chance under the lottery. Each now has none. Whether that loss wrongs them depends on why the lottery governed selection. Privacy alone does not establish a right to one-third. Nor does the fact that the applicants previously enjoyed that chance. But if the programme owes these qualified applicants equal chances under a justified procedure, Ada's permission to read her diary does not authorize the programme to take their shares away.

This essay defends that narrower claim. Its central case is an institution that justifiably distributes a fixed opportunity by an equal lottery among eligible applicants and limits what evidence may affect eligibility. Here the size of the protected chance follows from the allocation principle, the number of eligible claimants, and the available capacity. Privacy helps justify the evidential boundary; it does not independently generate a numerical entitlement.

The protection must also accommodate correction. A diary might expose an unjust exclusion while revealing exceptional merit. Such evidence should receive the effect needed to repair the injustice under the justified procedure. That does not automatically license every competitive benefit the evidence could support. The resulting account protects a correction-sensitive entitlement, rather than an applicant's position before errors are discovered.

## 1. Access, assessment, and allocation

Privacy unraveling provides one reason to question voluntary disclosure. People with favourable private information disclose it, making silence increasingly suspicious and placing others under pressure to follow. Peppet's account, as described in the available abstract summary, identifies this threat without assuming forcible collection. Voluntary individual decisions can undermine privacy collectively. [Peppet 2011, abstract](https://scholar.law.colorado.edu/faculty-articles/177/).

Other discussions already challenge the idea that individual permission settles the legitimacy of an informational practice. The available account of Nissenbaum emphasizes justified contextual norms, including norms governing use. Its account of Pasquale emphasizes effects on third parties and the manipulation of baselines against which benefits are offered. The distinction between consent and a justified practice is therefore not offered here as a new discovery. [Nissenbaum 2004, §§III.C–IV](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/H.%20Nissenbaum%2C%20_Privacy%20as%20Contextual%20Integrity.pdf); [Pasquale 2021, §§3.4–3.5](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108914857.004).

The available discussion of Moore and Martin raises a useful question about first disclosures: a waiver made before disclosure pressure develops may be permissible. But what does its permissibility authorize? Ada may permit access to her diary without thereby settling the institution's obligations to Ben. [Moore and Martin 2020, pp. 218–219](https://faculty.washington.edu/moore2/PTPD.pdf).

Three claims need separating. A claim against access governs whether the programme may inspect Ada's diary. A claim concerning assessment governs which evidence may affect institutional decisions. A claim concerning allocation governs what opportunity an applicant receives under that assessment procedure. Ada can waive the first claim without possessing authority over Ben's claims under the other two.

This distinction matters even without unraveling. Suppose Ada submits her diary immediately before the deadline. Ben and Cy have no opportunity to respond, everyone knows that their silence is uninformative, and the diary concerns only Ada. There is no adverse inference, no subsequent pressure, and no disclosure of another person's secrets. Awarding Ada the place still removes the others' lottery chances.

An institution can consequently protect silence in several different senses. It can prohibit compelled disclosure, refrain from treating silence as evidence of a defect, or preserve the opportunity assigned by its authorized procedure. The first two protections do not entail the third. A score can remain unchanged while its holder's chance disappears.

None of this yet shows that the chance was owed. A competitor's better authorized work sample can legitimately reduce another applicant's prospects. To identify a wrong, we need a defended allocation rule and an account of which changes that rule permits.

## 2. Where the size of a protected chance comes from

Adequate access is weaker than an entitlement to a particular chance. Ben might retain a reasonable opportunity to apply even if Ada's disclosure reduces his likelihood of success. It would therefore beg the question to infer a one-third entitlement simply from the need for a usable route that does not require intimate exposure.

The stronger conclusion requires an independent distributive premise. Consider a programme whose justified purpose is to offer training to people who can benefit from it, with an equal lottery among those who meet a suitable threshold. The programme is distributing an opportunity to develop a capacity. It need not treat the place as a prize for whoever can demonstrate the greatest existing capacity.

Why might this arrangement be justified? First, all eligible applicants have established the qualification that makes the training appropriate for them. Second, the programme recognizes their claims to this opportunity as equal once that qualification is met. Third, no further relevant priority—such as a remedial priority for disadvantaged applicants—applies in the case. An equal lottery then expresses equal claims to an indivisible benefit. With one place and three eligible applicants, equal treatment requires equal probabilities; allocating the place requires those probabilities to sum to one. Each receives one-third.

The arithmetic determines the size only after the moral premises identify equal claimants and commit the whole place to them. Probability conservation does not establish either premise.

There are reasons to endorse those premises in some training programmes. A public institution may reasonably distribute development opportunities broadly instead of directing every scarce benefit toward the strongest existing performer. Among applicants capable of benefiting, small differences in predictive confidence need not establish stronger claims. Where further comparison also invites costly exposure of private life, equal allocation among qualified applicants can serve both the distributive purpose and a proportionate limit on institutional inquiry.

These reasons do not show that every training programme must use a lottery. A programme pursuing a defensible objective of selecting the very strongest candidate may justifiably compare eligible applicants further. A programme serving a remedial purpose may owe unequal priority. The argument applies where equal claims among qualified applicants withstand those alternatives. Calling a lottery a privacy measure cannot substitute for that defence.

Privacy contributes a related but distinct reason. An institution's need for evidence does not entitle it to every increment of predictive information. Diaries about grief, intimate relationships, or religious life may reveal perseverance, but a work sample and a proportionate interview may adequately establish the relevant capacity. A programme can then reasonably limit eligibility assessment to those methods, while providing fair alternatives and correction procedures. The protected boundary concerns how qualifications may be established; it does not assert that intimate evidence is invariably unreliable or irrelevant.

Once the programme justifiably recognizes equal claims and allocates the entire place through their lottery, a smaller guarantee needs a further justification. Suppose it instead gives Ada, Ben, and Cy a guaranteed one-sixth chance each, reserving the remaining half for applicants who provide revealing biographies. Each retains some access. Yet the scheme no longer treats their claims as equal after eligibility. It introduces a second principle under which intimate evidence earns priority.

That second principle might be defended in another institutional arrangement. It cannot be justified merely by observing that one-sixth is still a meaningful chance. Nor can an institution reserve a discretionary portion on paper and thereby establish its moral freedom to distribute that portion however it likes. Administrative noncommitment is not the same as justified discretion.

The published lottery also creates a reason for procedural fidelity. Applicants organize their participation around the announced terms. A legitimate commitment to an equal lottery should not be abandoned during selection simply because one applicant offers information the institution finds attractive. This supports the entitlement but cannot make an otherwise unjust lottery legitimate.

The claim is therefore specific: where equal lottery shares are independently owed, privacy-related disclosure cannot by itself justify reducing them. The protected amount comes from the justified allocation rule. Privacy explains part of the case for limiting evidence and identifies why a purportedly optional disclosure route may conflict with that rule.

## 3. Correction without unrestricted competitive credit

A protected procedure must contain a way to challenge its own errors. Otherwise privacy protection could preserve a defective assessment and the advantages it gives some applicants.

Suppose the programme initially excludes Ada because it treats an interrupted work history as proof that she cannot sustain a demanding course. Her diary establishes that the interruption resulted from a serious caregiving obligation. The same account shows exceptional perseverance. It both corrects an unjust assessment and supplies favourable comparative evidence.

There are two inadequate responses. Counting everything because the diary contains a correction turns an appeal into an unrestricted route for competitive autobiography. Rejecting everything because it contains a bonus prevents Ada from contesting an injustice. The appropriate question is which decision the correction warrants under the justified allocation rule.

In the threshold-and-lottery programme, the answer can be determinate. If the correction establishes that Ada meets the eligibility threshold, she enters the lottery. Her exceptional perseverance gives her no additional weight because superior perseverance above the threshold gives no eligible applicant additional weight, whatever its evidential source. The programme must acknowledge the correction and give it its full procedural consequence: equal membership among the eligible claimants.

If Ben and Cy previously appeared to be the only eligible applicants, their apparent one-half chances become one-third. That reduction is permissible. Their claim was to an equal share among everyone rightly eligible, subject to the programme's fair correction process. It was not a claim to benefit from Ada's mistaken exclusion. A preliminary estimate of their chances overstated what they could demand once the error was repaired.

This case distinguishes three matters that can otherwise be conflated: the prospect generated by an uncorrected assessment, the prospect owed under a properly corrected assessment, and the additional prospect that unrestricted use of the diary would confer. Only the second supplies the relevant baseline. Correction may change that baseline; comparative praise need not change it further.

The programme should implement this distinction through a public correction standard. An applicant should be able to identify an assessment decision, explain its alleged error or unfairness, and establish the facts necessary for an appropriate remedy. The institution should seek less intrusive evidence when it can do so without making correction unduly difficult. If intimate evidence is the only practical means of establishing the error, an absolute ban may itself be unjust. A suitable route must also be available to similarly situated applicants, with reasonable time to use it.

Corrective credit should be complete, not grudging. If the error wrongly excluded Ada, merely allowing her to submit an appeal while keeping her outside the lottery would fail to repair it. Conversely, giving her the entire fellowship because her appeal is moving would exceed the remedy unless some further claim justified that result.

The distinction concerns the evidence's authorized decision effect, rather than the physical separation of its contents. One passage can establish both that Ada was misjudged and that she is unusually persevering. A selector need not forget the latter fact to recognize that, under this programme's rule, both findings lead to the same eligibility status.

Comparative merit schemes present a harder case. Suppose applicants receive perseverance scores and Ada's diary shows that the usual test systematically understates her ability. A fair reassessment might place her above Ben, not merely remove a penalty. If so, limiting correction to restoring their former tie would preserve another error. A valid correction can confer a large competitive advantage.

What decides that case is the substantive account of fair assessment: which capacity the institution may compare, which distortions it must remedy, and which alternative evidence fairly establishes performance on that dimension. The amount of benefit does not distinguish correction from a bonus. Nor does the applicant's description of the submission as an appeal.

Sometimes those standards will not decide the case cleanly. The evidence may be indivisible, and the institution may lack a defensible way to distinguish accurate reassessment from an additional evidential opportunity unavailable to others. In that situation this argument supplies no numerical correction formula. The institution may need a common reassessment, a suitable alternative task, or a revision of its assessment procedure. If none is feasible, the conflict remains substantive: exclusion risks entrenching error, while unrestricted inclusion risks unequal evidential treatment. Calling either outcome privacy protection does not resolve it.

This difficulty limits the generality of the proposal. Its clearest application is a threshold-and-lottery scheme, where corrective evidence can determine membership without creating an extra scale of comparative merit. In more complex schemes, a protected chance can be specified only as far as the underlying account of fair correction can be defended.

## 4. What a disclosure advantage costs

After eligibility and timely corrections have been settled, the allocation constraint is simple. Fix one place and the set of eligible applicants. Let each applicant's justified baseline chance be bᵢ, with the chances summing to one. Let pᵢ be the chance assigned after optional disclosures seeking additional competitive credit. Protection of each silent applicant's share requires pᵢ ≥ bᵢ for that applicant.

If only Ada discloses, preserving everyone else's baseline leaves no capacity to increase hers. With several disclosers, let S contain those who disclose and N those who remain silent. Then:

Σᵢ∈S(pᵢ − bᵢ) = −Σᵢ∈N(pᵢ − bᵢ).

If no silent applicant loses, the disclosers cannot gain in aggregate. Some may gain at other disclosers' expense. But permission to disclose is not automatically permission to surrender a lottery share. Even explicit acceptance of such a loss would be insufficient if the institutional purpose made those shares nontransferable.

The result assumes fixed capacity, a fixed eligible group, and a baseline that already incorporates warranted correction. Applying it to Ben's preliminary one-half chance before Ada's successful appeal would misidentify what was protected. The equation does not decide which changes count as corrections, whether claims are equal, or whether a lottery is justified. It exposes an incompatibility once those issues have been settled.

A related limit affects voluntary disclosure contests. Suppose every participant can withdraw a report and recover the baseline, everyone values only their own selection probability, and capacity is exhausted by the baseline shares. In any outcome where no participant would benefit from withdrawing, everyone must receive at least the baseline. Since the shares sum to the whole opportunity, everyone must receive exactly the baseline. A contest cannot provide a stable probability gain under all those assumptions. Someone must accept a loss, value something else, or lack the right to recover the original share.

These observations identify possible institutional responses without endorsing them all. Additional capacity may permit benefits that preserve existing claims. A justified revision of the allocation principle may change what is owed. Where transfers are permissible, a properly authorized surrender may release a share. Each route needs its own justification. Ada's consent to access is not a substitute.

Nor does every scarce allocation need to protect the whole baseline. Partial guarantees are coherent. What matters is whether the institution has justified the reserved portion and accurately described the promise. A floor of one-sixth cannot honestly be presented as preserving the equal one-third share.

## 5. Merit, agency, and institutional change

The strongest objection is that the diary shows Ada to be the best candidate. Why ignore information that would improve selection?

In the defended case, selecting the strongest candidate is not the complete institutional objective. The programme gives qualified people equal claims to an opportunity for development. Evidence of greater perseverance does not automatically establish a greater claim to that opportunity. This is a substantive distributive commitment, open to criticism; it is not a conclusion derived from privacy alone.

The objection gains force where comparative excellence is essential to the institution's legitimate purpose. Evidence may also reveal that a supposed eligibility threshold misses a qualification necessary for others' safety. A lottery cannot be insulated against every discovery by calling its shares protected. Such discoveries may warrant reassessment or policy revision. The institution must explain why the existing rule fails and what a fair replacement requires. It cannot settle that question by pointing to the beneficiary's willingness to disclose.

A welfare argument may support such revision. The available summary of Daughety and Reinganum describes how the evaluation of waivable privacy can depend on observability and enforcement costs. Those considerations concern the merits of alternative arrangements. They do not make one person's permission sufficient authority to alter another's claim. [Daughety and Reinganum 2008, abstract and introduction](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2008/si2008/LE/daughety.pdf).

Another objection appeals to Ada's agency. She wants to tell her story, and declining to credit it might seem to impose privacy she does not want.

The objection has particular force when her story challenges an unjust institutional judgment. The correction procedure addresses that case. Beyond it, freedom to disclose does not entail a right to every desired institutional response. Ada may publish the diary or discuss it with an adviser. The programme can recognize her achievement without treating it as a claim to a larger lottery share. Admission priority is one form of recognition, governed by the rights and purposes of the selection process.

Selectors also need not pretend that the diary teaches them nothing. Believing that Ada is exceptionally persevering is compatible with recognizing an obligation to run the lottery. The constraint governs institutional action. In practice, avoiding unnecessary access or separating appeals assessment from final allocation may make compliance easier. Those arrangements require care: a separation that prevents the selection process from implementing a valid correction would defeat its purpose.

Finally, a protected procedure does not give each applicant an unrestricted veto over reform. Institutions may revise their practices for sufficient reasons, using fair procedures and giving due weight to existing commitments. A sudden change during an open competition raises different concerns from a justified policy for future cohorts. The argument blocks a shortcut across those questions: bilateral consent does not itself justify a change affecting other claimants.

## 6. The contribution and its limits

The defended claim is narrower than a general right to preserve one's chances whenever someone else discloses private information. No such right follows from privacy. Competitive loss alone does not establish a wrong, and a usable application route need not include an entitlement to its original success probability.

The contribution is an account of when a stronger protection has determinate content. A justified allocation rule establishes the claimants, the capacity, and the shares. A justified evidential boundary limits how the institution may revise its assessment. A fair correction process determines which apparent shares were never fully owed. Together, these identify a prospect that consent to access cannot by itself displace.

This account explains two outcomes that a blanket rule against disadvantage would confuse. Ada's successful challenge to wrongful exclusion may permissibly reduce Ben's chance. Ada's additional evidence of excellence after fair eligibility assessment may not increase her lottery weight. The difference lies in what the governing procedure owes each applicant, not in whether the diary is favourable or whether somebody loses.

It also identifies a wrong that does not require privacy unraveling. A single, freely offered disclosure can lead an institution to spend a chance it owes someone else, even when silence remains wholly uninformative. No claim of priority over the cited literature is needed for this distinction to be useful. It directs attention from the validity of the discloser's consent to the scope of the institution's authority.

Exact probabilities will often be unavailable in discretionary selection. Where neither the allocation principle nor its correction standard identifies a defensible baseline, the present argument cannot manufacture one. Its precision in lotteries should not be mistaken for a general method of measuring opportunity.

An honest assurance must therefore say what silence preserves. Freedom from compelled access, freedom from adverse inference, a partial chance floor, and an equal lottery share are different protections. An institution may defend different ones in different settings. Once it owes the last, however, a voluntary disclosure does not supply the missing justification for taking it away.
