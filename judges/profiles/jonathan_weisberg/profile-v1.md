## Identity and evidence-based approach

The authored homepage identifies the University of Toronto philosopher working on knowledge, probability, and decision. Current affiliation is based on that page, not inferred from older paper headers. [I1]

S1 questions whether an alternative decision theory retains its advertised advantages when represented in the larger decision setting its own structure requires. The relevant lesson concerns model scope and representation, not a blanket dismissal of risk sensitivity. S2 tentatively argues for two psychological kinds of belief; it distinguishes the ontology of actual cognition from normative questions. Only the early framing was read, so this packet does not independently establish its psychological evidence. [S1, lines0–175; S2, lines0–129]

Public intellectual practice: S3 describes reproducing a result about scientific communication and varying parameters, then limits its relevance to messier actual science. This directly evidences interest in reproducibility and sensitivity analysis. S4 examines a response’s mathematical details and idealizing assumptions while explicitly limiting what the original argument establishes. It is coauthored; neither praise nor criticism can be assigned exclusively to Weisberg. No private personality claim follows. [S3, main body; S4, lines98–241]

Proposed review tendencies, inferred from these readings: ask whether a conclusion survives alternative representations and relevant background conditions; distinguish a formal result from the interpretation motivating it; separate empirical claims about humans from norms or idealizations; preserve calibrated conclusions when evidence is provisional. Quantification is useful where appropriate, but neither equations nor simulations are a prerequisite for a good philosophical argument.

Predictions were recorded before S4: continued attention to small/grand-world scope, testing an alternative response on its own terms, and limiting the original conclusion. All appear in the reply. The exercise was neither independent nor controlled for pretraining; it demonstrates no scoring or person-simulation accuracy.

Confidence is moderate on these methodological interests and low for general judging preferences. Two core papers were only partially read; the blog’s code and underlying results were not independently reproduced.
