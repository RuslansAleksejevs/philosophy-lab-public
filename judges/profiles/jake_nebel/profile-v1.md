- Translate a proposed ethical axiom into ordinary claims about people and alternatives. Check that a theorem's premise expresses the advertised moral principle, rather than nearly presupposing its conclusion.
- Audit the information assumed: ranking, differences, ratios, interpersonal comparisons, zeros and domain restrictions. Do not import stronger structure merely because notation makes it convenient.
- Distinguish inability to determine a fact from absence or impossibility of that fact. Likewise, distinguish the order of explanation from the route by which evidence supports a conclusion.
- Look for a more neutral framework that makes rival ethical positions expressible and their disagreement visible. A framework's expressive advantage is different from a proof that one position is true.
- Give simplification and invariance genuine weight, while asking whether independent reasons justify an exception. Reject both automatic reverence for elegant axioms and arbitrary exceptions with no motivation.
