## Textual findings

Hooke’s text supports the draft’s main reconstruction. He records changing illumination and specimen position; observes rows of hemispheres and reflections; estimates nearly 14,000 by counting rows and extrapolating; dissects the cluster; and gives a structural and comparative case for an organ of sight. His obstacle-avoidance removal experiment concerns crustaceans, not the fly. The draft states that limitation correctly.

Hooke’s optical explanation is explicitly conjectural in important places. He calls each pearl or hemisphere a “perfect eye,” reasons that its surface refracts parallel rays, places a retina “in all probability,” and concludes that only near-axis parts would be distinctly sensed. He then says many pictures may be made while only one or a few are distinct, and that the fly is not thereby given a greater faculty of seeing. The draft accurately reports this local-image / distinct-vision distinction.

The manuscript underreports two nearby claims that matter to its description of whole-animal capacity. Before the optical analysis Hooke says the facets are directed toward every visible quarter, so the fly has “an eye every way” and is “circumspect” (Observation XXXIX, web lines 122–23). After denying a greater faculty of seeing, he says the fly can move its head a little and that these animals can direct an optical line toward an object, giving them a visual faculty “as compleat as any Animal that can move its eyes” (lines 149–51). The paper mentions directionality, but it does not reconcile these claims with its broad gloss that Hooke denies greater whole-animal vision. At minimum it should distinguish angular coverage and the ability to direct a view from distinctness, acuity, attention, and total visual capacity. This is a material qualification, not a correction to the paper’s core distinction.

Cavendish’s “Of the Eyes of Flies” passage is accurately represented in the draft on the supplied excerpt: she questions the count and the microscope, proposes that the globules may be reflective transparent knobs, invokes nature’s non-uselessness, and uses the spider and cold-weather examples. Her argument is not merely an empirical objection about an integrated faculty: she treats the count as a challenge to the usefulness of the alleged parts. The draft recognizes the teleological point, but its reply should address it directly instead of making lack of a demonstrated mechanism carry weight. The better reply is that her behavioral observations do not discriminate between no local optical function and functioning facets whose contribution is limited by orientation, resolution, attention, or integration.

## Literature, rival, and bounded novelty

Clucas’s selected pages 20–23 directly discuss the fly passage, Hooke’s own report that the fly’s surface appears differently under changed light and posture, and Cavendish’s reuse of that variability to challenge the eye interpretation. Pages 12–16 place the dispute within Cavendish’s broader opposition to microscopy and her preference for regular perception and reason, while also noting that she does not simply reject every possible contribution from experiment. Purcell’s selected pages 56–57 summarize the same fly-eye challenge and Cavendish’s spider example. These selections support the draft’s claim that broad anti-microscope framing is already familiar. They do not establish that the three-part taxonomy is unprecedented; no exhaustive literature search was performed.

The strongest rival reading is also close to the primary text: Hooke himself already distinguishes local image formation, distinct perception, and attentive selection, while still claiming broad directional coverage and a complete visual faculty. Cavendish’s challenge is broader than a mistaken inference from “many parts” to “more total sight”; she asks whether the alleged parts are really eyes and whether they serve a purpose. The essay’s contribution is therefore best presented as a careful comparison of which evidence supports which claim, with a limited clarification of the tension between Hooke’s count, field coverage, and distinct vision. Its general lesson that parts, functions, and capacities need separate warrants is sensible but familiar and should not be sold as a large new principle.

## Assessment for the critic stage

The source-sensitive central inference is valid as a reconstruction: Hooke offers defeasible reasons for local optical function and expressly limits what the large number implies for distinct seeing. The key repair is to make “visual capacity” less coarse. Explain Hooke’s “circumspect” coverage and “complete” faculty alongside his “not ... greater” claim, and explain why the claims can concern different dimensions of vision. Then state more precisely how Cavendish’s usefulness objection bears on local function and why her examples do not settle that question. Keep the novelty claim narrow and acknowledge that the essential distinctions are prompted by Hooke’s own text.

No substantive claim about modern insect vision was checked. The essay’s disclaimer that it is not testing the fly against contemporary entomology is appropriate.
