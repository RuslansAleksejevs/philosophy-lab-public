# Independent critique of D2: “Revocable Consent and the Creation of Duties”

## Recommendation

Revise the central justification before treating D2 as a settled three-cycle branch. The paper identifies a real and morally important distinction: Rowan may retain the power to end permission while acquiring a separate duty to protect the residents. The account of care exploitation is handled carefully, and the cited primary passages I could check do not reveal a source error. The unresolved issue is the bridge from Ellis’s discretionary sale of the reserve to a duty owed specifically to Rowan to preserve her exit. D2 names this as an additional premise, but mainly restates it as a presumptive allocation of precaution costs. That premise needs a fuller defense and a sharper statement of what counts as a burden created by Ellis.

## Reconstruction

D2’s central case gives Rowan permission to lend a pump on one day’s notice. Ellis sells an available reserve for a useful but nonurgent project, although keeping it would be affordable. If Rowan withdraws the loan, a replacement will take two weeks, and residents face a serious shortage unless Rowan keeps lending. D2 distinguishes three questions: whether Rowan can end the original permission, whether she may permissibly make Ellis stop immediately, and whether Ellis must stop all things considered. The first can be true while an independent emergency duty supports temporary continued use.

The proposed rule is prospective: a recipient who knows that permission leaves the giver control over continued use should take reasonable precautions against its own discretionary plans making cessation subject to a substantial new moral burden, absent adequate authorization or independent justification. This does not make consent sovereign over every other duty. Nor does it imply that the giver may ignore the people who would be harmed. The proposed wrong is the recipient’s avoidable reorganization of dependence, while the present duty to help and the later repair obligation remain real.

The paper’s contribution is expressly limited. It does not claim that Ellis’s lack of manipulative intent or Rowan’s assent distinguishes the case from care exploitation. It says McKittrick-Sweitzer’s account may apply if its care-based call, disproportionate benefit, likely acceptance, and actual answer conditions are met. D2 instead asks whether a recipient may use discretion over an arrangement to make refusal trigger a new obligation for the person who granted limited permission.

## Source fidelity and scope

The selected McKittrick-Sweitzer passages support D2’s description of care exploitation. The 2023 account requires a call to aid that is not open to refusal, a call grounded in the recipient’s care for the subject, anticipated disproportionate benefit, likely acceptance because of care, and an actual answer. A call can be passive, and the presumption can result from inattention or wishful thinking; willing assent does not by itself prevent exploitation. The 2024 passages repeat the conditions, explain the role of assent, and state that a passive presumption may be adopted unintentionally. D2 does not falsely claim that the pump case falls outside this account: it leaves the needed facts unsettled and allows the accounts to overlap.

Goodin’s publisher abstract supports the limited point D2 makes about reasonable detrimental reliance: it can count against revocation and in favor of compensation, while uncompensatable reliance can count in favor of treating consent as irrevocable. The full chapter was unavailable in the saved publisher representation, and D2 says it relies only on the abstract. I did not directly re-check D2’s uses of Dougherty, Healey, Keller, Liberto, or Rouméas because their text is not in this critique’s selected source packet. I therefore make no independent finding about those citations. The source packet is bounded evidence, not a novelty survey.

One saved 2023 opening excerpt ends mid-sentence at the start of the author’s discussion of the Kantian account. I read the complete saved excerpt, but it is itself incomplete. The conditions and outcome excerpts cover the decisive passages D2 invokes about passive calls, care-based motivation, disproportionate benefit, and assent. No new source retrieval was needed.

## Strongest objection: why does Ellis owe Rowan this precaution?

D2 is right that the extra premise is not a definition of consent: “the recipient bears a burden of justification when its discretionary planning shifts substantial costs of continuity” to the giver (D2 §3). But the paper has not yet shown why that burden is a duty owed to Rowan, rather than a general reason to plan responsibly or a duty Ellis owes to the residents. The phrase “the cost belongs presumptively to the undertaking Ellis organizes” states the proposed allocation; it does not establish it.

The distinction matters because Rowan’s duty to help is independently grounded in the residents’ serious need. D2 says Ellis’s sale makes her refusal morally costly, but it should show exactly which part of that cost is newly attributable to Ellis. The relevant counterfactual seems to be this: with the reserve available, Rowan’s one-day notice can be honored without a shortage; after Ellis sells it, the same notice leaves roughly thirteen additional days of support morally required. D2 should state this marginal change explicitly and explain why Ellis’s voluntary, foreseeable choice to create it gives Rowan a claim against him. Without that bridge, the case risks moving from “Ellis has made Rowan’s exit harder” to “Ellis wronged Rowan” without defending the intervening normative step.

The stranger comparison in §3 sharpens the problem. D2 says Ellis would owe much the same explanation if Ellis had been supplying the settlement with Ellis’s own pump and then made a nearby stranger’s pump the necessary backup. That is a useful test, but it suggests that the underlying duty is general: do not create avoidable rescue burdens for others. The permission relation then identifies the notice period and the dependence the giver accepted; it does not ground the precaution duty itself. D2 should either defend that general rule directly and present revocable permission as an important application, or explain what additional duty arises because Rowan granted permission. This also bears on the claimed contribution: the more the rule is general responsibility for avoidably creating emergencies, the less the paper establishes a distinct consent-based principle.

The example already contains facts that make Ellis’s choice look wrongful: a serious risk to residents, a modest cost of preserving the reserve, and a nonurgent benefit from selling it. But the principle ranges further than this case. “Substantial new moral burden,” “fairly understood reliance,” and “adequate authorization” remain open-textured. The limits in §4 help, but the paper should give a clearer decision rule for the baseline: what must a recipient disclose or preserve when permission is expressly revocable, and when may ordinary reliance justify letting the recipient dispense with a precaution? The one-day term strongly supports Rowan’s interest in a short transition; it does not by itself say who must finance every contingency.

## Repairs that would answer the objection

1. Make the pump counterfactual exact. State that the reserve would cover the full transition after one day’s notice, so selling it adds a specified period of continued use and a serious risk that was not inherent in the agreed arrangement.
2. Defend the missing bridge from Ellis’s choice to Rowan’s claim. For example, explain whether the duty rests on voluntarily controlling a foreseeable risk, on causing an avoidable need that will predictably conscript Rowan’s help, or on the terms of the permission. Then explain why the chosen ground creates an obligation to Rowan as well as a reason to protect residents.
3. Use the stranger case to state the scope precisely. If the principle is general, acknowledge that consent marks the baseline of accepted reliance rather than serving as the source of the duty. If a special consent-based duty remains, identify the extra fact that the stranger case lacks.
4. Test the principle with nearby cases: a reserve that is ruinously expensive, an unexpected emergency that makes selling it reasonable, a replacement available within the notice period, and informed authorization by Rowan before dependence grows. These cases can show that “reasonable precaution” and “independent justification” do real work.

These repairs need not deny the residents’ claim or make a later extension equivalent to renewed consent. The paper’s distinction between emergency assistance and endorsement is valuable. It should be retained while the prior responsibility for creating the predicament is made more explicit.

## Severity, novelty, and final assessment

I found no fatal source or inference defect in the paper’s account of consent’s effects or care exploitation. The principal problem concerns the defense of its own normative premise, not a misrepresentation of the adjacent literature. It threatens the central contribution because the precaution duty is the paper’s main claim, but D2 makes a plausible start: the notice term, foreseeable dependence, inexpensive reserve, and nonurgent sale all support a pro tanto reason for Ellis to preserve alternatives. I recommend a targeted revision, not setting the draft aside.

I add the following critical clarification: the argument needs to distinguish a duty to rescue that Rowan would owe because residents are in danger from the *incremental* duty allegedly produced by Ellis’s choice. The difference between the one-day notice period and the two-week replacement period is the clearest candidate for that increment. Establishing why Ellis is responsible for imposing that additional period would make the paper’s case substantially stronger.

The paper does not claim exhaustive novelty, and my source packet cannot establish it. Given the acknowledged overlap with care exploitation, moral blackmail, reasonable-reliance accounts, and fair-exit theory, the contribution should remain framed as a bounded clarification or application until broader literature comparison is complete.
