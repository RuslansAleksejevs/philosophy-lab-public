## The argument actually defended

The central claim is a defeasible duty of precaution owed by a recipient of revocable permission to its giver. The duty protects the practical value of a retained decision by constraining discretionary arrangements that foreseeably add substantial moral obstacles to ending the use. The claim is not that revocation settles everything, that all reliance is illegitimate, or that the resulting emergency duty disappears when its creation was wrongful.

The pump case separates two assessments. At the later time, innocent residents can have a claim to continued water supply. At the earlier time, Ellis controls an affordable means of avoiding reliance on Rowan's further support. The manuscript argues that, absent authorization or sufficient justification, Ellis may not redirect that means and unilaterally transfer continuity costs to Rowan. Revocability identifies a limited undertaking; control over prevention and the significance of the retained option explain the precautionary burden. The conclusion depends on this substantive allocation premise, not simply on the meaning of consent.

That structure is coherent. The distinction between a normative power and permission to exercise it blocks a misleading contradiction. The storm comparison separates responsibility for creating the predicament from duties within it. The version where residents are certain to receive water identifies a complaint about allocating responsibility even without an increased probability of their injury. And the stranger comparison appropriately limits the claim: the wrong need not be unique to a consensual relationship.

## The strongest objection, and how far the answer succeeds

The strongest objection is **reciprocal discretion**, rather than a bare appeal to reliance. Rowan owns the loaned pump; Ellis owns the reserve. Neither has expressly promised to keep a resource available. Why should preserving Rowan's discretion require constraining Ellis's? Calling the prior arrangement an allocation of responsibility can conceal the very default that needs defending. The fact that Rowan withheld a commitment does not entail that Ellis undertook a backup obligation. Nor does Ellis's ability to prevent the emergency by itself establish who should pay: sometimes the better prevention arrangement would burden the giver or share the cost.

The text recognizes the first difficulty explicitly in section 3, including the paragraph following the two proposed loan terms. Its affirmative answer is that Ellis voluntarily organizes a project around another person's limited permission, controls the additional dependence, and can preserve the authorized use at modest cost. The relevant action is not mere possession of a reserve but a discretionary change to the undertaking. Section 4 then allows different communicated terms, legitimate reliance, the cost and likelihood of prevention, and independent justification to alter the result.

For the stipulated case, this is a defensible answer. Ellis is not asked to insure Rowan against every circumstance or preserve an idle pump forever. Ellis can negotiate another arrangement or make a safe transition. The decision to spend the reserve on a useful but nonurgent project does not establish an entitlement to finance that benefit through Rowan's newly constrained options. This gives a reason for the default rather than merely repeating that consent is revocable.

The answer is still a disputed moral premise, not a deductive result binding every theory of fair cooperation. The paper properly presents it as presumptive and limited. Its present case does not settle arrangements in which both parties have comparably important interests, precautions are costly, or the permission predictably invites indispensable reliance. Those limits are already substantial parts of sections 4–5. I do not count their existence as a defect requiring a complete theory of responsibility allocation.

## Required scholarly repair: the close care-exploitation account

The new primary check located Lavender McKittrick-Sweitzer's “Care Exploitation: Taking Advantage of One's Caring about Another” (2023). Section 2 permits passive calls to aid and presumptions arising through inattention or wishful thinking; section 2.4 also identifies a wrong in attempted exploitation when the call is not answered. Thus neither the absence of an overt request nor the absence of calculated manipulation by itself marks new territory. Relevant passages are printed pp. 13–15 and 19, saved as exact publisher-PDF text in the source packet. [Primary paper](https://ojs.lib.uwo.ca/index.php/fpq/article/download/15370/12934).

The same author's “Preventing the Exploitation of Activists' Care” (2024), section 2 and note 5, expressly allows an unintentionally adopted presumption and acknowledges genuine consent by the person whose care is exploited. Its project includes prevention and protection of agency, not only retrospective classification. Only the specified excerpts, rather than the complete paper, were read. [Primary paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-024-10435-2).

This does **not** establish that D1 duplicates either paper. D1 already modestly describes its contribution as a connection and application, not the discovery of an entirely new kind of wrong. Nevertheless, its section “Beyond a manipulative intention,” its emphasis on no request or refusal being necessary, and its closing defense of the practical value of choice have a close, uncited interlocutor. This is a material literature-engagement gap because these features help explain why the argument matters. It is not evidence of plagiarism, invalidity, or general lack of originality.

**R1.** Add a concise, source-accurate comparison with the 2023 account, using the 2024 passage if helpful. Acknowledge the overlap concerning nonstrategic or passive reliance on another's care. Then either explain what the proposed precaution duty adds or present it expressly as a further application within that neighborhood. Do not claim that merely recognizing a wrong before refusal, or allowing valid consent, distinguishes this paper from all care-exploitation accounts. A paragraph and an appropriate reference may be enough; no mandatory expansion into the full care-ethics literature follows from this review. If the author considers the comparison unnecessary, the response should explain that judgment against these actual passages.

## A possible distinction, offered by this critic

**C2-N1 — critic-originated comparative proposal.** One promising way to position the contribution is to distinguish a change in a person's actual obligations from another's presumption about what that person will willingly do. The pump argument concerns a controllable change in the conditions that determine whether Rowan may stop. A disrespectful presumption about generosity could exist while those conditions remain unchanged; conversely, an avoidable new rescue burden can matter even before its creator tests or successfully exploits the giver's motivation. This would place the emphasis on prospective organization of responsibilities and the protection of a particular reserved option.

This is a suggested comparison developed in this review, not a conclusion attributed to the manuscript or the cited authors. It is not proof that the literatures are extensionally separate: an account of care exploitation can address many of the same cases, and a change in actual duties may itself be part of an exploitative arrangement. The author may accept, qualify, replace, or reject the proposal. If used to develop the essay, preserve its critic origin in the process record. The safe packet for a later independent critic must contain the primary evidence, not this proposed interpretation.

## Source accuracy and the strongest cited rival

- **Dougherty:** the passages checked support separating present dissent's priority over past consent from its relation to other moral reasons, and combining consent with a promise not to revoke. D1 does not conflate the two priorities. Coverage: extracted portions of pp. 27, 32–34, 38–39. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-013-0230-7).
- **Healey:** pp. 503–504 support the distinction between the effect on directed duties and overall permissibility, including several independent duties owed to one person. D1 signals that applying this across time is its own application. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11572-018-9484-7).
- **Keller:** the checked moral-blackmail and fiduciary examples support both the reality of an obligation produced through wrongful conduct and vulnerability generated by relationships. D1 credits the general phenomenon rather than presenting that paradox as its discovery. [Article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/japp.12234).
- **Liberto:** the authority-account discussion supports effective promissory release alongside a wrong of exploitation. D1 retains the qualification to that account and does not claim all theories of promising have the same implication. Coverage: portions of pp. 150–151 and 160–163. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1111/sjp.12191).
- **Rouméas:** the checked sections distinguish feasible exit from fair cost allocation and do not assign all costs to the remaining party. D1 represents these limits accurately. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470594X231156939).
- **Goodin:** a fresh publisher lookup confirmed the abstract's claims about reasonable detrimental reliance, compensation, and a pro tanto reason for irrevocability where costs are uncompensatable. D1 openly restricts its claim to retained control and does not purport to answer the full chapter. The complete chapter remains unavailable. [Publisher abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/55910/chapter/439317000).

Goodin supplies the strongest *cited* challenge to the starting scope. The manuscript's answer is appropriately conditional: it does not establish universal revocability; it asks what follows where that control remains. Within the pump case, reasonable reliance must be argued for rather than inferred from the mere fact of uncompensatable consequences. The disclosed notice period and existing reserve give grounds for denying the disputed reliance. Because the full chapter was not read, I cannot certify that the text addresses Goodin's strongest developed argument or that he would reject this distinction.

## Optional improvements, not acceptance conditions

1. The phrase “allocation already established” in section 3 can briefly specify that the established fact is the absence of a commitment to continued support, while the allocation of prevention costs remains an argued default. The surrounding prose already makes this distinction; this is a clarity improvement.

The existing express exclusion of insignificant restrictions is adequate to prevent the claim that every trivial extension is a violation.
