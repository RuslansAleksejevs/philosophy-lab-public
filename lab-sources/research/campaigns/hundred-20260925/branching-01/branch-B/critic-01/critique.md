# Independent review of D0: Revocable Consent and the Creation of Duties

Verdict: **accept the limited thesis with explicit limitations**. I found no decisive invalid inference or material misrepresentation in the passages checked. The central claim is defensible as a presumptive duty against a foreseeable, avoidable transfer of responsibility. Its contribution is a useful application and connection, not yet a demonstration that revocable consent generates a distinctive moral duty unavailable from more general principles. The most valuable revision would clarify this explanatory status. I do not recommend setting the essay aside, and I do not manufacture a mandatory substantive repair.

## The argument at its strongest

The manuscript does not infer a liberty to cease helping from the mere power to revoke consent. It expressly separates those positions. Its argument is instead:

1. Rowan authorizes a continuing use while withholding a commitment to maintain it. The authorized arrangement already allows some ordinary dependence and transition costs.
2. Ellis controls an additional discretionary choice: whether to preserve an affordable existing substitute. Keeping it permits the same authorized activity while preserving the practical value of Rowan's retained control.
3. Selling it predictably shifts the responsibility for continuity onto Rowan through innocent residents' needs. Ellis thereby obtains an extension without first obtaining Rowan's commitment.
4. A recipient normally needs justification to make this additional shift. Taking permission for the current activity does not itself supply that justification. Reasonable precautions, authorization, and exceptional needs determine the scope of the presumption.
5. Wrongfully creating the predicament does not erase the residents' claims once it exists. Rowan may now owe help while Ellis owes Rowan prevention or repair.

The decisive premise is 4. It is a substantive fairness and control premise, not an analytic truth about consent. Section 3 recognizes this and supplies reasons for it: who chose the undertaking, who controls the precaution, what responsibility was undertaken, and what value was retained by giving limited permission. With that premise, the conclusion follows. Without it, a valid revocation and a new rescue duty are compatible with Ellis having acted permissibly.

## Strongest objection: has consent supplied a new reason, or specified an ordinary one?

A serious opponent can accept every conceptual distinction and still ask why Ellis must pay to preserve Rowan's exit. The fact that Rowan made no commitment does not by itself settle which new duties Ellis may permissibly cause Rowan to acquire. The manuscript itself recognizes that ordinary human interactions generate unchosen duties. Moreover, preserving Ellis's reserve restricts Ellis's use of Ellis's own resource. Describing the sale as a change in the existing allocation is not yet decisive: an allocation can justifiably change, and deciding which previous position should be protected is the moral issue.

The manuscript has a real response. Ellis voluntarily organizes a project around another's limited permission; a modest precaution allows that project to proceed on its established terms; and the foregone alternative is merely another useful, nonurgent project. Rowan did not invite the additional dependence. This combination makes a presumption against the sale plausible. The principle also gives notice periods, costs, existing dependence and competing needs substantial work. It is therefore unfair to describe it as simply assuming that owners control every consequence of withdrawal.

The remaining pressure concerns explanatory significance. The same considerations may support a general duty not to arrange avoidable rescue burdens for another person, independently of a prior grant of consent. If so, the essay identifies an important application of that duty rather than a new consent-specific ground of duty. That is compatible with the introduction's deliberately limited contribution claim. It restricts what the argument establishes, rather than refuting it.

Here is a diagnostic comparison introduced by this critic. Ellis initially supplies the settlement with Ellis's own pump. Rowan, a nearby stranger to this undertaking, owns a second available pump. Ellis sells the working pump for a useful but nonurgent purpose, knowing that a resulting shortage would leave Rowan with the same temporary duty to rescue and knowing that continued use of Ellis's pump was affordable. There has been no revocable loan. Nevertheless, Ellis seems to owe Rowan much the same explanation for deliberately organizing this dependence. This is not a counterexample to D0's principle, which is sufficient rather than necessary. It is a test of what consent adds to the explanation.

One defensible answer is that the general duty supplies part of the reason, while the particular permission identifies the protected option and a baseline for judging the additional burden. Another is that limited permission gives a further relational reason against appropriating the giver's decision. Either answer would improve the essay. The second needs an argument showing what difference remains when general rescue-cost considerations are held fixed. The first should be stated without claiming a uniquely consent-based duty. A short paragraph is enough; an entire general theory is not required.

## A clarification about the size of the burden

The opening assumes that extending the loan imposes little burden on Rowan, while the principle concerns a substantial new moral burden. This need not be inconsistent. A modest cost of continuing can coexist with a significant restriction of a protected option. But two measures are in play: the cost of doing what the new duty requires, and the loss of authority or freedom to choose otherwise. Their distinction should be explicit, especially because section 4 makes reasonableness depend on seriousness and likelihood.

The essay's claim cannot plausibly be that any small new duty is a substantial burden just because violating it would be wrong. It already avoids that claim through its discussion of ordinary transitions and reasonable precautions. It would help to say directly what makes this particular restriction material: the period, the use controlled, the foreseeable reasons for wanting cessation, and the avoidability of placing that option under another person's discretionary planning. This is a clarification of a defensible view, not a discovered contradiction or a demand for a numerical formula.

## Objections that do not defeat the essay

- **Revocation remains valid.** D0 explicitly grants this. The claimed wrong concerns the circumstances of obtaining cessation, not invalidation of a normative power.
- **The residents still need water.** The essay preserves the later rescue duty. Permitting Rowan to punish them is not its proposed remedy.
- **Ellis had no manipulative purpose.** The duty is formulated as a precaution against a foreseeable organizational effect. The author must defend that duty, but negligence or knowing disregard can be wrongful without an intention to constrain Rowan.
- **Consent invites reliance.** D0 allows ordinary transitions, assurances, compensation and fixed terms. Its distinction between reasonable prediction and an entitlement to shift costs is relevant. The lack of a complete theory of reliance is a scope limit, not by itself a refutation of this carefully stipulated case.
- **An expensive reserve would be wasteful.** The manuscript expressly makes the precaution's cost and competing purposes relevant. Changing a cheap existing reserve into an impossibly costly duplicate system changes its case.

## Source accuracy and closest arguments

No material source error was found in the passages examined. Healey 2019, pp.503–504, supports the separation of directed duties and overall permission. Dougherty 2014, pp.27 and 39, supports the distinctions concerning other moral reasons and promises against revocation. Neither supplies D0's disputed precaution premise; D0 does not claim otherwise. Liberto 2016, pp.150 and 160–163, supports the comparison with effective release that can still wrong its giver; the manuscript distinguishes its own case from release-seeking.

Keller is the nearest account of imposing obligations through dependence. The checked publisher text, lines 181–193 and 203–224, supports D0's description. The additional family-paper lookup found only limited indexed passages; it does not establish an unacknowledged anticipation. Roumeas 2023, saved publisher lines 73–97 and 108–123, supports distinguishing affordable exit from fairly allocated costs. These are substantial antecedents, so the modest description of the contribution should stay modest.

There is one useful source clarification. The saved Goodin abstract also identifies uncompensatable reliance as a pro tanto reason for treating consent as irrevocable. D0 accurately attributes the reliance-and-compensation part and explicitly discloses abstract-only access, so its citation is not false. Still, acknowledging this stronger nearby position would make the disagreement clearer. D0 can answer that its case stipulates retained revocability and challenges whether Ellis's additional reliance was reasonable. It should not attribute a complete theory of that reasonableness to an unread chapter. The new attempt to open it failed.

## Recommended disposition and attribution

Retain the thesis and the distinction between prospective wrong and present duty. Prefer a limited clarification of the general-duty rival, the two senses of burden, and Goodin's stronger abstract claim. These are ordered recommendations, not compulsory replacements of the author's argument. The author may reject them with reasons. No stylistic rewrite, added formal model or longer manuscript is needed to satisfy this review.

The pump-without-prior-consent comparison and its explanatory test originate in this critic session. If the author uses them, the response and private lineage should record this critic contribution. They must not be represented as an argument already present in D0. The two-burden distinction is a critic articulation of a tension in D0, not a claim to have originated every underlying idea about options and welfare.

The review does not certify exhaustive novelty, philosophical truth, contest eligibility, or intellectual independence. It supports a defensible limited contribution under a disputed but argued normative premise. No code or empirical model was present, so no executable validation was applicable.
