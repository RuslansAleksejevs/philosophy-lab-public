# Independent review: Revocable Consent and the Creation of Duties

**Recommendation: specific revision, retaining the central proposal.** The pump case makes a credible case for a prospective duty and does not contain a demonstrated contradiction. The decisive remaining task is to establish what work the revocable-permission relationship does in justifying that duty, and to locate the contribution against a closely related argument newly found during this review. I found no material misrepresentation in the six cited-source claims checked. This is not acceptance of the essay's originality, or a prediction of contest results.

The argument as I understand it

1. Consent's revocation can be effective although independent moral reasons still require continued access or assistance.
2. A recipient can foreseeably produce those reasons by its organizational choices, without intending to manipulate the giver.
3. Where consent is explicitly limited, the recipient has not thereby acquired authority to transfer additional continuity costs to its giver.
4. If a reasonable precaution protects that allocation and no adequate authorization or defeating justification exists, its discretionary removal wrongs the giver.
5. The wrong calls for precaution beforehand and restoration afterwards; it does not cancel the innocent beneficiaries' claims once the emergency exists.

Premises 1–2 and the consistency of 4–5 are well supported. Premise 3, as connected to 4, carries the main normative burden. D0 expressly acknowledges this at lines 45 and 53: it does **not** purport to deduce a protected practical exit simply from a revocation power. The criticism below therefore concerns the positive justification offered, not a confusion the author has already avoided.

What succeeds

The distinction between a normative power, permission to exercise it, and ability to secure cessation is clear and useful. The comparison between deliberate manipulation, foreseeable dependence and storm damage isolates responsibility without denying the later emergency. The no-injury variant at lines 59–61 also correctly prevents a duty to residents from exhausting the possible complaint to Rowan. The reasonable-precaution conditions acknowledge cost, probability, authorized reliance and ordinary safe transitions; they are substantive restrictions, not a demand that all relationships remain costlessly escapable. Section 6 avoids the important mistake of treating later cooperation as retrospective approval.

Required revision 1: engage the closest new argument and identify the remaining contribution

Spafford's chapter 2 formulates a constraint on foreseeable, discretionary, unilateral disadvantage under moral compliance; it includes negligence, alternative-choice baselines, and consent. Its rescue discussion permits a later duty to help while assigning compensation to the person who created the predicament. These are close to the conjunction on which D0 relies. Relevant places are §§2.1–2.2 and §2.5, especially note 25, in [*The Moral Tyranny Constraint*](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009375429.003). The saved evidence is `web-open-06.json` and `web-open-find-07.json`.

This does **not** show that D0 duplicates the chapter. It does show that contrasting the paper only with intentional blackmail and successful release-seeking leaves its literature positioning incomplete. A short comparison must say whether D0 supplies an application, a more limited justification, or an additional control claim. The author need not defend or reject Spafford's broader political theory. The read source does not license that demand.

One possible distinguishing question is whether making a giver no worse off is sufficient while her retained option remains unavailable. D0 appears to answer no at lines 61 and 107. But a contrast stated merely as “welfare versus control” would be too quick: Spafford leaves the currency of advantage open. Establish the claimed remainder, rather than assigning the rival an exclusively monetary or welfare-based metric. This literature repair matters to the central contribution; it is not a request to expand the bibliography for its own sake.

Required revision 2: specify whether revocable permission grounds an additional complaint

The allocation defense at lines 55–57 has force: Ellis organizes the project, knows the loan is limited, controls the cheap precaution, and can seek different terms. But the fact that Rowan made no commitment establishes only that the commitment was not undertaken. It does not itself settle the default duties governing Ellis's independently owned reserve. The author recognizes this and gives an agency-based reason, yet the connection between the general wrong of shifting costs and the specific relation remains underdeveloped.

Consider the following diagnostic variant, introduced by this critic. Ellis initially supplies the settlement entirely with Ellis's own equipment; Rowan has never lent or promised anything. Ellis disposes of the relevant equipment for a useful but nonurgent project, knowing that Rowan is the only nearby owner who can prevent an acute shortage by lending a spare at little cost. A modest available precaution would have avoided this situation. If Rowan now has a rescue duty, Ellis can plausibly have wronged Rowan even though no permission has been received. The same features—foreseeability, discretionary control, a cheap precaution and displaced continuity costs—support that complaint.

This variant does not refute the conditional claim that recipients can owe precautions. A general duty can entail a special case. It tests the explanation of the paper's contribution. If the complaint in the original case is the same general complaint, present the result clearly as a consent-specific application and explain its payoff: valid withdrawal and respectful acknowledgment do not settle the recipient's conduct. If the prior permission grounds an additional or stronger directed duty, explain why, using a matched contrast or an explicit argument. Do not merely repeat that practical control is valuable; that consideration also applies to the untouched nearby owner's plans.

Nor is an additional promise to maintain the reserve needed to answer this objection. Adding such a promise would make the example easier by changing the problem. The useful revision would defend the default in the actual no-reserve-promise case. The current paper already has the materials for a plausible answer; the missing step is making their relational significance explicit.

Required clarification: distinguish the strength of the duty from the cost of compliance

The opening stipulates that continuation imposes little burden on Rowan, while the principle concerns a “substantial new moral burden.” These are compatible, but the criterion is ambiguous. Does “substantial” track the importance of the obligation, the loss of a significant option, or the cost of complying with it? A grave wrong in refusing a nearly costless act is not the same thing as a costly duty. Identify which property matters and apply the distinction to the pump case. The reasonableness factors at line 75 should remain; the requested clarification is not a demand for an algorithm or unrestricted freedom from minor duties.

Tempting objections that do not defeat this draft

- “The later duty is not real because Ellis created it wrongly.” That simply denies the distinction the manuscript and Keller defend; it offers no reason to abandon the residents.
- “There is no manipulation, so there is no wrong.” Foreseeable effects can ground duties of care. Intentional blackmail need not exhaust the relevant wrongs.
- “Permission always authorizes reliance.” D0 distinguishes prediction from justified reliance, allows notices and compensation, and excludes a giver's assurance in its case. The real question is which reliance is justified, not whether all reliance is forbidden.
- “Consent is always revocable, so Rowan may immediately reclaim the pump.” This conflates a power with overall permissibility. Conversely, the existence of an emergency does not retroactively authorize its creation.
- “The argument undermines friendship.” It expressly limits itself to particular retained permissions and accepts other bases for relational duties.
