# Sources and argument scope

## Result of the fresh source check

The central inference survives only in a narrow conditional form: selecting one feasible allocation under independently defensible, claim-sensitive reasons does not logically establish a complete weak-betterness ordering of the recipients' whole lives. The allocation procedure and the relation it asserts must be named separately. The procedure may rank actions, settle a permissible tie, or establish who has a claim to this resource. None of those relations is automatically a judgment that one person's life as a whole is at least as good as another's.

The source check also sharply limits what this can claim. Sen explicitly distinguishes social choice from a society-wide ordering and says that some choice problems do not require completeness, while noting that a choice between two unranked options can still be a problem. Chang argues that a failure of the better/worse/equally-good trichotomy does not establish incomparability: parity is another possible evaluative relation. She also treats duty-relative comparisons as possible. Taurek's proposed equal-chance lottery and Parfit's reply directly contest what equal concern requires when one person faces a choice between claims. Parfit's later priority discussion emphasizes that history and lifetime disadvantage can change the basis for priority. These arguments make any claim of novelty or a universal lottery rule untenable.

## Narrow thesis used for the manuscript

A justified allocation decision need not entail a complete whole-life betterness ordering when all of the following hold: (1) its relevant reasons concern a specified intervention, claim, duty, right, or fair procedure; (2) those reasons are defended independently, rather than inferred from a concealed total evaluation of each recipient; (3) the stipulated context includes the histories and distributive facts that the adopted reasons treat as relevant; and (4) the local priority relation is coherent. A unique maximal option can be selected if the relation has one. Where several options remain tied or unranked, an independently defended tie procedure can select one; absent such a defense, the decision remains unsettled.

This is a claim about logical entailment and a diagnostic for arguments about allocation. It is not a general allocation rule, a proof that incomplete comparisons are superior, a claim that a lottery is always fair, or a claim that no complete-life ordering exists. It is compatible with a complete-life ordering supported by other premises. It is also compatible with parity, incomparability, uncertainty, or disagreement about lives. None is inferred from the bare allocation.

## Closest arguments and the proposed diagnostic

The strongest nearby result is Sen's distinction between orderings and choice rules. His 1970 discussion makes a formal point in social choice, not the moral claim defended here. It both supports the distinction and warns that incompleteness alone may fail to settle a menu. Arrow's completeness axiom is a condition on a formal preference ordering over alternatives; the bounded passage does not show that every ethical allocator must rank complete lives. Neither author supplies the manuscript's ethical premises.

Chang provides a more precise warning against treating the failure of an all-things-considered trichotomy as incomparability. The manuscript therefore does not infer incomparability. Its question is whether the act of choosing entails a complete weak-betterness relation between the recipients' lives. It can be answered no even if their lives are on a par, or if there is some other evaluative relation, so long as that relation is not the ranking the policy uses or entails. Chang's discussion of duty as a covering consideration also makes clear that choice can rest on an action-guiding comparison with a different object.

Taurek's lottery is a close substantive analogue but not a settled solution. Parfit argues that giving equal weight to each person's loss can support saving more people. Their disagreement shows that equal concern alone does not decide the weights or the tie rule. The manuscript's equal-claims case holds the number, type, and urgency of claims fixed and uses a lottery only as a conditional procedural option. It does not extend Taurek to one-versus-many cases.

Parfit's discussion of priority makes the clinic example especially risky. Lifetime disadvantage, current need, urgency, and the history that produced present positions can be different reasons for priority. A count of prior treatments is therefore not accepted as a morally adequate corrective measure. The revised manuscript uses a deliberately thin case with equal current claims and an explicitly stipulated equal-claims procedure; it treats all differences in history, rights, efficacy, and urgency as defeaters that reopen rather than silently settle the case.

The essay's modest diagnostic is a bridge audit: identify the objects being compared; state the reasons and their scope; identify any additional premise that would carry an action or claim ranking into a whole-life betterness ranking; and check whether ties, cycles, history, rights, or efficacy defeat the procedure. This is not presented as a novel theory. It is a way to keep a formal or local action conclusion from being overstated as a conclusion about people's lives.

## Source access and limitations

The full articles and books were not all read. The source manifest states the actual coverage item by item. The fresh retrieval retrieved short primary excerpts and article sections, not complete editions. Sen's relevant chapter text was accessible through an unofficial web mirror of the 2017 expanded edition, which identifies the 1970 text and copyright history; this was not an official publisher access path. The Arrow, Sen (1970 article), and Parfit (2000) short excerpt files are byte-for-byte copies of materials already selected and read in the assigned probe lineage. Their use here is inherited evidence, not a fresh full-text retrieval.

The Taurek PDF opens only its cover/copyright page in the browser reader; the quoted page-303 passage was available in the indexed extraction of the Pittsburgh-hosted PDF. The Parfit page-301 sentence was available in an indexed public PDF copy; the browser reader redirected to a page that displays the quotation, not the article body. Both are excerpt-level access, not full readings. No facts about hidden pages, later developments, or field-wide originality are claimed.

Search-result identifiers and exact queries are saved in search-log.json. The browser tool exposes the retrieval result but not a reliable per-query timestamp or full browser/network log. Times below are therefore a bounded observation window, not exact per-source timestamps. An indexed search hit is recorded as such and not mistaken for a full article reading.
