# Source note

The selected claim is narrower than a general theory of group privacy. It concerns optional disclosure mechanisms for a group that has chosen to protect a legitimate confidential exception. Even if a person may freely refuse to disclose, the pattern of other members’ responses can make that refusal informative. The central question is then who, if anyone, has a duty to reduce the avoidable signal.

The closest philosophical antecedents found are Nissenbaum’s contextual integrity (published pp. 138–145, 154–156 in the selected passages) and Rumbold and Wilson’s account of privacy in information inferred from public material. Both support evaluating the roles, context, expected flow, and inferential consequences of a disclosure practice. Loi and Christen directly distinguish the privacy of shared secrets from inferential privacy and caution against an absolute right to block inference. Rawls offers an analogy for distinguishing an accepted practice’s rules from the justification of individual conduct, but his account does not entail the proposed obligation. These sources make any broad originality claim untenable.

The closest practical analogue is the Tri-Council Policy Statement for research involving humans. It recognizes an ethical duty after a confidentiality promise, institutional support for that duty, and group-level anonymity interests where one member seeks to be named. Its domain is research ethics. It does not prescribe that every other participant lie or refuse disclosure to conceal the first participant’s choice. That distinction motivates, but does not prove, a separate question about refusal-pattern signals.

A defensible contribution, if any, is a conditional design-first test: where an organizer or governance participant has expressly accepted responsibility for confidentiality, invited reliance, and can foresee that an opt-out will identify an exception, the first obligation is to change the procedure in a proportionate way that actually reduces the risk. A peer’s truthful cover refusal is a last-resort possible option, not a default duty. No duty follows from capacity, title, or group membership alone. The test remains a proposal, not a settled field-wide novelty result.

The inherited numerical probe assumes ten people, one protected exception who always refuses, and independent non-exempt cover refusal with probability 1/3. Under its stated uniform-prior model, a named refusal vector gives the true protected identity a posterior probability of about 0.295, compared with 0.10 before observing the vector. Under repeated rounds, its model gives about 0.623 after two and 0.849 after three. These are outputs of an idealized Bayesian toy model, not measurements of real audiences. Correlated choices, prior knowledge, unequal behavior, or repeated observations can substantially change the result. The numbers establish only that the proposed channel can carry information under one stipulated model.

The source search found established work on contextual information flow, inferred privacy, group confidentiality, explicit confidentiality duties, and privacy-preserving research procedure. It did not establish a general moral duty for peers to imitate an opt-out. This is a bounded search, not proof that no such argument exists. Field-wide novelty remains unknown.
