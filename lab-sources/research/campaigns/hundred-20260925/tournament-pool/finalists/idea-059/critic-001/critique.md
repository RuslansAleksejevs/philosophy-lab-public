# Independent critique: “When a New Concept Makes Fewer Complaints”

## Overall assessment

The essay offers a clear and defensible warning: when a reform changes the category or procedure used to record objections, a smaller complaint count may reflect a changed recording channel rather than fewer relevant concerns. Its distinction between policy outcomes, deliberative process, and evidence about either is useful. The essay also avoids the stronger and less defensible claim that old concepts must be retained or that every objection must determine the result.

I recommend **specific revision**. No fatal source or logical defect defeats the conditional warning. The main problem is that its possible contribution is not yet distinguished from established work on measurement validity, classification, and deliberative-quality assessment. The practical test it proposes is also underspecified. At present the essay reads as a thoughtful application of a familiar measurement principle, while its novel status remains unshown.

## Reconstruction

The argument has four parts. First, lower complaint counts are offered as evidence of deliberative improvement only if they continue to track the objections relevant to that claim. Second, conceptual or procedural reform can change what the intake system recognizes and records. Third, that change supplies an alternative explanation for a decline: fewer reports may be counted even if the underlying concerns remain. Therefore, officials cannot infer deliberative improvement from the count alone; they need a sufficiently comparable route or independent evidence. The hypothetical agency form makes the mechanism intelligible, and §§2–4 distinguish loss at the recording stage from failure of uptake after a concern reaches deliberation.

That inference is sound as a warning against an unsupported inference. It does not yet establish a new deliberative norm. Its contribution, if any, is the application of a general measurement concern to complaint counts after conceptual change.

## Major issues

### 1. The novelty claim needs a direct comparison with measurement-validity work

The manuscript recognizes the strongest objection in §5.1: work on conceptual revision, epistemic injustice, and uptake already supplies much of the normative ground. Its reply is that the essay isolates the evidential inference from a quieter record. That is plausible, but the essay does not yet show that this inference adds anything beyond established measurement validity.

The source-check found close work omitted from the essay. Adcock and Collier’s 2026 chapter distinguishes disputes about a background concept from the validity of an indicator and asks whether scores can be interpreted through the concept they operationalize. Köhler’s 2025 account of conceptual engineering treats classificatory devices and their practical effects as relevant, not just changes in word meaning. In democratic-measurement work, Fleuß, Helbig, and Schaal (2018) and Fleuß (2022) already require an explicit account of what counts as deliberative quality, theory-appropriate indicators, and evidence that measures capture the relevant dimensions and communication between sites. These works do not, in the passages checked, make the exact complaint-count point in this essay. They do make its general reasoning look familiar.

Section 5.1 answers the broad “the literature already says this” objection by naming the essay’s measurement focus, but it does not compare the specific claim with these measurement frameworks. The author should add a focused comparison and state exactly what the deliberative setting contributes beyond the general rule that changing an indicator or classification can change what its scores mean. If no distinctive contribution survives that comparison, present the essay as a useful illustration rather than a new philosophical result. No field-wide novelty claim is warranted by the current bounded search.

### 2. “Comparable route” is not yet a test, and is not sufficient for the conclusion

Sections 1–3 repeatedly condition the count’s evidential value on the continued availability of a route for relevant objections. Section 2 usefully says the route includes expression, recognition, and recording, but “sufficiently comparable” is not operationalized. A form can remain formally open while reporting falls because users trust the agency less, expect no response, lack knowledge of the channel, fear retaliation, or no longer think their reasons count. Those changes can defeat the inference without any new label or coding rule. Conversely, an unchanged count category can remain a poor proxy for whether speakers’ reasons received fair consideration.

There is a second gap. Even when the route is comparable, fewer reports show at most fewer recorded objections. They do not by themselves show better deliberation. The same drop could reflect apathy or disengagement; it could also occur because officials answered concerns earlier and people no longer need to file them. The latter may support improvement, but through the response history rather than the count alone. The essay already distinguishes complaint volume from failed uptake in §2 and policy outcomes from process in §4; the argument should carry that distinction into its stated condition. Separate (a) whether counts are comparable, (b) what complaint frequency measures, and (c) what evidence warrants a claim about deliberative quality. Independent evidence should be specified as evidence of process or uptake, not left as an all-purpose escape clause.

The suggested checks in §4—a cross-category sample, open-text submissions, or an audit—are useful illustrations, but none is shown to reveal people who could not or would not use the channel. Explain what each check can establish and what remains unknown. A feasible example in which a route is formally preserved but is not meaningfully used would make the standard sharper.

### 3. The boundary for “relevant objections” needs an independent basis

Section 3 limits relevant objections to those challenging a public reason offered for the reform or identifying a reasonably foreseeable cost tied to its stated purpose. Section 5.4 correctly sees the danger of endless scope disputes, but “foreseeable” is left open: foreseeable to whom, under what information, and at what time? If officials can define the purpose narrowly and decide what effects count as foreseeable, the criterion risks letting the reformers exclude the very concerns whose disappearance they then treat as evidence of success.

Give a bounded standard that does not depend only on the reformers’ preferred description—for example, prior complaint patterns, affected groups’ testimony, or a defined set of public reasons and reasonably available evidence. Explain how the standard handles a concern that was excluded by the new category precisely because the old record did not make it visible. This is a real scope problem, but it need not become a demand to hear every imaginable objection.

### 4. Keep the concept change distinct from the intake change

The essay alternates between reforming the meaning of “fair,” changing the form, and changing the coding rule. In the agency example these occur together, so the case does not show which mechanism matters. If the new concept is adopted but a different-language route still captures the same reasons, the count may remain comparable. If the form changes while the underlying concept does not, the same recording problem can occur without conceptual reform. Clarify whether the thesis concerns conceptual change itself, administrative classification, or their combination. The label “expressively closing” should help distinguish these mechanisms; as currently defined in §2, it mostly renames the result that an objection is unavailable to the assessment procedure.

## Source accuracy and engagement

The central source summaries are accurate within the supplied excerpts. Scudder is correctly treated as the closest normative precedent: uptake is fair consideration, not agreement, and complaints or participant reports are suggestive rather than conclusive evidence. The descriptions of Haslanger, Fricker (2017), and Plunkett and Sundell’s metalinguistic negotiation are broadly supported. The essay also properly admits that its example is hypothetical and its literature search bounded.

Two small corrections would improve source control. Young’s supplied evidence is only an opening indexed excerpt and does not verify the printed page; the broad claim is plausible but should either receive a traceable page citation or be narrowed to what that excerpt supports. The Plunkett–Sundell reference gives the 2019 online-first date, while the journal issue is 2021; use one consistent citation convention.

## Significance, scope, and presentation

The topic matters because agencies and evaluators often use administrative records to claim that reforms reduced disagreement or improved public deliberation. The contribution is currently modest: it diagnoses one way an indicator can become misleading, but supplies no empirical case, threshold of comparability, or tested assessment method. That is acceptable for a conceptual essay if the claim is framed as a limited diagnostic and its relation to measurement theory is made explicit.

The prose is clear and the division between recording loss and failed uptake is especially helpful. The argument is repeated across the abstract, §§1–3, and conclusion; some repetition could be removed to make room for the comparison and test above. Premise 1 in §3 says participants’ reasons “could affect” the process; use wording that clearly means fair consideration, not a guarantee of influence, since §5.3 rightly rejects any right to dictate the outcome.

## Critic’s additional argument

The following point is introduced by this critic, not claimed as part of the author’s original contribution: route comparability is a condition on comparing counts, but cannot by itself validate the inference from fewer complaints to better deliberation. A stable channel may produce fewer complaints because concerns were resolved early, because people disengaged, or because trust changed. These possibilities have different implications and require process evidence beyond a count. The essay should present the count as one possible indicator within a broader evaluation, not as a measure that becomes decisive once the intake route is shown to be stable.

## Required revision

1. Compare the proposed inference directly with existing measurement-validity and deliberative-measurement frameworks; state a narrower contribution that survives that comparison.
2. Define what evidence makes an objection route comparable, and separate measurement comparability from evidence of deliberative improvement.
3. Ground “relevant/foreseeable objection” in a criterion not controlled solely by the reformers.
4. Distinguish conceptual revision from changes to forms and coding rules; show what “expressive closure” adds.
5. Correct the two bibliographic precision issues noted above and retain the bounded originality claim.

**Recommendation:** specific revision. The central warning survives as a limited and intelligible application, but its originality and practical test remain unresolved. This recommendation assesses the manuscript only; it does not decide campaign acceptance.
