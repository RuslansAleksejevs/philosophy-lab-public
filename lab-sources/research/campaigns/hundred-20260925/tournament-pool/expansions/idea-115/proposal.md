# When a thinner future matters: a calibration test for cross-temporal compensation

**Status:** conditional argument proposal, not a finished essay. It develops the selected candidate `idea-115` as an explicit descendant. No outside literature or empirical claim about any animal species was checked in this expansion.

## Question and revised claim

When a theory uses weak psychological continuity to discount a sentient being’s stake in distant future events, may it still count later pleasure as compensation for present suffering in that being’s life?

The answer is: sometimes, but not by continuity-talk alone. Psychological continuity may govern a present stage’s anticipatory or future-directed interests while a different, independently defended relation governs which experiences belong to one welfare-bearing life. Those grounds need not be identical. A theory that relies on this distinction can be coherent. It still owes a clear account of the welfare bearer, the rule for combining welfare across time, and the difference between a higher lifetime total and permission to impose suffering. The surviving contribution is therefore a **calibration challenge**, not a proof that cross-temporal compensation is inconsistent.

The claim is conditional. It targets theories that make both moves: they use weak continuity to reduce the significance of a later outcome for an earlier stage, yet use that later outcome to offset an earlier bad experience. It does not claim that simple sentient creatures actually lack particular psychological capacities, that all welfare must be aggregated, or that every later benefit must be excluded from a life-level total.

## The relations that must be kept apart

“Continuity” can name several relations. This proposal uses three labels to prevent an equivocation.

- **Psychological connection (`P`)**: relations such as memory, carried intentions, stable preferences, or anticipation between stages of a life. The proposal does not assume a single measure or threshold for `P`.
- **Welfare-bearer relation (`W`)**: the rule that identifies which experiences count as good or bad for one continuing welfare subject, or as parts of one subject’s life. `W` might be defended by organismic persistence, a sentient subject, or some other account. This proposal does not endorse one of those accounts in advance.
- **Compensation rule (`K`)**: a rule that says how one good and one bad experience relate. “Compensation” may mean only that a later good raises a total welfare score. It may instead mean that a bad has been made up for from the standpoint of a subject. Neither meaning by itself establishes that it is permissible to impose the bad.

A theory can hold that `P` is weak while `W` remains strong. It can then say that a present stage has little anticipatory stake in a remote future, yet that later pleasant experience still contributes to the welfare of one life. This is not a formal contradiction: the first claim concerns a stage’s relation to a future outcome, while the second concerns which experiences enter a life-level assessment. But the distinction needs independent support. Naming two relations does not show that the chosen boundary is morally appropriate.

## Argument

**P1.** A claim that weak psychological continuity reduces the importance of a distant future must specify what importance is being reduced. It might concern a present desire, anticipation, a future-directed interest, the harm attributed to deprivation, or the identity of the welfare subject. These are distinct claims.

**P2.** A reduction in one kind of present stake does not, by itself, establish that later welfare is excluded from the subject’s life-level welfare. That inference requires a bridge premise connecting `P` to `W` or to `K`.

**P3.** If a theory says that later pleasure compensates for present pain, it must state what makes the two experiences eligible to be combined. It also must say whether “compensation” means an improved aggregate, a benefit to the earlier stage, a benefit to the same welfare subject, or a moral permission to impose the pain.

**P4.** Different rules for `P` and `W` can be coherent when each is independently motivated and applied consistently. For example, a theory could ground present-directed interests in a stage’s actual mental orientation while grounding lifetime welfare in a continuing sentient subject. The first ground would not imply that the second is false.

**P5.** A distinct-ground reply does not settle whether the relevant welfare subject or aggregation rule is defensible. A proposed `W` rule should be stated without reference to the desired result in the case, apply to comparable benefits and harms, and remain stable when the signs of the experiences are reversed. Otherwise the distinction risks being selected only when it permits the preferred compensation.

**P6.** Even a justified positive lifetime total does not entail that imposing severe suffering is permissible. A further moral principle must connect aggregation to permission, and that principle may face constraints about distribution, consent, rights, or avoidable harm.

**Conclusion.** The original inconsistency charge is too strong if it assumes that psychological continuity must govern every temporal relation. A narrower challenge survives: where a theory invokes weak `P` to discount future-directed stakes but invokes later welfare to offset present suffering, it must defend a separate `W` and `K` account and show how those claims bear on permission. Without that account, the theory has not earned its cross-temporal compensation claim. With a clear and independently defended account, the apparent inconsistency may disappear, though a separate justification of imposed suffering remains.

This conclusion takes the assessors’ central doubt seriously. The Astra assessment asks whether deprivation-based interests and compensating welfare can rest on different relations without ad hoc selectivity. The Sol assessment likewise treats two grounds as a possible defeat. The answer here is that they **can** be different, but the distinction is explanatory work rather than a free pass. The root selection reason correctly warns against assuming that continuity does the same work on both sides. This proposal therefore preserves the selected idea’s conditional core and narrows its burden: it tests whether a theory has a principled cross-temporal accounting rule, not whether every theory must use one relation for all purposes.

## Worked case: Mica’s two stages

Consider a stipulated hypothetical sentient being, Mica. At stage `t0`, Mica undergoes a severe painful experience and has no memory, plan, or anticipation that represents a later stage. At `t1`, Mica undergoes a pleasant experience. Stipulate for this case that the same living organism persists between `t0` and `t1`; do not infer that any real animal has Mica’s psychology. The stipulations isolate the conceptual question.

A simple one-ground view might say that only strong `P` joins experiences into one prudential subject. Since `P` is stipulated to be weak, it may count the `t1` pleasure as no compensation for Mica at `t0`. That view still needs to explain what it means by “one subject” and whether the later stage has a distinct welfare claim.

A two-ground view might say that `P` governs anticipation and stage-relative interests, while `W` follows the continuing sentient life. It can include both experiences in one lifetime assessment even though Mica at `t0` did not anticipate `t1`. For illustration only, if a stipulated accounting scale assigns `−8` to the pain and `+10` to the later pleasure, the view may report a positive net lifetime total. Those figures are placeholders, not measurements or a proposed welfare metric. This can be coherent without saying that the later pleasure was experienced by a psychologically connected earlier self.

That answer has two limits. First, it must defend why `W` follows the life or organism in this case, rather than selecting bodily persistence because it conveniently retains the later benefit. A stable rule that counts later harms as well as benefits would help show that the account is not sign-selective, though it would not by itself prove that the rule is morally correct. Second, a positive total does not show that causing Mica’s pain was permissible. If “compensated” is meant to justify imposing the pain, the theorist must provide a further permission rule. A theory with a lifetime sum and a constraint against deliberately inflicting severe pain can accept both experiences in one total while rejecting the act that caused the pain.

The case thus separates three questions that the sketch left compressed: whether Mica at `t0` has an anticipatory stake in `t1`; whether the two experiences belong to one welfare-bearing life; and whether the later benefit makes the earlier pain permissible. A lower answer to the first question does not determine the other two.

## Strongest objection and proposed answer

**Objection.** This proposal still demands too much. Future-directed interests and total welfare answer different questions. A present stage need not anticipate or desire a later pleasure for that pleasure to make the same continuing life go better. A theorist can use psychological continuity for the former and the welfare subject’s biological or sentient persistence for the latter. Requiring a shared ground would confuse interests with aggregation and make the original consistency challenge question-begging.

**Answer.** The objection succeeds against a stronger version of the sketch: different grounds are not inherently inconsistent, and no single relation must explain anticipation, subjecthood, aggregation, and moral permission. This proposal accepts that point. Its challenge is not “use the same ground or contradict yourself.” It is “state and defend the distinct grounds, and do not slide between their conclusions.” If the theorist’s claim is only that a later experience improves the total welfare of a continuing life, the proposal does not show inconsistency. If the theorist goes further and says that this total makes present suffering compensated or permissible while weak continuity had been used to discount the future, the bridge from `P` to `W` and from `K` to permission must be made explicit. That is a narrower, answerable burden.

The objection also identifies the main way this proposal could fail as a substantive challenge: a primary account may already give independent, general, non-selective grounds for `P`, `W`, `K`, and the permission rule. If so, the expansion should report that account accurately and treat the selected idea as a diagnostic question about scope, not as a new inconsistency argument.

## What would defeat or narrow the claim

The calibration challenge is defeated for a target theory if a primary-source account supplies distinct grounds for stage-relative interests and life-level welfare, states why each ground applies, treats benefits and harms by the same rule, and does not infer permissibility from a positive total without a separate moral principle. That result would not prove every application correct; it would show that the proposed inconsistency is not automatic.

The challenge should be narrowed or set aside if “future interest” in the target theory means only a present preference, while “compensation” means only a neutral whole-life total. Those claims can coexist without answering whether suffering is justified. The argument would then be a warning about terminology, not a challenge to the theory’s consistency.

It should also be set aside if the relevant theory uses psychological continuity only for personal identity but supplies a separate, stable account of the welfare subject that independently licenses the aggregation at issue. Conversely, if a theory shifts its subject boundary across cases or valence signs without a principled reason, the concern becomes stronger. No empirical claim about actual animal memory, anticipation, or self-understanding follows from the hypothetical case. Any application to a species must add reliable empirical evidence and distinguish observed capacities from the moral rule.

## Significance if correct

The result would give animal-ethics arguments a useful audit: do not infer from a “thinner self” either that future matters less in every sense or that later pleasure straightforwardly pays for present pain. The theory must say which relation is doing which job. This could expose selective accounting while also protecting coherent views from a false charge of contradiction. It may clarify debates about distant welfare, deprivation, and lifetime aggregation, but no novelty or field-wide significance is established here.
