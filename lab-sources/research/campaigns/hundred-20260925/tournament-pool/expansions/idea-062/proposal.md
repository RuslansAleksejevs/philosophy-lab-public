# What an error theorist can mean by “I’m sorry”

## Status and question

This is an expanded argument proposal, not a finished essay. It asks: **Can a person who rejects objective moral wrongness sincerely apologize to someone they have harmed, without pretending to believe the moral claim their words may appear to make?**

The answer defended here is conditional. An error theorist can sincerely apologize when the exchange is a truthful acknowledgment of what happened, an expression of regret, and a real undertaking to repair or change. That does not show that the speaker sincerely asserts that an objective moral property of wrongness exists. If the apology’s wording or context requires that assertion, the speaker cannot sincerely make it while rejecting it. The proposal therefore separates an apology’s **moral avowal** from its **accountability undertaking**. The latter can survive in some cases; it does not automatically replace the former.

The scope is deliberately narrow. “Moral error theory” here means denying that actions have objective moral properties or that moral claims report such properties. A stronger, global normative error theory also denies objective normative facts of every kind. Those positions face different burdens. The proposal does not assume that every expression of regret is an apology, that every apology must be accepted, or that a practice-relative commitment has the authority of an objective moral right.

## Terms and the proposed distinction

An **objective moral avowal** is an assertion or sincere endorsement that the action was wrong in virtue of an objective moral fact, or that the injured person had an objective moral claim against the speaker. If the speaker believes no such facts obtain, this avowal cannot be sincere in the ordinary belief-based sense.

An **accountability undertaking** is a first-person commitment to face the injured person’s complaint, stop using a discredited excuse, and take specified steps such as undoing harm or avoiding a repeat. It can be described without saying that the commitment is backed by an objective moral property. The speaker’s sincerity consists in actually intending the undertaking and being willing to act on it, not in secretly believing the rejected property exists.

“Standing” must also be used carefully. It can mean an objective moral entitlement to demand an answer, or a more limited role in a shared arrangement: the person affected is the one the speaker has agreed to answer, and may call the speaker back to the agreement. A voluntary undertaking can establish or accept that second role within a practice. It cannot, by itself, prove the first. Calling both things “standing” without this distinction would simply hide the original objection inside a new word.

## Argument

1. A familiar apology can carry several things at once: a claim about what happened, recognition of the other person’s injury, regret, an answer to the other person’s complaint, and an offer or promise of repair. The proposal does not assume that all of these have the same sincerity condition.

2. If, in a particular exchange, “I should not have done that” is offered as an assertion that an objective moral requirement applied, an error theorist who denies such requirements cannot sincerely assert it. A voluntary promise to behave differently does not make that asserted proposition true, and the speaker must not use a practical commitment to imply that it does.

3. Some apologies can make their force clearer. A speaker can state the action and its effects, acknowledge the relevant agreement or request, express regret, and offer repair while being candid that they do not believe in objective moral wrongness. These statements may still be painful or inadequate to hear, but their sincerity need not depend on a belief the speaker lacks.

4. A real undertaking differs from a report of taste. “I dislike that I did this” can describe the speaker’s reaction while leaving the injured person with no answer, repair, or constraint on repetition. By contrast, “I will delete the copies, correct what I told others, and answer you if you ask whether I kept the agreement” commits the speaker to observable actions. The directedness of the act is explained by who was affected and who the speaker is addressing, rather than by a claim that the speaker has discovered an objective moral property.

5. For a moral-only error theorist, the undertaking need not conflict with the theory. Such a speaker may accept nonmoral facts about an agreement, trust, consequences, or a relationship, and may have practical reasons arising from those commitments. The argument does not infer that these are objective moral facts.

6. The global error theorist has a harder case. They cannot honestly say that the injured person possesses categorical moral authority if they deny every objective normative fact. They may nevertheless adopt a policy, keep a promise, or accept a role in a shared practice because they care about the person, value the relationship, or choose to be held to that arrangement. If even the word “standing” sounds like an objective normative claim, the speaker should say what they will do instead of claiming that the other person has a moral right. Whether that is enough to count as an apology is a genuine boundary question, not something settled by stipulation.

7. Speaker sincerity, apology adequacy, and successful repair are different outcomes. A speaker may sincerely offer an accountability undertaking; the recipient may reasonably judge that it avoids the moral recognition they need; and the relationship may remain unrepaired. Recipient acceptance is evidence about uptake and adequacy, but it does not by itself determine whether the speaker truly intends the offered actions.

**Conclusion:** Error theory does not rule out every sincere apology. It rules out a sincere objective moral avowal by a speaker who rejects that avowal. A narrower apology can remain sincere as acknowledgment, regret, and undertaking, provided the speaker is transparent about what they mean and follows through. The claim is possibility, not universality: some recipients and contexts rightly require more than this form can give.

## Strong objection and reply

The strongest objection is that the distinction relocates the contradiction. An apology that gives the other person “standing” or undertakes repair already treats that person as having a normative claim. If the speaker denies all normative facts, then “I will answer to you” is either an insincere concession or only a strategic display. The familiar practice of apology may require the speaker to admit that they were wrong, not merely to report harm and volunteer future behavior. On that account, the proposal redescribes a promise as an apology and leaves the error theorist unable to apologize sincerely.

This objection succeeds against any version that treats a self-imposed promise as proof of an objective right, or presents an accountability undertaking as equivalent to moral acknowledgment. The present claim is narrower. A promise can create a real descriptive fact about what the speaker has committed to do and a practice-internal role for the parties, without proving an objective moral duty. A global error theorist may accept those facts while denying categorical moral authority; if their theory also prohibits any sincere first-person commitment, then this route is closed for them. That is a substantive limit, not a verbal escape.

The addressee’s response must be tested separately. If the injured person says, “I need you to recognize that what you did was morally wrong, not just offer a repair plan,” the speaker cannot honestly pretend to meet that condition. The apology may fail as moral acknowledgment even while the undertaking remains sincere. Conversely, the recipient’s refusal does not establish that the speaker was lying about intending repair. The proposal should count as defeated or sharply narrowed if ordinary apology practice necessarily makes objective moral avowal a condition of sincerity, rather than one possible component of a larger act.

## Worked case

Mara and Lee agree that Lee’s private draft will not be shared. Mara forwards it to a group, exposing Lee’s unfinished work. Mara is a moral error theorist and, in the stronger version of the case, denies objective normative facts generally. She says: “I forwarded your draft after agreeing to keep it private. That exposed work you had not chosen to share. I regret doing it. I will ask everyone to delete their copies, send a correction, and let you ask me what I did to prevent it happening again. I do not believe that an objective property called wrongness exists, so I will not claim that I do. I am willing to be held to our agreement and to do those things.”

The case separates the claims at issue. Mara can believe that she forwarded the file, that Lee had asked for privacy, and that the sharing exposed the draft. She may genuinely regret the loss of trust. Her specified commitments can be sincere if she intends to carry them out. Under a moral-only error theory, she may also accept nonmoral reasons supplied by the agreement or the relationship. Under global error theory, she cannot honestly describe Lee’s complaint as an objectively binding moral demand; the most she can offer is a chosen commitment and a practical role for Lee within their arrangement.

Lee may reply: “I appreciate the correction, but I do not accept this as an apology because you still refuse to acknowledge that you wronged me.” If Lee does, Mara has not achieved repair and may not have offered the kind of apology Lee reasonably requires. That response does not show that Mara’s intended deletion and correction were insincere. It shows that accountability undertaking and moral acknowledgment can come apart. If the proposal cannot explain why Mara’s speech is recognizable as an apology at all, rather than only as a promise or damage-control offer, the thesis must be narrowed further to “sincere apology-like accountability” or rejected.

## What would defeat or narrow the claim

The central claim would be substantially weakened by a strong account of apology showing that sincere apology necessarily asserts or presupposes objective moral wrongness, even in transparent cases where the speaker distinguishes regret, factual acknowledgment, and repair. It would also be weakened if the proposed practice-internal role cannot be described without attributing normative authority that a global error theorist denies, or if commitments that lack such authority are necessarily strategic rather than sincere.

The claim should be narrowed to moral-only error theory if the global variant cannot sincerely adopt even a voluntary policy or relationship-based constraint. It should be narrowed to a possibility for some recipients if an apology without moral avowal can be sincere but often fails the recipient’s reasonable conditions for adequate repair. It should be rejected if “accountability undertaking” is only a relabeling of insincere moral concession, or if closest existing arguments already establish the same distinction without the proposed boundary test.

## Significance and research status

If correct, the proposal explains how a person can remain intellectually honest about a metaethical view while still answering another person in a morally charged exchange. It also gives a concrete test for what an error theory rules out: not necessarily all regret, acknowledgment, commitment, or repair, but perhaps only those apologies whose content or force depends on a moral claim the speaker rejects. This could make the interpersonal consequences of metaethical positions more exact.

No primary philosophical source was retrieved or read for this expansion. The idea’s originality is therefore **unknown**, and no closest-literature claim is made. The next probe must compare primary work on moral error theory, expressivist and fictionalist treatments of moral language, sincerity and force in speech acts, apology, and practice-based promises or standing. The probe must determine whether the avowal–undertaking distinction is already familiar, whether it answers the relocation objection, and whether it preserves an apology rather than only a commitment to repair.

## Response to the supplied assessments

The Astra assessor’s challenge asks whether directed standing can be adopted without objective authority and whether the recipient accepts the resulting position. This proposal accepts the first as a limiting question and distinguishes practice-internal answerability from objective moral entitlement. It treats recipient acceptance as a test of uptake and adequacy, not as a simple test of the speaker’s sincerity. The assessment’s separate contribution remains attributed to its Astra session; this proposal develops a response to it rather than presenting it as the original idea.

The Sol assessor deferred because the relocation objection threatens coherence and because expressivism and fictionalism require investigation. The objection is granted as the strongest threat: merely replacing “moral duty” with “standing” would fail. The reply therefore narrows the claim to transparent commitments and leaves open whether a global error theorist can sincerely offer enough for an apology. The requested engagement with expressivism and fictionalism remains an unperformed source test, not a result.

The root selection reason adds that granting standing cannot itself settle whether the apology sincerely avows what its words say. The proposal agrees. Its central distinction is not evidence that ordinary words lack assertoric content; it is a conditional claim about cases where the speaker explicitly distinguishes the assertion they reject from the undertaking they make. Whether those cases remain apologies in ordinary usage is unresolved.
