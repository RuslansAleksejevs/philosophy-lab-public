# Opportunity Without Acknowledgment: A Notice Rule for Unilateral Withdrawal

**Status:** expanded argument proposal; not a finished essay. No primary-source or closest-literature search was performed for this stage. Novelty is unknown.

## Question

When a person has permission to continue an activity only because another person presently authorizes it, can the permission-holder prevent its withdrawal simply by refusing to acknowledge a clear, fair notice of withdrawal? The narrow case is an ongoing, otherwise permitted use that the authorizer has authority to end unilaterally. The question is whether actual recognition is necessary for the change in permission, or whether an accessible and intelligible opportunity to understand the notice is enough.

## Revised claim and scope

The initial idea treated the issue as a possible condition on the success of certain speech acts: perhaps actual hearer uptake should be replaced by an available fair opportunity to understand. The decisive objection is that this may confuse linguistic success with a change in moral permission. I accept that objection as a limit on what this proposal can establish.

The defensible claim at this stage is narrower and normative:

> When A has an independently established, unilateral authority to end B’s permission for an ongoing cooperative use, A can end that permission by intending to do so and giving B clear notice through a reasonably accessible channel, after B has had a fair opportunity to understand the notice. B’s actual acknowledgment is not an additional condition. Actual recognition can still matter to blame, reliance, and remedies.

This is a proposed rule about the exercise of a revocation authority, not yet a theory of illocutionary or linguistic success. It preserves the original idea’s anti-veto concern while moving the conclusion to the level the argument can presently support. It does not show that warnings, promises, appointments, declarations, or speech acts generally succeed without uptake. It also does not establish that every refusal changes an existing normative relation: the authority to refuse or withdraw must be independently present.

“Fair opportunity” is more demanding than a message being somewhere in the world. At minimum, the notice must state the withdrawal clearly; use a channel that A is entitled to treat as available because it was agreed or is an ordinary, accessible channel for this cooperation; enter that channel without a known delivery failure; and remain available for a reasonable period suited to the context. A may set a later effective time to protect reliance. A known outage, an inaccessible format, a wrong address, or an unreasonable deadline defeats the condition. The relevant threshold is an objectively fair chance to understand, not proof that B formed the thought “A has withdrawn permission.”

## Argument

1. **The case begins with an authority, not with a sentence.** Assume A granted B permission for an ongoing use, and that the permission is defeasible at A’s unilateral choice. This assumption must be established from the parties’ original arrangement or other relevant reasons; the notice itself cannot create a revocation power that A did not have. If permission was irrevocable, jointly controlled, or limited by a different condition, this argument does not apply.

2. **A unilateral power would be incomplete if B’s acknowledgment could keep the permission alive.** If A may end permission without B’s consent, then B cannot have a discretionary power to prevent that ending merely by withholding recognition. Otherwise B, whose conduct is the object of A’s authority, would acquire the final say over whether A can exercise it. This premise concerns control over the permission, not the semantics of a particular utterance. It rules out acknowledgment as a veto; it does not yet tell us what notice is fair.

3. **Private intention alone is not a fair trigger for a change in B’s practical obligations.** If A silently changes her mind, B cannot reasonably be guided by that inaccessible fact. Treating an uncommunicated intention as enough would allow A to make B’s previously permitted conduct wrong without giving B a chance to adjust. The authority to revoke therefore needs a communicative condition, even if that condition is not actual uptake.

4. **The condition should track fair access to the reason for adjustment.** A clear notice in an accessible channel gives B a reasonable basis to learn that permission has ended. Acknowledgment adds a fact about B’s mental response, but does not add a fairness reason when B already had an adequate opportunity. Making acknowledgment constitutive would let deliberate silence preserve a permission that A is entitled to terminate. Conversely, mere intention, inaccessible communication, or a failed delivery supplies no comparable opportunity and cannot fairly impose the changed practical demand.

5. **Therefore, in this restricted case, the permission ends at the fair-opportunity threshold, not at A’s private intention and not at B’s discretionary acknowledgment.** The conclusion is defeasible: a defect in authority, clarity, channel, delivery, or timing means the threshold has not been met. Acknowledgment is one possible way to establish that B understood, but it is not the only way to establish that A gave a fair opportunity.

The inference turns on a modest control principle: the holder of a unilateral revocation power should not need the addressee’s cooperation to exercise it, while the addressee should receive a fair chance to adjust. The proposed notice threshold attempts to satisfy both sides. It is not derived from the importance of refusal alone, and it does not redescribe moral fairness as a fact about linguistic felicity.

## Worked case

Suppose Mira has allowed Lee to include a set of her drawings in future releases of a collaborative project. Their arrangement leaves Mira free to withdraw permission for future releases. They have used one shared project channel for this work and have treated it as an ordinary place for project notices. Before the next release, Mira posts a direct message there: “I withdraw permission to include my drawings in releases from this point onward.” She sends it early enough for the scheduled release to be changed. The channel is functioning, the message is readable, and no special circumstance makes it inaccessible. Lee sees the notification but deliberately leaves the message unopened and later says that Mira’s withdrawal did not count because he never acknowledged it.

On the proposed account, Lee’s non-acknowledgment does not preserve permission for the next release. The authority to withdraw was already part of the arrangement; the notice was clear, reached the ordinary accessible channel, and allowed a reasonable interval for adjustment. Lee’s refusal to inspect it cannot give him control over Mira’s authority. Whether Lee is blameworthy for later use depends on what he knew, why he avoided the message, and the available evidence. Those questions are not needed to decide whether Mira’s permission remains in force.

Change one fact: the channel has been unavailable for several days, and Mira knows messages are not reaching Lee. Posting the same words there does not create a fair opportunity. Mira must use another reasonably accessible channel or set an effective time after adequate notice. The view therefore does not turn every attempted communication into an immediate change in permission.

A harder variant is an ordinary message that reaches the agreed channel but is missed accidentally. If it remained available for a reasonable period and Lee had the normal opportunity to check the channel, permission can still end even if Lee never actually reads it. This is the proposal’s most contestable implication. It keeps acknowledgment from becoming a veto, but it may place a real burden on someone who did not in fact know. If that burden is unacceptable even when the channel and interval are fair, the rule must be narrowed—perhaps to require actual receipt rather than mere opportunity—or rejected.

## Strongest objection and proposed answer

The strongest objection, independently raised in the inherited assessments, is that the anti-veto case may establish only a moral change in permission. It does not establish that the withdrawal succeeds as a speech act. The Astra assessor’s exact challenge was whether the opportunity condition can be defended as speech-act success rather than merely a change in moral permission (contribution `astra-assessment-idea-005`, assessor session `01a0dbb8-f987-7ab3-887f-5f4d38ed758a`). The Sol assessor likewise asked whether intention plus fair notice fully explains the normative effect without any claim about speech-act uptake; Sol supplied no separate substantive argument contribution. These are related but distinct checks: the first tests the proposed linguistic level, and the second tests whether the narrower notice account has explanatory work to do.

The answer here is a concession followed by a narrower defense. The argument does **not** defend the opportunity condition as a general condition of speech-act success. A recipient might fail to recognize an utterance even though the utterance is linguistically well formed; a normative permission might change without settling whether the utterance counted as a successful refusal or withdrawal. This proposal lacks the primary-source and conceptual work needed to decide that question. On this point the Astra challenge succeeds against the initial formulation.

The narrower rule is not simply the claim that an intention plus any fair notice magically settles the matter. Intention identifies A’s exercise of an already existing power; clear notice gives B a reason and opportunity to adjust; the fair-channel and timing conditions limit the burdens A may impose. The anti-veto premise explains why B’s acknowledgment cannot be an extra consent requirement. The proposed account thus aims to explain when a particular authority is exercised, not how language in general works. It remains possible that a familiar account of consent or notice already supplies the same result. Sol’s concern therefore remains a serious novelty and redundancy test, not something answered by the narrower wording alone.

An additional objection is that objective opportunity may be unfair when B did not actually know. The proposed distinction between permission status and culpability helps but may not fully answer this concern. A can lose the authority to permit a future use even if B deserves excuse for a use undertaken without awareness; remedies or responsibility may be sensitive to actual knowledge and reasonable reliance. Yet if treating an unknowing use as unauthorized is itself too severe, that is evidence against the status/blame separation, not a point that can be settled by stipulation. The next probe should compare accidental nonreceipt, deliberate avoidance, known delivery failure, and reasonable reliance before retaining the rule.

## What would disprove or narrow the claim

The proposal should be abandoned or materially narrowed if any of these tests fails:

- **Authority test:** In the target cases, permission is not in fact unilateral or revocable on A’s choice. Then the anti-veto premise has no application.
- **Fairness test:** A recipient who had only an objective opportunity, but no reasonable way to learn the notice, is still burdened in a way that cannot be justified by accessible-channel rules, a reasonable interval, or reliance protections. The threshold must then require actual receipt or a narrower delivery event.
- **Redundancy test:** A well-supported account of intention and notice already explains the normative result without the proposed distinction between opportunity and acknowledgment. If no further explanatory or practical difference remains, this proposal is not a distinct contribution.
- **Counterexample test:** Clear cases show that actual acknowledgment is constitutive of revocation in this domain even when the addressee’s refusal would leave the authorizer without the unilateral control the arrangement grants. The control premise would need revision.
- **Boundary test:** Applying the same rule to refusals, warnings, promises, or appointments yields implausible results. That would support keeping the claim confined to the narrower revocation case rather than generalizing across speech acts.
- **Source test:** The closest primary literature already states and defends this exact opportunity-based normative rule, with the same authority and timing conditions. The novelty claim would then be withdrawn; at most the project could offer a critical reconstruction or a case-specific qualification.

## Significance if correct

If the rule survives, it separates three questions that the original sketch risked running together: whether A has authority to withdraw; whether A gave fair notice sufficient to exercise it; and whether B deserves blame for later conduct. This can explain why actual recognition should not be a veto without pretending that intention alone can change another person’s practical situation. The result would be a limited account of control over ongoing permissions, not a universal theory of consent or language.

## Research status and remaining originality questions

No primary text was consulted, and no literature or novelty search was performed in this expansion. The present argument is conceptual and conditional. It does not establish that the opportunity rule is original, that it matches any established theory’s terminology, or that the hypothetical cases settle the issue. The assessor materials themselves also report no external-source checking. The next probe must search the nearest primary treatments of speech-act uptake and illocutionary recognition, theories of consent and withdrawal, and accounts of notice and unilateral authority. It should compare their actual conditions with the rule above, not infer novelty from a search phrase or from the sketch’s name.

The most important unresolved distinction is between **the normative exercise of an authority** and **the success conditions of the utterance used to exercise it**. This expansion adopts the first as its provisional target and leaves the second open. If source work shows that the relevant authority is not exercised by communicative notice, or that the proposed category is already well understood, the descendant should be revised or set aside while preserving the original idea and this argument proposal as separate stages.
