# The epistemic cost of making someone stop checking

## Question and provisional answer

Can a reliable expert or information service owe more on withdrawal than truthful notice and ordinary financial compensation when its own assurance led people to give up independent ways of checking it?

**Provisional claim.** When a provider deliberately or foreseeably invites people to replace their own checking with its dependable service, and that substitution materially weakens their ability to recognize or assess later claims in the same domain, the provider has a defeasible duty to make exit epistemically usable. It should preserve or restore the minimum capacity recipients need to evaluate a successor, inspect relevant evidence, or resume independent inquiry. It need not serve forever, guarantee future truth, or recreate every former skill. This is best treated, at this stage, as a role-specific specification of responsible assurance and withdrawal, not as a newly established, irreducible kind of moral duty.

The claim is intentionally narrower than “those who cause dependence must repair it.” It applies only where the impaired thing is recipients' capacity to tell whether later information deserves reliance, the provider helped cause that impairment through an assurance-backed substitution, and the requested transition is proportionate and practically relevant. The argument does not establish that every specialist, platform, or public institution has this duty.

## Argument

1. A person who presents a source as a reliable route to information gives others some reason to rely on it. This is not a promise that the source will remain available indefinitely. It is, however, more than a sequence of isolated true sentences when the source encourages recipients to organize their inquiry around its ongoing reliability.

2. Reliance can alter the recipient's epistemic position. If people stop checking because a provider represents independent checks as unnecessary or redundant, they may lose access to records, practice, monitoring routines, or a working sense of what counts as an error. A later shutdown can therefore leave them unable to judge a replacement source, detect a gap, or reconstruct the relevant evidence. This is a loss of epistemic agency even if they lose no money and every forecast the provider issued was true.

3. A provider that materially helped create this condition has special control over the transition. It can often preserve records, explain the limits of its service, give warning while a fallback remains usable, transfer procedures, or connect recipients to a successor. Its role and contribution make those measures a more direct response than compensation alone.

4. A transition obligation should match the loss. The relevant remedy is enough support for recipients to assess and use a successor or to restart a feasible checking practice. This is a lower and more defensible requirement than restoring full self-sufficiency. A recipient might need an accessible archive and a short explanation of its reliability limits, not a permanent duplicate service.

5. The duty is defeasible. It weakens or disappears when the provider did not induce the abandonment, when an independent fallback remains genuinely usable, when recipients knowingly chose a clearly described and reversible arrangement, or when the proposed transition is disproportionate. It may also be discharged by timely notice and a usable handoff rather than by rebuilding the old system.

**Conclusion.** Under those conditions, truthful notice and financial compensation can be insufficient. A provider may owe an epistemically adequate transition because its assurance-backed service helped make recipients less able to evaluate what comes after it.

The inference is not that truthfulness creates an unlimited guarantee of future access. It is that a provider's role in changing the recipient's ability to assess future testimony changes what adequate withdrawal requires. The conclusion is a limited repair obligation, not a right to continuous service.

## Worked case: two weather services

Imagine two services that have always issued equally accurate forecasts. Service A publishes forecasts but leaves a community's weather stations and local observation routine in place. Service B does more: it tells the community that its own monitoring is wasteful because B's continuous service is more dependable, and it supplies a workflow that gradually replaces the stations and the skill to interpret their readings. The community follows that advice. B later closes, gives equal notice to A, and has never issued a false forecast.

A's closure does not create the same transition problem. The community still has its independent records and the people who know how to use them. B's closure can leave the community unable to tell whether a proposed successor is covering the same local risks or whether a gap matters. That difference survives assumptions that the forecast records are accurate, both contracts permit closure, and neither service caused financial loss. B's duty, if the conditions hold, could be met by transferring machine-readable records, documenting known blind spots, and funding a short period of training or successor overlap. It would not require B to remain open indefinitely.

Now vary the case. If B warned clearly that the service could end, left the stations functional, and made sure the community could retain its own data and skills, the special transition duty may already be discharged. If the community chose to retire a costly station system after receiving a realistic, comprehensible account of the risk and retained a practical re-entry option, the case for requiring B to restore that system is much weaker. These variations make the duty track induced loss of evaluative capacity rather than mere use of an expert.

## Strong objection: this is only ordinary dependency repair, and people accepted the risk

The strongest objection is that there is no distinct epistemic duty here. If B caused a harmful dependence, ordinary duties of care, contract, or repair already explain why it should help. Conversely, if the community knowingly chose specialization and fairly accepted shutdown risk, B owes nothing beyond the agreed notice. Calling the remedy “epistemic” may only rename a familiar obligation.

The reply should concede part of this. The proposal does not yet show a new moral category. Its narrower contribution is a diagnostic and remedial distinction that general dependency language can miss: the relevant harm is a reduced ability to assess later sources, and an adequate repair must restore a usable route to epistemic judgment. Money alone may not do that; an accurate data dump may not do it either if recipients cannot interpret the records. The same provider might owe ordinary compensation for a financial loss and separately owe a proportionate handoff that addresses an epistemic loss. Whether that is a genuinely distinct duty or a domain-specific specification of general repair remains open.

Voluntary specialization also matters, but “accepted the risk” cannot settle the case without describing what recipients could actually choose. Informed, feasible, reversible delegation can narrow the duty or discharge it in advance by preserving records, alternatives, and a workable exit route. Mere boilerplate permission to close does not establish that recipients understood or could preserve their capacity to judge a successor. If the provider actively encouraged them to abandon checks while controlling the information needed to restart them, the fairness of treating the loss as wholly theirs needs argument. This response is provisional: a future probe must test whether a coherent consent condition can eliminate the duty without simply defining away the induced-capacity-loss cases.

## Scope and defeat conditions

The proposal should be rejected or substantially narrowed if any of these tests succeeds:

- A convincing case shows that recipients suffer the proposed epistemic loss but have no claim against the provider once the agreement was informed, feasible, and fairly reversible.
- The duty always reduces to ordinary harm repair, and the proposed epistemic criterion adds no distinct guidance about either who owes what or what remedy is adequate. In that event, “epistemic transition duty” should be dropped as a new label; the result may survive only as a useful application of general repair.
- Restoring evaluative capacity is not a coherent or measurable remedial aim, and proposed measures such as records, training, or successor overlap do not reliably answer the problem.
- Cases where a provider induced dependence but did not weaken recipients' ability to assess claims generate the same duty and remedy. That would undercut the claim that the epistemic feature does explanatory work.
- A close existing account already derives the same trigger, limits, and remedies. That would defeat any originality claim even if the argument itself remains sound.

## Significance and present research limit

If defensible, the account would clarify why responsible withdrawal from epistemic services can require more than notice, truthfulness, or money, while also explaining why the obligation is limited and sometimes avoidable. It could help assess expert services and information infrastructures without treating dependence itself as wrongful.

No primary philosophical source was retrieved or read for this expansion, and no literature or novelty claim is established. The two assessors likewise performed no source check. The next probe must compare this account with work on epistemic dependence, trust, assurance, consent, and duties to repair dependence; it must check whether the proposed trigger and remedy already appear there. Until then, “epistemic transition duty” is only a provisional label for the question, not a claimed discovery.
