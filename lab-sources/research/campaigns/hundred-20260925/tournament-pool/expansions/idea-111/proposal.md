# Optional Signals, Unequal Burdens: A Narrow Duty to Supply Cover

## Status

This is an expanded argument proposal, not a finished essay. It develops the selected idea as a conditional claim. No external source was retrieved or read, and no claim of originality is made.

## Question and claim

Can someone who has no confidential status of their own nevertheless owe a limited refusal to disclose harmless information, because their routine compliance helps make another person's permitted refusal reveal that status?

**Claim.** A person who has voluntarily accepted a governance role in an optional disclosure practice can have a defeasible duty to prevent that practice from making a legitimate confidential exception readily identifiable. If a less restrictive redesign is unavailable in time, one permissible way to discharge the duty may be a truthful refusal by a non-exempt participant. The duty applies only when the participant's role helps sustain the revealing convention, the confidentiality interest is legitimate, the cover is expected to reduce rather than relocate the risk, and the burden is small. Mere membership or ability to help is not enough.

This narrows the selected idea. The original sketch says that some participants in a shared practice may owe cover refusals. The present proposal distinguishes ordinary participants from people who accepted responsibility for setting, administering, or maintaining the practice. If that role distinction cannot be defended, the stronger participant-specific claim should be set aside; the remaining case would be a general duty of easy assistance.

## Terms and scope

An **optional disclosure practice** invites people to reveal a fact, document, or status while formally permitting them to decline. A **revealing convention** exists when observers expect almost everyone without a protected exception to comply, so that declining is evidence of the exception. **Cover** is a pattern of truthful non-disclosure by some people who lack the protected status. It makes the refusal signal less diagnostic; it does not promise anonymity. A **co-maintainer** is someone who has accepted a role in choosing, administering, or publicly reinforcing the practice. The label does not include every person who happens to be invited.

The claim concerns a legitimate confidentiality interest. It does not assume that every secret deserves protection, that observers have no right to infer public facts, or that an affected person must conceal their status. It also does not require a person to make a false statement. Where an independent duty to report, a serious safety need, a strong expressive interest, or a material cost defeats the presumption, the proposed duty does not arise.

## Argument

**P1. Formal permission and practical freedom can come apart.** A rule may say that disclosure is optional while the practice makes refusal costly or informative. A person can be legally free to decline yet face pressure because the audience will treat declining as evidence about them.

**P2. A public routine can turn an otherwise harmless response into a signal.** Suppose a community invites each member to display a certificate. A confidential exemption is valid, and the exempt person may keep it private. If observers expect every non-exempt member to display the certificate, a refusal identifies the exempt member. The public act is voluntary in form, but its social pattern defeats the point of allowing an exception.

**P3. An individual opt-out does not by itself change the signal.** The target may decline without lying, but if the other participants keep the same predictable pattern, observers still learn the protected status. The relevant unit is therefore the practice's distribution of responses, not only the target's formal right.

**P4. A role can create responsibility beyond equal capacity to help.** Someone who undertakes to design or administer the practice has authority over its default, invites reliance on that default, and helps make it common knowledge. If the resulting inference foreseeably burdens a legitimate exception, the co-maintainer has a reason to repair the terms of the practice. A random bystander may be equally able to help, but did not accept this governance role or help establish this expectation. This premise is the proposal's most contestable step: it relies on accepted responsibility, not mere participation.

**P5. A small, truthful, effective repair is a fair term of that role.** When the co-maintainer can change the protocol, hide the individual responses, or arrange non-exempt cover at low cost, refusing to make any repair leaves the exceptional person to bear the whole cost of a defect in a shared arrangement. A refusal to display makes no false assertion where there is no duty to display. It withholds information that the practice has no established right to demand.

**Conclusion.** Under the stated conditions, the co-maintainer has a defeasible duty to repair the practice. If redesign cannot be implemented and a cover refusal is both low-cost and effective, the co-maintainer may owe that refusal as one form of repair. This is not a blanket duty to hide truthful information and not a right to control another person's voluntary self-disclosure.

## Worked case and leakage limit

Assume a group of ten is asked to display a certificate. Exactly one person has a justified confidential exemption and declines. With no cover, the other nine display theirs; an observer who knows the convention can identify the exempt person with certainty. Now suppose, as a deliberately simplified model, that each of the nine non-exempt people independently declines with probability one-third, without using information about who holds the exemption. If the observer sees four people decline, and the observer has no other identifying information, each of those four is equally likely to be the exempt person under this model. The protected person's status is no longer certain, but the observer still narrows the possibilities to four.

That calculation establishes only a conditional model result. It does not show that a real refusal pattern creates meaningful privacy. If cover is selective, correlated with the protected status, repeated across rounds, or visible to observers with side information, the posterior may remain high. A refusal may also move attention to another person or another data source. The duty therefore requires a credible assessment of net leakage; breaking certainty alone is not enough.

In practice, the co-maintainer should first consider less burdensome designs: collect responses privately, disclose only an aggregate, delay release, or make the same minimal response routine for everyone. A refusal by a non-exempt participant is a fallback, not a required default. If it fails to reduce the risk, it cannot discharge the duty.

## Strong objection: autonomy and truthful self-disclosure

The strongest objection is that the proposal treats other people's speech as a privacy tool. A person may have a legitimate interest in displaying a truthful certificate or explaining their own status. Requiring them to remain silent can burden expression and can make innocent compliance look suspicious. The person whose status is inferred has no general veto over what others truthfully say. In some cases, coordinated refusals may even mislead observers by creating a false impression that many people hold exemptions.

The answer is deliberately limited. The proposed duty attaches to a role in governing a practice, not to every speaker. It arises only where the participant helped establish an optional routine with no independent claim to each individual's disclosure, and where redesign is unavailable or more intrusive. It does not override a separate reporting duty, a serious public-interest reason to disclose, or a person's own claim to speak about their status. A refusal is not a claim that the person is exempt; it is an exercise of an option the practice already grants. Still, if strategic silence would predictably function as a false assertion, or would materially impair another person's legitimate agency, that fact can defeat the duty.

This response does not prove that an undertaking creates the relevant obligation. It identifies the premise to test: voluntary acceptance of a governance role may commit its holder to fair terms for exceptions, but merely joining a community or complying once does not establish that commitment.

## Rival explanation and discriminating test

The main rival is a general duty of easy aid. On that view, anyone who can cheaply protect a person from exposure should help; the co-maintainer has no special obligation. A counterfactual test separates the accounts: hold capacity, cost, expected privacy gain, and knowledge fixed, then remove the person's accepted governance role. If the duty remains unchanged, the case supports easy aid rather than a practice-specific duty. If the role changes what is owed because the person undertook to maintain fair procedures, the role account gains support. The same test must compare organizers, routine participants, and uninvolved bystanders rather than treating them as one class.

## What would defeat or narrow the claim

The broad version should be rejected if mere participation does not create a role responsibility distinct from easy aid. The narrowed claim should also be rejected or further limited if co-maintainers have no relevant undertaking; if the confidentiality interest is illegitimate; if the cover pattern leaves observers with near-certain identification; if the risk is displaced to another person; if refusal carries a material burden; or if it interferes with a stronger duty or right. A worked counterexample involving whistleblowing, urgent safety information, or a person's chosen self-disclosure may show that even the narrow rule needs a sharper boundary.

## Significance if correct

If the argument survives, a practice can respect an individual's formal right to refuse and still make that right ineffective through predictable group behavior. Fairness may therefore require procedural protection at the level of the practice. The practical implication is modest: designers and co-maintainers should assess what observers can infer from response patterns, and should prefer protocol changes that protect exceptions without demanding that the exceptional person alone bear the cost. The proposal does not establish that this contribution is new; related work on inferential privacy, group privacy, role responsibility, and duties of assistance may already contain the central argument.
