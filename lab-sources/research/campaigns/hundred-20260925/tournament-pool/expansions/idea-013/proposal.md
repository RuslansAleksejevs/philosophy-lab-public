# Choosing Without Ranking Lives: A Scoped Procedure for Claim-Sensitive Allocation

## Abstract

A resource decision often asks which intervention to fund, not which recipient has the better life. This proposal develops a conditional argument for making that distinction normatively serious. A decision can be justified without a complete ordering of recipients' lives when the available interventions, their relevant effects, and the distributive context are made explicit; when a defensible, possibly incomplete priority relation over the interventions is coherent; and when the procedure explains how it handles incomparable options. The proposal does not celebrate incompleteness as such and does not claim that every allocation problem can avoid whole-life comparison. Its narrower claim is that a complete ranking of whole lives is not a necessary premise in every justified choice. A finite acyclic local priority relation always has a maximal option; a unique maximum determines the choice, while multiple maxima require a separately defended fair tie procedure or leave the decision underdetermined. No literature search or primary-source reading was performed for this expansion, so originality and closest-source questions remain open.

## Question and revised claim

Can a decision procedure legitimately select which benefit to provide while leaving two recipients' complete lives unranked?

The initial idea answers yes by comparing intervention-caused changes and treating unaffected life features as irrelevant. That formulation is too broad if it suggests that background features are irrelevant merely because the intervention does not change them. Prior access, accumulated disadvantage, urgency, rights, and the distribution of past benefits can affect what is owed now. The revised claim is therefore conditional:

> **Scoped claim.** A resource decision can be justified without a complete ordering of recipients' complete lives when (1) the procedure states which intervention effects and background distributive facts count as reasons; (2) those reasons yield an acyclic, stable priority relation over the feasible interventions in the specified context; and (3) the procedure selects a unique maximal intervention or supplies an independently defensible way to choose among incomparable maxima. This justifies an action without entailing a ranking of the recipients' lives as wholes.

The claim is a sufficiency claim, not a universal theory of allocation. It does not say that incomplete comparisons are always fair, that all life comparisons are impossible, or that an official can avoid explaining a choice by calling its reasons “local.” It also distinguishes two objects that are easy to conflate: a relation among available interventions or claims, and a relation ranking each person's complete life against another's. A decision rule may need the former without needing the latter.

## Terms and procedure

Let `M_z` be a finite menu of available interventions in context `z`. The context records morally relevant facts that may include baseline need, severity and duration of a burden, treatment efficacy, prior access to similar benefits, urgency, protected rights, and the distribution of advantages and disadvantages. The context does not contain a complete evaluative summary of each person's life. A fact about family, achievement, pleasure, or loss enters the context only if a defensible distributive or other moral reason makes it relevant to this decision.

For interventions `a` and `b`, write `a ≻_z b` when the stated reasons support choosing `a` over `b` in context `z`. The relation is allowed to be incomplete: if the reasons conflict or do not settle the comparison, neither direction is asserted. It must, however, meet three coherence tests.

1. **Context fidelity.** The rule must identify the distributive facts it uses. A change in a relevant fact may change a priority; an unlisted or irrelevant change may not silently do so.
2. **Menu stability.** If the context and two interventions are unchanged, adding or removing a third option does not reverse their pairwise priority. A reversal needs a stated reason, such as a genuinely changed feasibility constraint, rather than a hidden change of standards.
3. **Acyclicity.** The directed graph of justified strict priorities across connected menus with the same context has no cycle. A cycle would give a chain of reasons for preferring an option to itself and defeats the procedure's claim to coherent direction.

For a finite menu, define a maximal intervention as one for which no other available intervention is strictly preferred. In a finite acyclic graph, at least one maximal intervention exists. If there is exactly one, the local reasons determine the action. If there are several, the partial ordering alone does not determine which to implement. A fair lottery, rotation, or another neutral tie procedure may finish the choice only if that procedure is independently justified and tracks relevant history across repeated decisions. If no such procedure is acceptable, the conclusion is underdetermination, not permission to choose arbitrarily.

This procedure makes no claim that a complete ranking is mathematically impossible. A complete extension can be added to an acyclic finite relation. But where two complete lives remain incomparable under the reasons the procedure recognizes, more than one extension can preserve every justified local priority while ranking those lives in opposite orders. The extension is then one possible representation, not a conclusion established by the local reasons. The procedure's grounds do not require the official to assert which complete life is better.

## Argument

**Premise 1 — A choice needs a justified comparison of feasible actions, but not automatically a comparison of every feature of the people affected.** If a clinic has one treatment slot, its immediate question is which feasible treatment to provide under the relevant evidence and duties. This is already a demanding moral question. It does not, without an additional premise, ask for a total verdict on the entire future and past of each patient's life.

**Premise 2 — Reasons can be action-relative and context-sensitive.** The comparative case for one intervention may depend on what it changes, how serious and preventable the burden is, and how that benefit fits the distribution of prior access. Such reasons can be specific to the intervention while still including background facts that matter to fairness. “Local” therefore means bounded by an articulated set of decision-relevant reasons; it does not mean “ignore everything outside the immediate treatment effect.”

**Premise 3 — A complete-life ranking requires more than those reasons establish.** Suppose the reasons support relieving one person's severe recurring pain rather than eliminating another's mild burden, partly because the first person has repeatedly been denied comparable care. This supports a priority among the available claims if the severity and corrective-priority premises are defended. It does not establish that the first person's life, taken as a whole, is better, worse, or equal to the second's. The comparison concerns a particular benefit under specified distributive conditions.

**Premise 4 — Incompleteness need not make a finite decision directionless.** A coherent partial priority relation can identify a unique maximal action. If it does not, a justified procedure can select among its maximal actions, or it can report that the reasons leave the choice unsettled. This is a real limit on what the procedure can decide, but it is not the same as ranking all lives.

**Premise 5 — Coherence and relevance must be tested, not assumed.** Acyclicity, menu stability, and context fidelity are substantive constraints. A local rule that produces a cycle, changes verdicts when only irrelevant life details change, or ignores relevant disadvantage has failed. The proposal does not infer its success from the modest vocabulary of “local” comparison.

**Conclusion.** Under these conditions, an intervention can be justified without a complete ranking of the recipients' lives. Requiring that ranking adds a further normative commitment unless an independent argument shows that the allocation's reasons cannot be complete without it.

## Worked case

A clinic has one course of treatment available for two patients. Patients A and B have equally severe, recurring pain, and the course is expected to reduce it to intermittent moderate pain for either patient. The clinic's publicly defended rule gives priority first to preventing severe burdens, then—where that priority does not settle a choice—to correcting a documented pattern of unequal access. Its records show that B has received comparable scarce treatments twice, while A has repeatedly been denied them for reasons unrelated to clinical need. Assume the rule's clinical classifications and the access record are reliable and that no overriding right or contraindication is present.

On those assumptions, the severity priority leaves the two claims tied, and the corrective access rule makes A's treatment the unique maximal option. The explanation cites the equal severity and recurrence of both burdens, then the corrective relevance of A's prior denials and B's prior access. It does not require a judgment that A's complete life is more valuable, happier, or better than B's. Changing an unrelated fact—such as either patient's occupation or artistic accomplishments—should not change the result unless the clinic can defend its relevance. Changing a relevant fact—such as evidence that A had already received comparable remedies or that B faced a more serious protected claim—may change the relation or make the options incomparable. That would be context responsiveness, not inconsistency, if the rule states why.

The example does not prove that severity priority or corrective priority is correct. It shows what the argument needs: an explicit rule whose premises can be criticized independently of any total ranking of lives. If the rule cannot be defended, the example fails as a justification even though the distinction between intervention comparison and life ranking remains conceptually available.

## Objections and replies

### Objection 1: Consistent choice implies a complete ordering

The strongest version of the objection says that if an allocator must choose consistently across connected decisions, the allocator must possess a complete ordering of outcomes. If each outcome includes all effects on all recipients, a total ranking is merely being hidden behind local language.

The objection identifies a real risk: choices can reveal cycles, and an allocator can disguise an all-things-considered ranking as an unexamined tie-break. The proposal therefore accepts acyclicity and demands disclosure of any tie procedure. It rejects the further inference that every coherent choice requires a complete ranking of individual lives. A partial relation can have maximal options; a unique maximum can settle a choice. When two lives remain incomparable, multiple total extensions may preserve the same justified edges while reversing the comparison between those lives. Nothing in the action choice selects one extension as the true life-ranking. A theorist who defines rationality to require complete preferences may reject that thinner standard, but completeness is then an additional axiom whose normative role must be defended. It is not supplied merely by the fact that an action was chosen.

This reply is limited. A complete order over feasible interventions may be useful or even required for a particular administrative system. The claim concerns the absence of a necessary inference from “we have a justified action” to “we have ranked the recipients' complete lives.”

### Objection 2: Separability erases distributive disadvantage

Astra's assessment notes that unchanged features support local comparison only if distributive reasons really are separable, and asks whether the procedure can respect background disadvantage that legitimately changes claims. This is decisive against a simple rule that ignores all facts unchanged by the intervention.

The proposal accepts the correction and replaces unqualified separability with context fidelity. Prior disadvantage, earlier allocations, urgency, and rights may be reasons even when the present intervention does not cause them. They must be entered into `z`, with their role in the decision defended. Only facts that the defended rule treats as irrelevant are subject to the invariance test. If a full trajectory of disadvantage is needed for a distributive claim, the context must include that trajectory or a justified summary of it. The proposal fails if the relevant claim can only be represented by ranking complete lives and no narrower reason can do the work.

### Objection 3: The local priority rule is arbitrary, and a lottery is not automatically fair

Severity thresholds and corrective priority can themselves encode controversial judgments. A lottery among incomparable choices may also obscure unequal histories or abandon people whose claims are not fungible.

The objection is correct that the formal procedure cannot certify its substantive rule. This expansion supplies conditions and a worked candidate case, not a universal priority principle. A future probe must defend the rule against an alternative that gives different priority and must test repeated allocations, cumulative benefit, rights, and unequal access. If no fair tie procedure can be justified, the procedure must preserve the unresolved result; it may not describe arbitrary selection as a consequence of incomplete ranking.

## What would defeat or narrow the proposal

The central claim should be rejected or narrowed if a plausible, fully specified rule of this kind produces a directed cycle across connected decisions held at the same relevant context; if the same context and pair of options receive reversed priorities when only allegedly irrelevant details of whole lives change; or if a defensible distribution-sensitive reason cannot be represented without an all-things-considered comparison of complete lives. It should also be narrowed if every non-arbitrary procedure for resolving multiple maximal options requires the very ranking the proposal aims to avoid.

A source investigation could show that the central distinction and its strongest version are already familiar. That would defeat a claim to originality, not by itself the conditional argument. The next author should then either identify a genuinely narrower contribution—such as a diagnostic separating context changes from hidden menu effects—or set the candidate aside. No novelty claim is made here.

## Significance and status

If correct, the proposal offers a disciplined middle position between two extremes: pretending that every allocation requires a total ranking of people and pretending that local reasons excuse incomplete explanation. It asks allocators to expose the reasons that actually govern a choice, account for relevant distributions, test connected choices for cycles, and say plainly when the procedure leaves alternatives unresolved. This could clarify what a justification of a benefit decision does and does not commit an institution to saying about the people affected.

The proposal remains conceptual and provisional. It contains no verified primary-source reading, no literature review, no novelty assessment, no implemented finite model, and no proof that the worked priority rule is just. Its formal observation about finite acyclic relations is a basic structural point, not an originality claim. The closest debates about incomplete orderings, population ethics, person-affecting reasons, and distribution-sensitive priority remain for a separate source-focused probe.
