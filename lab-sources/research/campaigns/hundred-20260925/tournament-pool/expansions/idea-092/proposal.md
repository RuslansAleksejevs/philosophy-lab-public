# Failure before deadline: inevitability and nonachievement

**Status:** expanded argument proposal; not a finished essay.  
**Source status:** no primary-source search or reading was performed. Originality is unknown.

## Question and claim

If the only means of meeting a deadline is irreversibly destroyed before the deadline, has the failure already occurred, or has only its outcome become unavoidable?

The answer depends on which claim “failure” expresses. For a fixed deadline criterion, the proposal distinguishes (1) the time when successful performance ceases to be possible, (2) the time at which the deadline condition is evaluated as unmet, and (3) whether an operative obligation was violated. The first can precede the second. The third can differ from both. A future nonachievement may therefore be settled in advance without the deadline-relative failure itself being relocated to the earlier time.

This is a conditional, modest thesis. It does not say that every omission is an event, that every future proposition is already true, or that all uses of “failure” have one temporal profile. It concerns a task whose success condition and deadline remain fixed. If those conditions change, the failure claim must be stated again.

## Terms and scope

Let **d** be a deadline and **G** the success condition “the designated item arrives no later than d.” Let **c** be a time before d when the only means of satisfying G is irreversibly removed. A failure is **settled at c**, in the relevant practical sense, when every continuation still physically open at c fails to satisfy G. This is stronger than an agent merely believing that success is impossible. It is also defeasible if a backup route, repair, waiver, or change to the success condition later becomes available.

Call the time at which the live continuations first cease to contain a success route the **closure time**. Call d the **criterion time**, the endpoint specified by G. These labels do not decide the metaphysics of absences. “No delivery by d” can be treated as a proposition about an interval and its endpoint; the argument needs no positive absence-object.

The target is the past-tense or adjudicative claim “the delivery deadline was missed,” assessed against a completed, fixed criterion. A prospective claim such as “the deadline will be missed” may be warranted at c. That is not the same claim as saying that the deadline has already passed unmet.

## Argument

1. A deadline success criterion refers to performance within a specified interval ending at d. Whether G was met is evaluated by whether the specified arrival occurred in that interval.
2. The loss of every route to G before d establishes a modal fact about the remaining continuations: from c onward, G cannot be met, absent a later change in the relevant possibilities or criterion.
3. Modal impossibility now does not make d occur now. It determines what can happen by d; it does not change the time named by the deadline or complete the interval that G measures.
4. Thus at c one may truthfully say “success is no longer possible” or “the deadline will be missed,” while reserving “the deadline was missed” for evaluation at or after d under the fixed criterion.
5. An obligation violation is not entailed by either claim. It additionally depends on whether a relevant duty was in force and on what that duty required.

The conclusion is not that a single entity called “failure” travels through time. It is that three predicates often run together in ordinary speech: loss of possibility, nonachievement relative to a deadline, and violation of an obligation. Their truth conditions can diverge.

The original sketch compared this with a positive event whose future occurrence is already causally fixed. That analogy is suggestive but not decisive: an omission need not be an event of the same kind. The stronger support is the structure of the deadline criterion itself. “By d” specifies the interval against which performance is judged. Prior certainty about the interval's outcome does not, by itself, shorten that interval.

## Worked case

A sealed parcel contains the only copy of a required document. It must arrive at a named office by noon on Tuesday. At 10 a.m. Monday, a person with access destroys the parcel and all copies; assume no reconstruction, substitute, or other delivery path is physically available. The requirement remains active.

At 10 a.m. Monday, the person may have made success impossible. If the stipulated conditions really exclude every remaining route, “the deadline will be missed” is already warranted. The person may also have committed a present act of sabotage or breached a present duty to preserve the document. Neither point shows that the parcel failed to arrive *by noon Tuesday* on Monday. The deadline interval has not ended.

At noon Tuesday, the fixed delivery criterion can be evaluated: no qualifying arrival occurred by its endpoint. If a replacement route unexpectedly opens on Tuesday morning and a qualifying delivery occurs by noon, the earlier claim of settled failure was mistaken because the supposed closure was not irreversible. If delivery occurs at 12:10 p.m., the item is no longer absent from the office, yet the “by noon” criterion remains unmet. This shows why failure to meet a deadline cannot simply mean the current absence of the item.

Now suppose the office validly cancels the requirement at 9 a.m. Tuesday. Two questions must be separated. The historical outcome question—did this designated item arrive by noon?—still has the answer no, on the stated facts. The normative question—was an operative obligation violated at noon?—can have the answer no, because the obligation ended before the criterion time. Whether one should still call the canceled task a “task failure” depends on what makes that task token persist: a fixed outcome target may remain unachieved, while an institutionally constituted assignment may end upon valid cancellation. This ambiguity narrows the thesis; it does not support a universal temporal rule for the word “failure.”

## Strongest objection and reply

**Objection: possibility closure is the only important failure time.** Once every success route is gone, nothing remains for the deadline to add. The person and institution can act on the failure immediately; calling the outcome a failure at noon merely records a later administrative description. The distinction may be verbal, and it may be especially implausible when the responsible agent intentionally destroyed the only route.

**Reply.** The objection identifies a real and important time: closure time. It is the right time for claims about irreversibility, prediction, intervention, and possibly an earlier breach of a duty to preserve the route. The reply is not that nothing important happens at c. It is that practical importance does not make c the endpoint of every predicate. If a benchmark asks whether arrival occurred “by noon,” closure does not answer that question by making noon arrive early. The distinction matters in the cancellation case, in late-delivery cases, and whenever an alternate route reopens. If “failure” is stipulated to mean “point of no return,” then the objection wins for that stipulated sense; it no longer describes the narrower deadline-relative claim under discussion.

This response accepts the central Astra challenge: task failure and obligation violation cannot simply be assigned separate temporal profiles without specifying what the task and duty are. It also accepts the Sol assessment's concern that the topic is narrow and literature engagement is missing. The proposed disagreement is limited: cancellation is not a counterexample to the temporal distinction once the outcome criterion and the live obligation are tracked separately. It is a reason not to treat them as one thing. No claim is made that primary literature already endorses this analysis. On Sol's priority concern, I agree that the topic is narrow; I disagree that narrowness alone makes it trivial. If the distinction survives, it may prevent explanations of responsibility from confusing when intervention became futile with whether a stated deadline was met. That payoff is conditional and not yet shown to be broad.

## Scope, risks, and possible revision

The argument applies only where an outcome criterion has a specified endpoint and the alleged closure is robust against relevant later continuations. It does not yet establish a general account of omissions, negative events, causal responsibility, blame, or the semantics of future tense. A purely physical absence without a task or deadline may have a different temporal profile. A duty to maintain the possibility of performance can be breached at c even if the eventual performance failure is evaluated at d.

The position should be narrowed or set aside if the closest primary accounts already make the same distinctions and no further explanatory payoff survives; if ordinary deadline judgments do not support the distinction between an already settled prospective outcome and a not-yet-completed deadline interval; or if the distinction cannot be stated without silently fixing a task identity that cancellation itself removes. It should be revised if later routes, waivers, or criterion changes routinely undermine the proposed closure test. A source probe should compare the proposal with primary treatments of omissions or negative facts, temporal predication and future outcomes, and deadline-based obligations or cancellation. None has been checked here.

## Significance if correct

The modest payoff is a clearer vocabulary for explanations and responsibility. A cause can make a later nonachievement inevitable before the criterion time; that does not automatically make the cause and the deadline-relative result the same occurrence. This helps distinguish when intervention became futile, when a performance condition was unmet, and when a duty was breached. Those distinctions may matter in task evaluation and retrospective explanation, but their significance remains provisional until tested against close literature and ordinary cases.

## Relation to the selected idea

This proposal is a direct descendant of idea-092. It retains the contrast between earlier inevitability and later deadline-relative nonachievement, the sealed-delivery case, and the warning that obligation cancellation may change the analysis. It makes the implicit temporal claims more precise, rejects any unqualified reading on which “the failure” is one event with a settled time, and adds a three-part distinction among closure, criterion evaluation, and duty violation. It does not claim a new result in the literature or an established metaphysics of omissions.
