# Fair Lotteries Need Binding Acceptance Rules, Not Literally Unowned Seeds

## Argument proposal

### Question and thesis

When two people have equally strong claims to an indivisible benefit, what makes a lottery a fair way to decide between them? Is it enough that the implemented procedure gives each person the same probability of winning, or must the procedure also constrain who may decide whether a revealed draw counts?

I defend a narrower version of the selected idea. Equal ex ante chances are an important distributive condition, but they do not by themselves establish procedural fairness when an interested party retains unreviewable authority to make exceptions after seeing a draw. In the cases where a lottery is meant to replace discretionary ranking between equal claims, a fair procedure needs a rule that binds or checks this post-draw authority. The rule need not use a literally “unowned” seed, and fairness does not require that every claimant co-author every detail. Informed delegation, an impartial and answerable trustee, or another credible constraint can suffice.

This proposal does **not** yet establish the stronger claim that unequal procedural standing is unfair when every possible use of the authority is guaranteed to preserve all relevant chances. That status-only claim remains an open, central test. If a public, enforceable, outcome-neutral delegation removes the complaint, the thesis must stop there.

### Scope and distinctions

The target is fairness in allocating one scarce good between claimants whose claims are stipulated to be equal for the purpose of the case. It is not a general theory of randomness, risk, or every lottery. A lottery can be fair for several reasons: it may give equal chances, avoid ranking claims that cannot be distinguished, or make the decision less dependent on an allocator’s personal preference. These reasons can come apart.

I use **outcome neutrality** for equality of the final probability of each claimant receiving the good, conditional on the relevant information fixed by the mechanism. I use **acceptance authority** for power to decide, after observing a draw or a relevant history, whether its result is binding or must be replaced. I use **constraint** for a prospective rule, review procedure, or valid delegation that makes this authority answerable. Publishing a rule is not itself a constraint if the official can disregard it without review.

The thesis concerns the difference between a decision rule and the person’s unreviewable power to depart from it. It does not treat every physical rerun as suspicious. A public, symmetric rerun rule can itself be fair. Nor does it assume that a procedure is unfair merely because one official administers it. The question is whether an interested party can change which outcome binds after learning the result, without a rule that the other claimant can invoke.

### Argument

**P1.** When a lottery is offered to resolve equal claims, one central justification is that neither claimant has a relevant basis for being ranked above the other. The lottery replaces discretionary comparison with a procedure that treats the claims alike.

**P2.** Equal final probabilities can establish an important form of distributive equality. They do not, by themselves, describe who has authority to decide whether a realized result is final. Two procedures may have the same probability distribution while assigning different decision rights.

**P3.** If a claimant or an actor aligned with one claimant may make an unreviewable exception after observing a draw, that actor has a second-order decision right: the actor can decide which realized outcome is allowed to govern. This authority is distinct from the random device’s ordinary operation.

**P4.** Where the lottery is justified as a substitute for discretionary ranking, an interested actor’s unreviewable post-draw exception power reintroduces discretionary control at the point when the result becomes authoritative. A neutral-looking coin toss does not remove that control merely by having equal probabilities under the rule the official is expected to follow.

**P5.** A procedure that reintroduces such control is not procedurally fair unless the affected parties have validly delegated it or can hold the decision-maker to a public, contestable standard. Disclosure helps people understand the procedure, but disclosure alone does not make an announced rule binding.

**C.** Therefore, equal chances under an announced rule are not sufficient to establish procedural fairness when a party with an interest in the allocation can make unreviewable post-draw exceptions. A binding acceptance rule or a justified, answerable delegation is needed for this narrower class of lottery.

P4–P5 carry the main philosophical burden. They depend on a procedural conception of fairness: a fair lottery must do more than distribute chances evenly; it must avoid placing one claimant’s standing at the mercy of another claimant’s uncontrolled decision. That conception is plausible when the lottery is selected precisely because neither claim should be settled by personal discretion. It is not a theorem from probability alone. Someone who defines fairness exclusively by outcome distributions will reject it, and the proposal must defend the procedural premise rather than hide it inside the word “fair.”

### Worked case

Two claimants, A and B, have equal claims. Let a fair coin produce 1 or 0; 1 awards the good to A and 0 awards it to B. Under this simple rule, each has probability 1/2.

Now consider a published two-draw rule. Draw independent fair bits X and Y. If they differ, use X. If they match, discard both and draw a fresh independent fair bit Z. The rule treats the labels symmetrically and preserves equal chances:

- X=0,Y=0 occurs with probability 1/4 and produces A with probability 1/2 after the rerun: contribution to P(A) is 1/8.
- X=1,Y=1 likewise contributes 1/8 to P(A).
- X=1,Y=0 occurs with probability 1/4 and selects A: contribution 1/4.
- X=0,Y=1 occurs with probability 1/4 and selects B: contribution 0 to P(A).

Thus P(A)=1/8+1/8+1/4=1/2, and P(B)=1/2. This shows that an outcome-dependent discard or rerun is not automatically unfair. A public, binding symmetric rule can include post-draw acceptance decisions while preserving equality.

But suppose an official may make an exception after seeing X and Y. Under the stated rule, X=Y=1 is rerun. If the official instead accepts A on that history, P(A) rises by 1/8 to 5/8. The participants may know the published rule and still lack protection if the official has no reviewable duty to follow it. This case illustrates the limited claim: an equal-probability rule does not establish fairness if an interested actor can unilaterally depart from it after seeing the result.

The example does **not** settle the hardest version of the objection. The exception changes the outcome distribution, so a critic can say the problem is simply unequal chances. To isolate a purely procedural complaint, the next probe must construct a mechanism in which the decision-maker retains genuine post-observation authority while every allowed exercise is guaranteed to preserve all relevant conditional chances. If no such case yields a defensible complaint, the status-only extension should be rejected.

### Strongest objection and proposed answer

The strongest objection is that once the rule is fully disclosed, all relevant probabilities are guaranteed equal, and the parties may waive or delegate the procedure, no complaint remains. Any residual “status” difference may be merely symbolic. If A and B knowingly accept a neutral administrator who can select among outcome-neutral procedures, the administrator’s authorship need not make the resulting lottery unfair. If the claimed problem is only hidden bias, disclosure solves it; if the problem is a change in probability, the probability condition detects it.

The objection is right against a blanket claim that every sole administrator or every acceptance decision is unfair. The worked rule itself is a counterexample to that overbroad view: rerunning after observed outcomes can be fair when the rule is symmetric and binding. I therefore do not defend the original thesis as a universal demand for an “unowned” seed.

The proposed answer is narrower. Disclosure and equal probabilities are facts about what the procedure says and what outcomes it produces under that procedure. They do not alone establish whether an interested actor is answerable for departures from it. A waiver matters only if it is informed, voluntary, and made under conditions in which the parties have a genuine option to refuse or later contest the delegation. A role that is transparently delegated and enforceably limited may be fair; an announcement that leaves one claimant exposed to an interested official’s unchecked exceptions is different.

Still, this response does not prove that such unchecked authority is unfair when every actual exercise is independently constrained to preserve all relevant chances. On that stronger case, the answer may depend on whether fairness requires equal standing to contest rule application, or only acceptable outcomes and valid consent. I leave the issue open rather than claim that disclosure can never cure it.

### What would defeat or narrow the claim

The proposal should be substantially narrowed or set aside if any of the following succeeds:

1. A finite model shows that an interested party can retain meaningful post-draw acceptance authority, while a credible mechanism guarantees equal chances conditional on every morally relevant history, and the authority creates no defensible standing or contestability objection.
2. A source-grounded account shows that lottery fairness in the closest cases is exhausted by the distribution of outcomes, with procedural constraints serving only as evidence that the distribution is trustworthy.
3. A valid, informed, and genuinely optional delegation removes the alleged inequality even when the delegate has post-draw authority. This would make the thesis conditional on the absence of such delegation, not a general requirement of shared control.
4. The supposed distinction between “unreviewable authority” and ordinary authorized administration cannot be stated without assuming that all personal discretion is already unfair.
5. The closest existing literature already establishes the same distinction and scope. The contribution would then need to identify a defensible refinement or be rejected as not original.

Conversely, a surviving argument needs a case that isolates the procedural factor: equal conditional probabilities, full disclosure, no hidden policy, and a real asymmetry in who can make the result binding. It must explain why that asymmetry matters without relying on an unnoticed probability shift.

### Significance and originality status

If defensible, the distinction would help explain why “both people had a 50% chance” may not answer every fairness complaint about a lottery. It would also identify when a public commitment is doing substantive work: not as a ritual around randomness, but as a limit on an interested party’s power to reopen an allocation after seeing the result. The practical consequence would be modest and conditional: auditability, a binding rerun rule, or an answerable delegation may matter when a lottery replaces discretionary comparison.

No primary source was read for this expansion, and no literature or novelty check was performed. Originality remains **UNKNOWN**. The selected idea’s closest questions include theories of fair chances and lotteries, procedural equality, arbitrary or dominating power, public commitment, and consent to delegated decision procedures. These are targets for the next source phase, not claims that the proposal is new or that any named source supports it.

### Relation to the selected idea

The selected idea argued that fair randomization needs a publicly constrained rule for committing to the draw rather than equal probabilities alone. This proposal preserves that question and its contrast between outcome symmetry and authority over which draw counts. It narrows the thesis in two ways: the concern applies most clearly when an interested party has unreviewable post-draw exception power, and binding symmetric rerun rules or justified delegation can satisfy the procedural requirement. It leaves the stronger equal-probability/status case unresolved instead of treating it as proved.
