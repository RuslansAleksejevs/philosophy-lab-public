# When an Edition Narrows a Work’s Permissions

## Argument proposal — idea-085 descendant

### Question and claim

Can an edition become less faithful to an artwork by learning something true about its maker? Yes, if it turns evidence of a private preference into a claim that one publicly authorized version is the only version that can count as faithful. The claim is deliberately conditional: it concerns editions that present their classifications as claims about the work’s authorized performance range. It does not condemn an editor who clearly offers a recommendation while leaving the status of alternatives open.

The proposed contribution is an **authority-fidelity test**. Keep three questions separate: what alternatives the artist authorized; what the artist preferred; and what rule, if any, lets later evidence supersede that authorization. Evidence answering the second question does not answer the third by itself. An edition may therefore become more accurate about the artist’s preference while becoming less accurate about the permissions it attributes to the work.

Here “permission” is interpretive and work-relative: whether a performance is presented as eligible to count as a faithful realization under the relevant artistic practice. It is not legal permission, physical possibility, or a guarantee that every realization is equally good. The claim is about a critical edition’s representation of status, not about an editor’s power to prevent anyone from performing a version.

### The argument

1. A critical edition can do more than preserve documents. When it calls one version definitive and another superseded, it can assert that the former belongs to the work’s authorized realization range and the latter does not. Sometimes “canonical” is only an editorial preference; the claim below applies only when the label purports to settle faithful performance.

2. Suppose an artist deliberately releases two incompatible endings, A and B, as admissible alternatives, and neither the artist’s act nor the governing practice delegates later selection to editors. This establishes a public authorization status for both endings, subject to any valid later change. The premise does not assume that every published draft is authorized or that public facts always defeat private intention. The case stipulates deliberate joint authorization.

3. A later diary entry establishes that the artist privately preferred A. That is evidence about preference. It becomes evidence of revocation only if an additional bridge principle applies: for example, that any later private preference automatically changes the work’s authorized status, or that the artist validly delegated to editors the power to infer such a change. Neither bridge follows from the bare proposition “the artist preferred A.” Liking A more is consistent with continuing to accept B, regretting B without withdrawing it, or having no intention about the status of either ending.

4. Compare two editions that preserve the same endings and the same diary. Edition One reports the preference but continues to present A and B as authorized alternatives. Edition Two calls A the authentic final ending and marks B as outside the faithful work. Their archival evidence is the same; their status claims differ. Edition Two has not merely learned more. It has inferred a narrower permission set for the work.

5. If no valid supersession rule or revocation supports that inference, the narrower status claim is mistaken. The edition is then less faithful in its representation of authorization, even if it is more accurate about preference. This is an error in what the edition says the work permits, not proof that a performer will obey it or that the edition has physically altered the work.

So gaining true historical knowledge can accompany a loss of fidelity. The source of the loss is not knowledge. It is an unsupported move from a descriptive fact about the maker to a normative status claim about the work.

### Worked case and label test

Imagine a score that deliberately prints two incompatible codas, with an explicit note that either may close a performance. Years later an authenticated diary says the composer personally liked coda A better. A new edition includes the score and diary, labels A “the authentic ending,” and calls B “an abandoned alternative.” A conductor who chooses B can still physically perform it. The issue is whether the edition represents that performance as a faithful realization.

The label’s function can be tested by asking what it commits the editor to. If “authentic” means only “the editor recommends A,” and the edition plainly leaves B within the work’s authorized range, there is no authority-fidelity error. If it means that B no longer counts as faithful because A is the real ending, the edition has made a status claim. The test therefore separates three cases: a preference recommendation; a report that the artist preferred A; and a claim that the artist’s preference removed B’s authorization. Only the third requires a supersession warrant.

This answer takes seriously the Astra assessor’s attributed challenge: “When does a canonical label purport to settle permissible faithful performance rather than merely recommend one editorial reading?” The proposal cannot answer that by the word “canonical” alone. The edition’s explicit wording, its stated editorial policy, and the status it assigns to performances must determine which case it is. The Sol assessor’s concern that the sketch verified no sources also stands: this hypothetical identifies a logical distinction, not an established fact about any actual edition or artistic practice.

### Strongest objection and reply

A strong intentionalist may object that an artwork’s identity is fixed by the artist’s intentions, including private intentions. The diary could reveal that A was always the artist’s authoritative intention, while B was never genuinely part of the work. On that account the later editor recovers the work’s real status; public presentation is evidence, not authority. If a well-supported intentionalist theory already explains why preference is distinct from authoritative intention and derives the entire test, the proposed argument adds little or nothing new.

The reply is limited. A preference report and an intention to determine a work’s identity are different contents. The diary in the stipulated case records the first and says nothing about rescinding B, defining the work, or empowering editors. An intentionalist can defeat the case by showing either that the diary reveals a relevant constitutive intention or that the artist’s practice makes the preference itself status-changing. But that conclusion requires an account of what makes an intention authoritative and how it interacts with the deliberate public release; it cannot be obtained by relabeling every preference an intention. If private intentions can silently revise a work, the proposal must explain why that rule is justified and how editors can identify it without conflating preference with revocation.

This reply does not establish a new theory against intentionalism. The live possibility that a sophisticated existing view already contains the authority-fidelity test is the central novelty risk, not a gap to be hidden by a new name.

### Scope, significance, and possible failure

The argument applies only where the maker deliberately authorized alternatives, no valid delegation or later revocation is stipulated, and the edition’s label purports to settle faithful performance. It does not apply to an unfinished draft never offered as an alternative, a public later withdrawal, a delegated editorial choice, or a plainly advisory ranking. It also does not show that all artwork identity is created by public acts. It proposes a burden of argument for the narrower case: an editor who claims that an authorized option has lost its status must identify the rule or act that licenses the change.

If correct, the distinction matters because archival discovery can look politically and aesthetically neutral while shifting authority from maker to editor. Reporting a preference preserves evidence; declaring that it cancels a public permission allocates status. Separating those acts makes editorial reasoning answerable without treating an artist’s wishes as irrelevant or an editor’s judgment as forbidden.

The argument fails or narrows if primary accounts of the relevant practice show that a private preference of this kind automatically supersedes public authorization; if the diary actually records a valid revocation or constitutive intention; if the artist delegated final status to editors; if performances of B remain explicitly faithful under the edition’s own policy; or if the label is only a recommendation. Its originality fails if established intentionalist or editorial theories already articulate and defend the same distinction and test. In that event, the case may remain a useful application, but it should not be presented as a new general contribution.

### Next investigation

No primary-source or literature check was performed for this proposal. A later probe should read primary discussions of open or plural works, authorial intention, revision and critical-edition authority; compare their rules for preference, revocation and delegated choice; and inspect at least one actual editorial apparatus that retains alternatives and one that demotes them. The decisive question is whether any existing account already licenses the transition from private preference to exclusive faithful status, and what evidence it requires. Until that test is done, originality is unknown and the hypothetical remains conceptual.
