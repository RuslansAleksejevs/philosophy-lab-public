# Repairability and permission to assert

## Abstract

Can a speaker with fixed evidence be more permitted to make a categorical assertion merely because the communication channel allows a later correction? The original sketch suggests that reliable correction can change permission without changing knowledge. This proposal separates two questions that the sketch risks combining. First, is the speaker epistemically entitled, given the evidence available at the time, to present the proposition categorically? Second, all things considered, may the speaker perform the whole communicative act, including its foreseeable uptake and the duties that follow? Correction infrastructure does not by itself improve the speaker’s evidence for the proposition, so it cannot establish the first permission. It can affect the second by changing expected reliance, repair costs, and the speaker’s ability to discharge a duty to correct. A further, more ambitious possibility is that an assertion belongs to an updateable public record and that repairability changes the epistemic quality of that record. That possibility needs a distinct argument; it must not be smuggled in as a change in the speaker’s warrant. The proposal therefore narrows the original thesis while preserving a testable question about practice-level permission. No literature or primary source was checked for this expansion.

## Question and revised claim

The question is whether two speakers who have the same evidence for the same proposition can have different permissions to assert it categorically solely because one can reliably correct the assertion after publication.

The revised claim is conditional. A correction channel cannot, by itself, increase a speaker’s evidence-level entitlement to assert a bare proposition. It may change whether the speaker is permitted, all things considered, to issue the communication, and it may change whether the larger practice of asserting, monitoring, and correcting counts as a responsible contribution to an updateable epistemic record. The latter is a possible practice-level epistemic norm, not yet an established result. It concerns the act together with a correction undertaking, rather than a bare assertion considered only at the instant it is made.

This revision rejects the strong reading of the sketch if “epistemic permission” means that repairability makes an otherwise unsupported categorical assertion epistemically proper. It preserves a narrower research proposal: correction infrastructure may affect the norms governing communicative acts and shared records, while leaving the speaker’s initial warrant unchanged. If this difference reduces entirely to expected practical or epistemic costs, then the surviving result is about all-things-considered permission, not a new epistemic entitlement.

The scope is limited to an assertion made under uncertainty. It does not claim that correction makes a false assertion true, that a later retraction erases the initial assertion, or that a speaker may assert recklessly whenever a platform advertises a correction function. The issue is normative permission, not whether the speaker knows the proposition. “Same evidence” means the same evidence available to the speaker at the moment of assertion, with the same confidence, proposition, wording, audience, and information about the proposition. A correction channel that gives the speaker new evidence about the proposition is outside the core comparison.

## The permissions to keep apart

Three normative questions are relevant.

1. **Evidence-level propriety:** Does the speaker’s evidence meet the epistemic standard for asserting this content with this categorical force? This assesses the claim in relation to what the speaker knows, is justified in believing, or otherwise has epistemic warrant to present. Which standard is correct remains open.
2. **All-things-considered communicative permission:** Given foreseeable effects, obligations, and alternatives, may the speaker send this message in this situation? A speaker can fail an epistemic standard and still face a separate practical question about whether silence, warning, or qualified communication would be worse. Any permission here does not retroactively make the assertion epistemically proper.
3. **Practice-level record permission:** Is it permissible to add an assertion to a shared information practice that has rules for monitoring, correction, and visible repair? This question treats an assertion as an event in a record with a lifecycle. It could be epistemic in aim, but it may also be a prudential, moral, or institutional norm. The proposal does not assume its classification.

The distinction is not merely verbal. If a correction channel changes only expected downstream outcomes, the change belongs to the second question. If it changes the duties and public commitments constituting the record-making practice, it may bear on the third. Neither route shows that the speaker has better evidence for the proposition at the initial time.

## Argument

**P1.** Suppose two speakers make the same categorical assertion of proposition *p*, to the same audience, at the same time, with the same evidence and confidence. Their evidence about *p*, the truth of *p*, and the asserted content are fixed across the comparison.

**P2.** A fact about whether a message can later be corrected is not, by itself, additional evidence that *p* is true. Unless the channel changes what the speaker knows or justifiably believes about *p*, it does not change the evidential relation between the speaker and *p* at the time of assertion.

**P3.** If evidence-level propriety is determined by that evidential relation together with the applicable standard for categorical assertion, a change in the correction channel alone cannot improve the speaker’s evidence-level propriety. It might change a separate permission, but that conclusion requires a different premise.

**P4.** Communicative permission evaluates more than evidential support. It can depend on foreseeable uptake, the costs of leaving an error uncorrected, the availability of safer alternatives, and duties the speaker can actually perform. A reliable channel can therefore matter to all-things-considered permission even when P1–P3 hold.

**P5.** In a practice that treats public assertions as entries in an updateable record, a speaker’s ability and obligation to append a visible correction may be part of the practice’s rules. Whether this creates an epistemic permission, rather than only a practical or moral exception, depends on what makes the record norm epistemic. The mere fact that the channel is reliable does not settle that classification.

**C1.** Repairability alone cannot establish that a speaker is epistemically entitled to make a bare categorical assertion that falls below the relevant evidence standard.

**C2.** Repairability can still affect all-things-considered permission to communicate. It may also affect a distinct permission to participate in a correction-governed epistemic practice, but only if that practice-level norm is independently defended.

The inference from P1–P3 is limited but strong: an infrastructure fact cannot raise a person’s warrant for *p* unless it changes evidence, content, or the relevant epistemic standard. P4 supplies a separate route to a changed permission. P5 marks a possible further route without treating it as already proven. The argument thus preserves the selected idea’s contrast between fixed evidence and changing correction conditions, while locating the burden of proof on any claim that the changed permission is epistemic in the stronger, evidence-level sense.

## Worked case and discriminating comparison

Let *p* be “the east archive room is open.” Two staff members receive the same incomplete but credible report, have the same confidence, and send the same categorical sentence to the same group. The group initially relies on either message in the same way. In channel A, the speaker can attach a visible correction to the original message, and recipients will see it. In channel B, the original message cannot be edited by the speaker.

In the ordinary version of this case, A may reduce the time for which recipients retain a false belief or act on it. If that is the only difference, a changed permission is readily explained by expected harm and repair costs. That result would support a limited practical thesis, not the claim that the speaker’s initial assertion becomes epistemically warranted.

To test whether anything else is doing work, construct a cost-matched variant. In channel B, an independent moderator sends a correction to exactly the same recipients at the same time and with the same visibility as the speaker’s correction in A. Stipulate that initial uptake, final belief, practical consequences, and total time spent with the error are equal. In A, the speaker can repair the entry and is answerable for doing so; in B, the moderator repairs it and the speaker cannot alter the entry. The correction route, attribution of repair, and associated undertaking now differ while the stipulated audience outcome is held fixed.

If ordinary judgment still treats the speaker in A as more permitted, the difference may concern accountability or the constitutive rules of a shared record. That would motivate a practice-level account. It would not yet show that the speaker had greater warrant for *p*. If the permission difference disappears once outcome and costs are matched, the original contrast is explained by consequences or duties surrounding communication. If a difference remains only because recipients regard A’s channel as better evidence that *p* is true, the comparison has failed to hold audience evidence fixed and must be redesigned.

The example does not establish which verdict is correct. It makes the disputed variable explicit and offers a way to avoid treating a lower expected loss as proof of a changed epistemic standard.

## Strongest objections and proposed answers

### Objection 1: A knowledge norm leaves no room for repairability

If categorical assertion is epistemically permissible only when the speaker knows *p*, a speaker who lacks knowledge is not epistemically permitted to assert *p*, however reliable the correction channel. Repairability changes prudence or responsibility after speaking, not the epistemic status of the assertion. The proposal’s broader distinction may then add terminology without a new result.

**Answer.** This objection defeats the strong version of the original thesis unless that thesis offers an argument against the knowledge standard. The present proposal does not claim that repairability supplies knowledge. It accepts the objection’s consequence for evidence-level propriety and asks whether an independent, all-things-considered or practice-level permission can differ. That is a narrower conclusion. A future argument could still defend a practice-level epistemic norm, but it must explain why an updateable record’s epistemic quality is not just an expected-utility consideration and why the norm does not license reckless initial assertions. Until then, the knowledge-norm objection remains a live constraint rather than a refuted view.

### Objection 2: The entire effect reduces to expected harm

The correction channel matters because it lowers the duration or cost of error. That is ordinary prudential calculation above an unchanged epistemic floor. Once irreversible epistemic and practical costs are equalized, no permission difference remains. The proposal has no distinct epistemic contribution.

**Answer.** For the ordinary case, this may be right, and the proposal should concede it if the cost-matched comparison yields no residual difference. The cost-matched variant asks whether responsibility for an auditable correction matters even when audience outcomes are equal. A remaining judgment difference would identify an accountability or record-governance norm, not automatically an epistemic one. To earn the latter label, the account must show that visible correction improves a normatively relevant epistemic feature of the shared record, beyond minimizing expected loss, and must state why that feature changes permission rather than merely creating a duty to repair. If no such account is defensible, the thesis should remain about practical permission.

### Objection 3: The comparison changes the speech act

A speaker whose assertion comes with a correction duty may not be making the same speech act as a speaker whose message is final. The correction commitment could qualify the content or force of the utterance. If so, holding the utterance fixed while varying its channel is incoherent.

**Answer.** This is a serious boundary problem. The core comparison holds the sentence and categorical force fixed, while separately recording any public commitment imposed by the practice. If that commitment changes what recipients reasonably understand the assertion to mean, the case is not evidence that the same bare assertion has different permissions. It instead supports the more limited claim that a different communicative package—assertion plus update undertaking—may be permissible. The proposal must report that result as a change in the act’s institutional setting, not as a change in evidential entitlement to assert *p*.

## What would disprove or narrow the proposal

The proposed distinction should be abandoned or narrowed if any of the following tests fail:

- In cases where evidence, content, confidence, audience evidence, initial uptake, final beliefs, delays, and costs are matched, judgments about permission do not vary with the speaker’s ability or duty to correct. That would leave only the consequences-based explanation.
- The apparent effect disappears whenever the audience is prevented from treating correction availability as evidence about *p*. Then repairability may change perceived reliability, not permission at fixed evidence.
- The alleged practice-level norm cannot be stated without adding a practical-loss term, moral accountability term, or a change in the meaning of the utterance. Then it should not be advertised as a distinct evidence-level epistemic norm.
- A defensible account of categorical assertion establishes a fixed evidence threshold and the correction channel changes none of its relevant conditions. The strong epistemic thesis is then false for this case.
- The proposed correction route is unreliable, recipients cannot be reached, or correction is delayed beyond relevant reliance. If permission still rises merely because a channel is nominally labelled correctable, the account is too permissive and likely ignores actual repair capacity.
- The only cases supporting a residual permission difference involve different assertions, duties, or audience expectations. The conclusion must then concern those differences, not correction infrastructure alone.

Conversely, evidence for a practice-level norm would require a stable difference in considered permission under well-matched outcomes, an account of the relevant public-record duty, and an explanation of why that duty is epistemic rather than simply prudent or moral. A single intuitive example would not suffice.

## Significance if the narrowed claim survives

The limited claim would help separate two questions often compressed into “may the speaker assert this?” A person’s warrant can remain fixed while the permissibility of sending a message changes with its likely effects and the availability of repair. This distinction could make discussions of public communication more precise: it would explain how a communication system can create duties or permissions without magically improving the truth-directed evidence for a claim.

If a distinct record-level norm survives the cost-matched tests, the contribution would be more ambitious. It would locate some epistemic responsibility in the governance of public records: who can correct an entry, whether corrections remain visible, and who is answerable for updates. The view would still need safeguards against “correctable misinformation” as a blanket excuse. A correction duty could supplement an evidential threshold, not erase it.

The practical significance is conditional. This proposal has not shown that correction infrastructure changes epistemic permission, that any particular platform satisfies the relevant conditions, or that the distinction is original. It supplies a structured argument and a discriminating research plan for testing those claims.
