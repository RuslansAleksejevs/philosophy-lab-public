# A Foreknown Request Can Still Authorize a Response

**Status:** Expanded argument proposal, not a complete essay. **Literature and originality:** not checked in this stage.

## Abstract

A familiar challenge to petitionary prayer says that an omniscient listener already knows what a petitioner wants, so asking cannot add anything relevant. That inference moves too quickly from an epistemic claim to a normative one. This proposal argues conditionally that, for a temporally responsive agent governed by a norm of invitation or consent, an actual request can authorize a later response even when the agent infallibly knew in advance that the request would occur. Knowing that a future speech act will happen is not itself the speech act, just as knowing that consent will be given is not always equivalent to possessing consent now. The argument establishes only a possible noninformational role for petition. It does not show that God exists, that every divine conception is temporally responsive, that a request obligates an answer, or that prayer changes anyone’s welfare. Its central unresolved test is whether this distinction survives the best accounts of divine timelessness, perfect goodness, and petitionary prayer.

## Question and bounded claim

Can an actual petition make a normative difference to a perfectly informed listener when it supplies no new information about the petitioner’s desire?

The proposal’s answer is a qualified **yes**. Suppose an agent is temporally responsive and recognizes a legitimate norm under which a person’s actual invitation can authorize a form of involvement that would otherwise be intrusive. The agent may know with certainty that the invitation will be made, yet lack that invitation until the person makes it. Once made, it can permit a response under the description “answering this request.” The request need not change the agent’s beliefs, the material outcome, or the petitioner’s welfare for that relational description and permission to differ.

This is a conditional claim about a structure of agency. It is not a proof that a divine agent must be subject to human consent norms. It is also not a claim that asking creates a duty to grant what is asked. Permission, obligation, motivation, causation, information, outcome, and welfare are separate questions. The proposal is about permission and response-description; it leaves the others open unless a later argument connects them.

## The distinctions the argument needs

Four facts must not be collapsed:

1. **Epistemic fact:** the listener knows that a request will occur and knows its content.
2. **Speech-act fact:** the petitioner actually performs the request at a particular time.
3. **Normative fact:** under an applicable practice or principle, that performance confers permission to respond in a specified way.
4. **Outcome fact:** the listener performs an act and the world reaches some result.

The knowledge in (1) can precede (2). If the norm in (3) is constituted by the actual act in (2), knowing that the act is coming does not by itself make it current. And (3) need not alter (4): two histories may contain the same physical act and material result while differing in whether that act was invited. The last contrast is relational rather than material. It should not be treated as a demonstrated welfare difference; if relational respect is part of welfare, a later account must say so.

The view also distinguishes **having a reason** from **learning a reason**. A request might supply no new evidence and cause no update in belief, while still being a new normative fact to which a temporally indexed rule applies. The agent can foresee that fact without its obtaining early. This is the intended answer to the thought that omniscience makes every later event normatively present from the start. Whether this answer fits a timeless agent is a separate question, not a result smuggled into the distinctions.

## Argument

Let P be a person, A a listener, R P’s request at time *t1*, and X a response by A at a later time *t2*. A knows at *t0* that P will perform R at *t1*, and knows the request’s content. Assume for the model that *t0 < t1 < t2*.

**P1.** Some legitimate permissions are act-constituted: a person’s actual invitation or consent can confer permission that mere evidence of a future invitation does not yet confer.

**P2.** If a permission is act-constituted, knowledge that the act will occur is not identical to the act’s occurrence. The listener’s belief can remain unchanged across *t1* while the normative history changes when P actually performs R.

**P3.** A response can be governed by a temporally indexed policy: A may perform X as an answer to R after R occurs, while refraining from that form of involvement before R. The policy need not be adopted through uncertainty; it can be adopted at *t0* by an agent who knows that R will occur.

**P4.** Under that policy, R supplies no information to A, but after *t1* it makes a difference to whether X is permitted and to whether X is correctly described as a response to P’s request.

**P5.** Nothing in omniscience alone rules out an agent’s recognizing such an act-constituted permission or following such a conditional policy. Omniscience says what the agent knows; it does not, without an additional premise, say that all future permissions already obtain or that every known future act is normatively interchangeable with its performance.

**Conclusion.** Therefore omniscience alone does not entail that petition is normatively idle. It removes the request’s informational role for the omniscient listener, but it does not by itself remove a possible authorizing or relational role.

P1 is the substantive hinge. It needs defense from accounts of permission, consent, invitation, and speech acts; this stage has not supplied that literature. P5 is deliberately modest. The argument does not establish that an omniscient being exists, that an ideally good being ought to adopt this policy, or that a particular theological tradition accepts it.

## Worked case

Imagine a quiet room used by two people for a practice in which one may offer a short, welfare-neutral acknowledgment only after the other invites it. The listener is stipulated to know perfectly that the other will ask at 2:00, what they will ask, and that the invitation will not be withdrawn. At 1:59, the invitation has not yet been performed. The listener waits. At 2:00, the request is made. At 2:01, the listener gives the acknowledgment.

Assume the words and all material consequences would be identical if spoken at 1:59 or 2:01. The difference is that at 1:59 the listener has not yet been invited, whereas at 2:01 the listener is responding to an invitation that now exists. Perfect foreknowledge has removed surprise and information gain; it has not made the speech act occur earlier. If the practice’s rule is sound, the request changes permission and the relational description without changing the stipulated material outcome.

The case is not evidence about divine action. Its purpose is narrower: it supplies a coherent test model for the inference from “the listener already knows” to “the request cannot matter.” A critic can reject the consent norm, deny that permissions work this way, or argue that perfect goodness makes it inapplicable to God. Each response adds a premise; none follows from omniscience by itself.

## Strongest objection and proposed reply

The strongest objection is that the model’s temporal permission distinction has no application to God. A perfectly good omniscient agent already knows every desire and every future petition, has every reason that could bear on the petitioner, and chooses the best action. The request therefore cannot change the agent’s reasons, decision, or outcome. The consent analogy imports limitations governing finite humans—uncertainty, vulnerability, and risk of intrusion—into a being who has none. If the agent is timeless, there is no divine “before the request” and “after the request” in which authorization could arise.

The reply has two parts, and neither should be overstated. First, the objection does not refute the narrow modal claim that omniscience by itself is compatible with a noninformational role for a request. To reach that conclusion, it needs additional theses: that divine goodness eliminates the relevant permission norm; that a future-known act counts as presently performed or presently authorizing; or that a response relation requires a change in the listener’s belief or decision. The worked case challenges the last thesis and makes the first two visible as independent commitments.

Second, the objection may defeat the application to a specific conception of God. If perfect goodness means that no created agent’s invitation can alter the permissibility or relational form of divine action, the proposed route fails. If timelessness excludes any relevant event-indexed relation, the temporal argument cannot establish a timeless analogue. A defender would then need a separate account on which a timeless agent can eternally will an outcome *in response to* a temporal petition, with a defensible dependence relation that is not merely a label attached after the outcome is fixed. This proposal has not provided that account. Its reply therefore preserves a limited conclusion and leaves the theological extension at risk.

## Responses to the assigned assessments

The Astra assessor’s discriminating question is whether actual authorization can matter when the listener timelessly or infallibly knows that authorization. The argument answers only the infallible-foreknowledge, temporal-agent version: knowledge of a future act is not identical to its performance, so an act-constituted permission may arise at the act’s time. The timeless version remains unresolved and may narrow the conclusion further.

The Sol assessor emphasizes that perfect foreknowledge and perfect goodness may remove the timing difference, and that the human analogy may not transfer to divine action. Those are serious limits, not objections answered by relabeling the request “relational.” The proposal restricts its affirmative argument to a temporal model and treats the divine application as conditional. It does not infer novelty from the unusualness of the topic, and it reports no literature-based originality judgment.

The selection reason asks whether knowing a future request differs from having current authorization, requires a specified temporality and form of divine agency, and calls for an active search for prayer precedents. The proposal makes the first distinction its central claim, confines its model to temporal responsiveness, and records prayer literature as a required next test. It disagrees with any suggestion that a human consent example alone establishes a divine conclusion; that example establishes only a countermodel to the inference from omniscience alone.

## What would defeat or narrow the claim

The argument should be rejected or narrowed if a defensible account shows that all legitimate authorization is either reducible to the listener’s evidence of a person’s wishes or already in force whenever future authorization is infallibly known. It should also be narrowed if the norm in P1 applies only to agents with vulnerabilities that no proposed divine model can possess; if a request does not itself create permission without an independent prior convention; or if “answering” is only a causal description with no normative force. Finally, even if P1 and P5 survive, the conclusion about petitionary prayer does not follow for timeless or nonresponsive conceptions without an additional bridge argument.

## Significance if correct

The modest result would separate two objections often run together: “an omniscient listener learns nothing” and “a petition cannot make any normative difference.” The first does not entail the second. This leaves conceptual room for petition as an act of address or authorization, even where it is not a report of need. If the distinction cannot survive scrutiny, the failure would still identify which premise makes petition redundant: a theory of permission, divine perfection, temporal agency, or timelessness. Neither outcome would show that prayer changes external events or that a requested outcome is granted.
