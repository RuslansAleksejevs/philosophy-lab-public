# Safety without subjection: risk reduction and discretionary control

## Question and provisional claim

Can a policy be objectionable even when it lowers every person's probability of physical harm? Yes, if the improvement places access to protection under another agent's unaccountable discretionary control. The complaint is about the policy's governance relation: people may have safer prospects while their protection remains something an official can withdraw without a public rule, answerable reason, or appeal.

This is a defeasible reason to constrain or redesign a policy, not a proof that the safer policy must be rejected all things considered. Nor does it claim that lower probabilities are themselves a harm, that central coordination is always objectionable, or that the policy secretly raises expected physical harm. It distinguishes a risk profile from an authority structure. The argument is strongest against a decision procedure that evaluates safety only through probabilities and distributions of physical harm. A theory that includes standing subjection as a bad feature of outcomes can represent the concern within its outcome description; this proposal does not refute every form of consequentialism.

The key question is therefore conditional: once marginal and joint physical risks, expected misuse, and ordinary downstream effects are controlled, does unaccountable control over essential protection remain a reason for objection? The proposal treats that as a substantive normative premise to defend and test, not as a result already established by the sketch.

## Terms and scope

An **exposure profile** records each person's probability of a specified harm. A **joint risk distribution** records which combinations of people may be harmed together; it prevents a change in correlated catastrophe risk from hiding behind improved individual probabilities. A **control structure** records who may alter access to protection, under what rules, and with what duties to explain, hear challenges, or correct mistakes.

**Unaccountable discretion** is a practical power to change a person's access without criteria that constrain the decision and without an effective avenue to demand reasons or contest it. It need not involve malicious intent, frequent use, or a demonstrated increase in expected harm. Conversely, coordination by a central body is not enough: an authority governed by public rules, review, and effective appeal may lack the feature at issue.

The target is a policy governing basic protection from serious harm. The claim is about what makes a policy prima facie objectionable, not a complete decision rule for emergencies, resource allocation, or every form of delegated authority.

## Argument

1. A safety policy has at least two morally assessable features: the risks people face and the terms on which their protection is governed. A summary of individual probabilities omits joint risks; even a complete joint distribution of physical harms does not by itself specify who has authority to change a person's access.

2. Improving a person's exposure profile is a genuine benefit with respect to the specified harm. It does not entail that every feature of the policy is acceptable. That inference would require the further premise that only the specified physical-risk distribution matters.

3. When an official can withdraw essential protection without answerable rules, a person's secure access depends on that official's unreviewable choice. The affected person has a claim to protection only at the official's discretion, rather than an institutionally secured claim whose terms can be invoked and challenged.

4. That status is a prima facie moral concern. The case for this premise is that a person subject to another's uncontrolled power over an important interest lacks a standing assurance that the decision will track reasons they can contest. This remains so even if the official is benevolent and currently reliable: reliability describes a pattern or probability; accountability describes what the affected person may demand if the decision is challenged. This step is the argument's most contestable normative premise. It cannot be derived from probability arithmetic alone.

5. The concern is not automatically an extra expected physical loss. If the analysis already includes a probability of misuse, correlated failure, fear, lost options, or other consequences, those must be counted and not counted again under a relational label. The narrower residual claim concerns the standing control relation itself. A complete outcome theory may choose to encode that relation as an outcome; in that case the parties disagree about moral representation or value, not about the arithmetic of equal lotteries.

6. Therefore, lowering every person's physical-harm probability can coexist with a prima facie objection to the policy that delivers the reduction. The objection may support safeguards, a different allocation of authority, or—in some cases—rejection of the policy. It does not establish that the objection outweighs the safety benefit in every case.

## Strongest objection and proposed answer

The strongest objection is that the supposed relation either changes the complete outcome or makes no difference. If revocation is possible, its chance and consequences belong in the model. If preferences, contestability, and every downstream outcome are genuinely equal, there is nothing left to complain about. Calling the residue “subjection” would either double-count a harm already represented or simply assume the conclusion. The objection is strengthened by the possibility that centralization changes correlation or catastrophe exposure even while lowering each person's marginal risk.

The answer has two parts. First, the correlation point is correct and must be controlled before the example can support this argument. A comparison of marginal probabilities alone is inadequate. Second, the claim does not purport to force a complete-outcome consequentialist to rank two policies differently when that theory's complete descriptions are identical. Rather, it challenges the narrower inference from “every person's chance of this harm fell” to “the policy is therefore unproblematic.” If a theorist includes the standing authority relation in the outcome description, the relation can be represented there. If the theorist excludes it because only experienced or expected consequences count, then the theorist must defend that exclusion; the proposal's premise is that having one's basic protection subject to unaccountable choice can itself be a reason for institutional complaint.

This answer limits the ambition of the original sketch. It does not show that a moral residue survives after every normatively relevant fact, including the authority relation, has been stipulated equal. If “every downstream outcome” includes the person's status as subject to discretionary power, there is no difference left for this proposal to establish. The live dispute is whether that status is an independent feature of the policy or merely shorthand for other effects. If a defended account shows it is always reducible to those effects, the thesis should narrow to a warning about incomplete safety metrics.

## Worked case

Suppose a town replaces a decentralized rescue arrangement with a system that reduces each resident's chance of physical injury. Let the improved system's full joint distribution of physical injuries be **J**. Compare two ways to govern the improved system. Under **Rule**, access follows published eligibility rules, officials must give reasons, and residents can appeal. Under **Discretion**, one official may remove a resident from the rescue list without stated criteria, reasons, or appeal. For the comparison, stipulate that both systems have exactly distribution **J** for physical injury in the period under discussion; any difference in catastrophe correlation or expected physical misuse would defeat this isolation and must be analyzed separately.

Rule and Discretion then provide equal specified physical-risk benefits. Yet Discretion leaves each resident's access dependent on a choice they cannot challenge. A resident may reasonably object to that term even if the official has never used the power and no extra probability of injury has been established. The objection concerns the residents' standing in the scheme, not a claim that their lottery has secretly worsened.

The stipulation is deliberately demanding. If the official's future use is possible and that possibility changes the injury distribution, Discretion is also riskier and the example no longer isolates the relational point. One must not use that altered case as evidence for a residual beyond risk. The clean comparison instead asks whether a difference in entitlement and answerability matters when the specified physical lottery is held fixed. Someone who insists that the entitlement itself is an outcome may redescribe the case in outcome terms; that move clarifies the disagreement but does not settle which description is normatively adequate.

The case also shows why safeguards matter. If a public rule, independent review, and appeal remove the uncontrolled power while preserving distribution **J**, the relational objection is answered without giving up the safety improvement. If safeguards make the rescue system substantially less safe, the resulting choice requires a direct comparison of benefits, rights, and feasible alternatives. This proposal supplies no automatic priority rule.

## What would defeat or narrow the claim

The claim should be rejected, or reduced to a point about measurement, if a sound account shows that unaccountable control over protection has no normative force except through changes in expected outcomes, preferences, agency, or other consequences—and those are all equal in the controlled comparison. It should also be narrowed if the only persuasive examples turn on hidden future misuse, changed joint-risk distributions, fear, or lost options.

The argument would gain support if a carefully matched case preserved all specified physical risks and ordinary consequences while a defensible account of entitlement or non-arbitrary authority still explained why affected people may demand constraints. It would not be enough that readers dislike the official or feel anxious. The test must identify what claim the residents have, what makes the official's discretion unaccountable, and why safeguards answer the complaint.

## Significance and unresolved originality

If the distinction survives, safety evaluation should report both exposure and governance: marginal risks, joint risks, who can change access, which rules constrain them, and whether affected people can obtain reasons and effective review. This avoids treating lower physical risk as a complete answer to concerns about institutional control. The practical result may be a design requirement—secure entitlements and review—rather than a blanket rejection of risk-reducing coordination.
