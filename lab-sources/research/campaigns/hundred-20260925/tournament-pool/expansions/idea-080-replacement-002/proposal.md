# Retraction after the person’s death

## Argument proposal

### Abstract

Can an institution owe a retraction to someone whose life has ended, when no posthumous welfare, distressed relatives, or practical benefit to living people is at stake? I argue that a narrow case supports such a duty. When an institution authoritatively excluded a person on false grounds and still presents that judgment as legitimate, the institution is continuing a relation of unjustified power. The original breach generated a claim to fair treatment; death can end the person’s capacity to experience a remedy without automatically discharging the institution’s liability for the breach. A fitting retraction therefore does more than correct a datum or protect institutional credibility: it acknowledges and withdraws the institution’s wrongful judgment of this person. The argument depends on a contestable persistence claim about remedial duties. It does not establish general posthumous rights, and it remains open whether a sufficiently rich account of institutional integrity can explain all the same reasons.

### Question and scope

Suppose a public tribunal wrongly declares that a member of a community was properly excluded for misconduct. The declaration was made while that person was alive, rested on false evidence, and denied the person standing within the institution. The person later dies. Decades afterward, the institution finds conclusive evidence that its stated grounds were false. No relative is distressed, the person cannot experience a correction, and we stipulate that no living person’s interests or conduct will change as a result. The institution nevertheless continues to display the verdict as valid.

The question is not whether an archive should be accurate, whether institutions should protect their reputation, or whether every false statement about a dead person is unjust. Those questions can supply reasons for correction without establishing justice owed to the person. The narrower question is: **does the institution owe this person a retraction because it wrongfully exercised authority over them, even after their death and apart from benefits to anyone?**

The claim is conditional and limited. If an institution used authoritative power to impose an exclusionary judgment on a person, the person had a claim to fair treatment at that time, and the institution now continues to endorse the judgment as legitimate, then it has a pro tanto duty of directed reparation to that person. A complete retraction must identify the judgment as unjustified, withdraw its continuing authority, and acknowledge that the institution wronged the individual. This argument neither assumes that dead people retain every ordinary right nor settles whether all claims survive their holder’s death.

### The argument

The proposal turns on a distinction between the wrong’s **origin** and its **unfinished repair**. The wrongful exercise of power occurred during the person’s life. The proposed posthumous duty is not a new wrong to a newly conscious subject. It is a later duty to answer an earlier breach that remains institutionally unaddressed.

**Premise 1 — An authoritative exclusion is an act toward a person, not just a sentence about them.** An institution’s verdict can determine who is admitted, heard, trusted, or recognized under its rules. It purports to settle the person’s standing and to authorize how the institution treats them. Its content is propositional, but its force is also practical and relational: the institution uses its authority on a particular person.

**Premise 2 — A false verdict can wrong the person independently of later effects.** In the case at issue, the grounds were false and the decision unjustified when issued. The wrong is not merely that the institution held a false belief. It failed the person’s contemporaneous claim to a fair exercise of authority. The case stipulates away lost income, later distress, and other welfare effects so that this claim is not inferred from them.

**Premise 3 — The breach creates a remedial claim whose existence need not track the person’s later ability to receive a benefit.** The person’s claim arose when the institution exercised power over them. The institution’s liability arose with the breach. If the victim’s death automatically erased every unfulfilled remedial claim, an institution could escape the duty to answer a serious wrong simply by waiting until its subject could no longer experience repair. That consequence is not a proof that claims survive death, but it gives a reason to distinguish the end of a person’s welfare from the discharge of an obligation already generated toward them.

This is the argument’s bridge premise and its most exposed point. It treats directedness as grounded in who was wronged and what claim was breached, not in the current ability to demand or feel the remedy. A reader who holds that every directed claim requires a living present claimant will reject the bridge. The proposal should make that disagreement explicit rather than smuggle it in through the word “reparation.”

**Premise 4 — The institution remains a continuing answerable agent.** Individual officials may have died or left office, but the institution that made and preserves the verdict continues. It can still decide whether the judgment is authoritative, preserve an accurate history, and issue a correction. A change of personnel does not by itself settle the institution’s outstanding liability.

**Premise 5 — Continued endorsement renews the institutional posture.** An accurate archive may preserve the fact that a verdict was once issued without affirming that it was justified. But a record presented as a valid verdict still performs a current act: it asks the audience to treat the exclusion as authorized. Once the institution knows the grounds were false, continuing that endorsement repeats the exercise of authority in a form it cannot defend. The present duty to withdraw the endorsement follows even if all consequences for living people are stipulated away.

**Premise 6 — A fitting repair must answer the particular wrong.** A bare data correction may say only that the evidence was inaccurate or that the institution’s files have been updated. That can meet an accuracy duty. It need not acknowledge that the institution unjustifiably excluded this individual or restore their standing in the institution’s own account. Because the original wrong was a judgment of a person under institutional power, repair should name the wrong and its subject, not merely the institution’s error.

**Conclusion.** In the stipulated case, the institution has a pro tanto duty to issue a person-specific retraction. The action need not improve the deceased person’s welfare. It answers an unremedied claim generated by the institution’s treatment of them and stops the institution from continuing to represent its exclusion as legitimate.

The “owed to the deceased” language marks the direction of the original claim and the repair; it need not imply that the deceased can now hear, feel, or enforce it. Still, the persistence claim is substantive. If directed duties cannot outlast the claimant’s life, the proposal may support only a duty about the deceased, grounded in truth or institutional responsibility. Whether that weaker description is enough is a central unresolved issue.

### Strong objection and proposed response

The strongest objection is the one raised by the Sol assessment: after present institutional integrity and effects on living people are fully counted, no distinct duty to the deceased remains. The institution should retract because a public body must stop making a false, unjustified claim and must maintain trustworthy records. Once it does so, nothing is owed to a person who no longer exists as a welfare subject or addressee. Calling the correction “justice for the dead” adds a label but no reason.

The Astra assessment identifies the same explanatory gap in a pointed form: what makes the retraction owed to the deceased rather than a general duty to cease current false or unjustified public claims? Its suggested test is to compare a false record without authoritative exclusion to one that maintains exclusionary authority. The root selection reason likewise chose this proposal despite Sol’s deferral because continuing endorsement may isolate a current institutional act from the person’s ended welfare history; it asks that the expansion explain what directed justice adds to truthfulness or institutional integrity.

The proposed answer is a **remedy-content test**. Hold fixed the audience, factual correction, institutional credibility, and all effects on living people. Compare two statements. First: “Our file was inaccurate; we no longer rely on this verdict.” Second: “We wrongfully excluded this person; the evidence did not justify our judgment, and we withdraw our claim that the exclusion was legitimate.” The second does something the first need not do: it acknowledges the individual as the object of the institution’s breached obligation and repairs the status judgment made against them. Truthfulness can require that the institution say what happened. By itself, however, a duty to be truthful does not explain why the institution must acknowledge a wrong done to this person rather than report only that its prior evidence was defective.

This response is not decisive. An account of institutional integrity may require precisely this person-specific acknowledgment because a trustworthy institution must recognize whom it wronged. If so, it can explain the same action. The proposal’s answer is that such an account has either incorporated a relational duty of repair or shown that no additional ground is needed; the label alone cannot decide which. The philosophical test is whether the full account can explain why an error about an authoritative exclusion requires withdrawal and acknowledgment while a neutral clerical error requires only correction. If no difference in reasons or required action survives that comparison, the directed-justice thesis should be narrowed or abandoned.

A second objection challenges Premise 3 more directly: rights grounded in interests or present claim-making capacity cannot persist once their holder is dead. The response is not to assume a general right to posthumous welfare. It is to treat the remedial duty as a liability generated when both parties existed and the claim was breached. The institution’s present act can discharge that liability even though its original addressee cannot experience the discharge. Whether this is a coherent account of a surviving directed claim, rather than a metaphor for present institutional virtue, remains open.

### Worked case and scope tests

Let Nara be a community member whom a licensing tribunal excludes after falsely finding that she falsified an application. The tribunal’s order is authoritative and remains in the public record as a valid exclusion. Nara dies years later. A later inquiry establishes that the alleged evidence was fabricated. For the controlled case, no family member knows about the verdict, no living applicant is influenced by it, and correcting the record yields no measurable improvement in trust or institutional reputation.

The institution should preserve an accurate historical account that the tribunal issued the order. It should not erase the event. But it should mark the order as unjustified, withdraw its current endorsement, and acknowledge that Nara was wrongfully excluded. Merely changing a field from “valid” to “under review” would leave the central status judgment unsettled. A retraction need not pretend that Nara can receive compensation or that the past can be undone. Its point is to stop the institution from continuing to claim that its exercise of power over her was legitimate.

Four contrasts delimit the claim:

1. **Innocent factual error.** A record gives Nara’s birth month incorrectly but carries no accusation or authority over her standing. Correcting it is a matter of accuracy. This proposal does not infer a separate directed-justice duty from every false record.
2. **False accusation without authoritative exclusion.** A non-authoritative note falsely accuses Nara of misconduct. Other duties may arise, especially if the accusation harmed her while alive. The proposal does not settle all such cases. It predicts the clearest residual duty where the institution used the accusation to deny standing or impose exclusion.
3. **Expired and repudiated verdict.** The institution has already stated that the verdict was wrongful, withdrawn its authority, and corrected the person’s status while retaining a clearly labeled historical record. The central repair has been made. Continued storage of the historical fact alone does not renew the wrong.
4. **Currently endorsed verdict.** The institution knows the basis was false but still presents the exclusion as legitimate. This is the core case: an earlier person-directed wrong remains unacknowledged and the institution still performs its authority in the same direction.

These are not settled intuitions or empirical findings. They are controlled comparisons for testing whether the proposed distinction changes what the institution owes, beyond the demand for accurate and credible records.

### What would defeat or narrow the claim

The argument should be abandoned in its strong form if the required retraction and acknowledgment remain wholly unchanged when the record contains no authoritative exclusion, accusation, or denial of standing. That result would suggest that the case is fully explained by truthfulness or institutional integrity. It should also be abandoned if no coherent account can explain how the remedial claim survives its bearer’s death without assigning the deceased a present welfare interest by stipulation.

The thesis should be narrowed if its case depends on a living audience, family distress, future institutional trust, or present practical consequences after all. Those may be important reasons to retract, but they do not establish the intended no-benefit claim. It should also be narrowed to unjustified institutional decisions, rather than all false official judgments, if only power-backed exclusion creates the relevant person-directed relation. Finally, if an integrity account can explain the exact need for person-specific acknowledgment without presupposing any surviving claim to repair, the proposal may retain the practical recommendation while dropping its distinctive directed-justice explanation.

### Significance and remaining research

If the argument succeeds, it gives a limited account of posthumous justice without relying on the claim that the dead continue to experience harms. It distinguishes preserving a historical record from endorsing the exercise of power recorded there. It may clarify why the remedy for an unjust exclusion is not just a better database: the institution must answer the person it wronged, even where no compensatory benefit can reach them.

No primary sources or secondary literature were searched or read for this proposal. The argument has no verified originality claim. A later source stage should examine work on posthumous harm and rights, directed duties to the dead, reparative and corrective justice, institutional responsibility across changes in personnel, and the authority of public records. It should test whether existing accounts already distinguish a surviving remedial claim from continuing welfare interests, and whether theories of institutional integrity can derive person-specific acknowledgment without a directed duty. Until those questions are checked against exact sources, this is a candidate argument for investigation, not an established novel contribution.
