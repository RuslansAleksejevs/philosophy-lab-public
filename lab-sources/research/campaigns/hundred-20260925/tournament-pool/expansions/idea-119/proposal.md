# The Evidence Custodian’s Role in Repair: A Duty Without Inherited Blame

## Abstract

Can an institution owe cooperation in remedying a historical wrong even when it neither committed that wrong nor inherited liability for it? This proposal gives a narrow answer. A present custodian of evidence can acquire a role-based duty to preserve and provide fair access to records when the records are materially necessary to assess credible claims of repair and the custodian controls a non-substitutable route to them. The duty is directed to claimants as claimants and to a fair remedy process; it is not a judgment that the custodian is guilty or must pay for the original harm. The claim does not say that every holder of relevant information owes a special reparative duty, or that custody always creates a larger burden than an equally capable helper’s duty of aid. Custody matters because it places a particular remedy-enabling act within the institution’s control. If another agent can provide the same access at the same cost and with the same authority, the custodian-specific part of the claim weakens or disappears. No primary literature has been checked for this expansion, so its originality and the adequacy of its conceptual distinctions remain open.

## Question, claim, and scope

The question is whether control of indispensable evidence can ground an obligation to cooperate in repair independently of blame for the wrong and liability to compensate for it.

The revised claim is: **An institution that presently controls materially relevant, non-substitutable evidence of a credible claim of historical wrong has a proportionate, role-based duty to preserve the evidence and make it available through a fair process capable of assessing and supporting remedy.** The duty arises from present control over a remedy-enabling condition, not from inherited guilt. It may be satisfied by secure independent review, justified redaction, or other safeguards; it does not entail unrestricted public disclosure. Its burden depends on relevance, reliability, privacy, safety, cost, legal constraints, and available substitutes.

This claim is narrower than the initial sketch. Mere possession of records does not trigger it. There must be a credible claim, a meaningful connection between the records and the claim, and a real possibility that access can improve the process of establishing or implementing a remedy. The proposal concerns cooperation and access, not compensation for the underlying wrong. Causal involvement, knowing acquisition, a public undertaking, or a successor relationship may add duties, but none is necessary to the minimum custodial duty argued for here.

Three forms of responsibility must remain separate. **Blame** concerns culpable conduct. **Underlying remedial liability** concerns who owes compensation or other repair for the original wrong. **Remedy-enabling responsibility** concerns who now controls an act or resource needed for a fair repair process. The third can exist without establishing either of the first two. Calling it reparative identifies the duty’s object and direction, not inherited culpability. If that terminology predictably confuses the distinction, “remedy-enabling custodial duty” is the safer name.

## Argument

1. **A credible claim of wrong can require a fair route to assessment.** A claimant cannot be asked to establish a claim through evidence that is unavailable to them when another institution controls the only material record. This premise does not assume that the claim is true. It requires a fair way to assess it.

2. **A custodian with effective control has a distinctive power over that route.** The custodian can preserve, destroy, disclose, restrict, or arrange review of the records. An outsider who lacks access or legal authority may be able to fund assistance but cannot perform those same acts. The claim concerns actual control, not the abstract fact of institutional wealth.

3. **Control over a non-substitutable condition of remedy grounds a role duty.** When an institution’s present role gives it control over a necessary step in a fair process, its duty is to manage that step responsibly. The duty is not inferred from guilt for the past. It follows from the institution’s current power over whether a justified process can proceed.

4. **The duty is owed in a repair-directed form.** The relevant obligation is not simply to improve someone’s welfare by any available means. It is to preserve and provide fair access to evidence that bears on a claim of wrong, so the claim can be assessed and appropriate repair considered. That directed object determines what counts as compliance: a charitable payment by the custodian does not substitute for preserving records if access remains blocked.

5. **The duty is limited by proportionality and safeguards.** The custodian need not disclose irrelevant, unreliable, or dangerously identifying material without protection. A fair process can use redaction, confidentiality, independent review, notice, or a restricted evidentiary channel. If compliance costs are excessive relative to the records’ relevance, or if the proposed access creates serious unmanageable harm, the duty may narrow or be discharged through a safer alternative.

**Conclusion:** A non-blameworthy institution can owe a specific duty to support repair through responsible control of indispensable evidence. The duty’s source is its present remedy-enabling role; its object is fair access to a repair process; its limits track evidence quality, cost, privacy, safety, and substitutes.

## What custody adds in comparison with an equally capable helper

The assessors’ central challenge is correct: if the custodian’s duty is no stronger and changes no required action beyond attaching the word reparative, the proposed distinction is idle. The answer cannot be that custody always produces a greater total burden than an equally wealthy outsider’s duty of aid. This proposal makes a more limited comparison.

Suppose an outsider has equal wealth and can pay for counsel, research, or secure processing, but cannot access or authorize use of the records. That outsider may owe aid under whatever general duties apply. The custodian, however, owes the specific act of preserving the records and arranging justified access. A payment from the outsider cannot itself discharge that act. The difference is therefore in the content, direction, and priority of the obligation, not necessarily its cost or moral weight. The holder has first responsibility for the controllable access step; outsiders may have supplementary duties to help with burdens the holder cannot reasonably meet alone.

This is a defeasible role distinction, not a claim that property title creates moral privilege. If an equally capable helper gains the same effective control and authority over the relevant records, that helper acquires the same role duty. If an independent copy, public archive, or less costly route makes the custodian’s records substitutable, the custody-based priority narrows. In that case, any remaining obligation may be ordinary aid or another duty, and the distinctive custodial claim should not be maintained just to preserve the original label.

The proposal therefore partly disagrees with any reading of the initial sketch that treats exclusive custody by itself as sufficient or promises a stronger duty than ordinary aid in every comparison. It agrees with both assessors that the equal-helper test is decisive. Astra’s exact challenge asks why custody gives more than heightened rescue by a uniquely placed helper; Sol states the broader question of whether the duty is distinctively reparative rather than ordinary aid to equally capable outsiders. The reply is that the custodian’s required act is different and claimant-directed, but the proposal does not yet show that all theories of ordinary role-based aid would recognize this as a separate category. That classification remains an open conceptual test, not a settled result.

## Worked hypothetical

Imagine a public archive that lawfully receives the only surviving set of agency records relevant to a credible community claim that past property seizures were unjust. The archive did not participate in the seizures, and no fact establishes that it is the agency’s legal successor. The records contain personal details about claimants and third parties. A well-funded foundation, equally capable of financing lawyers and technical staff, has no access to the archive’s files.

On the proposal’s account, the archive’s minimum duty is to preserve the records, assess a properly supported request, and provide a fair access route. It might appoint an independent reviewer, redact third-party identifiers, allow claimant representatives to inspect relevant portions under confidentiality rules, and retain a record of exclusions. It need not post the files online or decide the claim’s merits. The foundation can fund representation or secure review, but cannot satisfy the archive’s access duty by spending the same amount of money elsewhere. If the archive refuses every review route, it obstructs a condition of fair assessment even though the foundation is equally wealthy.

Now vary one fact: a complete and trustworthy duplicate becomes available at a public institution that can provide the same review at comparable cost and with comparable safeguards. The archive’s special priority to provide access diminishes. It may retain ordinary preservation duties, but the argument for a custodian-specific repair duty is weaker because its records no longer control the remedy route. A second variation—evidence that the archive knowingly acquired the records to frustrate claims—would add a different basis for responsibility and must not be treated as necessary to the initial duty.

## Strongest objection and proposed answer

**Objection.** The argument converts an ordinary duty of rescue, public-spirited aid, or professional stewardship into a “reparative” duty without changing its normative content. Anyone able to help claimants may have the same moral reason to help. The archive’s possession is an accidental fact; treating it as a special relation risks smuggling the predecessor’s guilt onto an innocent holder. Worse, privacy risks and disputed claims make a duty to open archives dangerous.

**Answer.** The proposal accepts much of the objection’s warning. Custody does not establish guilt, and it cannot justify unfiltered disclosure. The duty is limited to fair, proportionate access, and an equally situated controller would bear it too. But the duty’s content is not simply “give more aid”: it requires the controller to preserve and govern access to the particular evidence under its control. The recipient is not only a person in need; it is a claimant seeking fair assessment of a claim against a wrong. That difference can affect which action discharges the duty, who has priority to act, and what counts as an adequate remedy process. If a theory of ordinary rescue already derives precisely those same role-specific and claim-directed requirements, then the proposal may have identified an application of that theory rather than a novel moral category. The next investigation must test that possibility instead of assuming a distinctive label settles it.

## What would narrow or defeat the claim

The custody-based claim should be rejected or narrowed if any of these conditions holds:

- A reliable substitute provides the same evidence through a fair process, so the institution’s control is not materially non-substitutable.
- The requested material has no meaningful bearing on a credible claim, or the claim lacks a fair basis for proceeding.
- Privacy, safety, or legal constraints cannot be managed by a proportionate alternative such as independent review or redaction.
- The costs or institutional burdens are so high that the proposed act is not a fair responsibility of the custodian; a public allocation or shared-cost process may then be required.
- The supposed role-specific duties are fully explained by ordinary stewardship or rescue principles, with no different recipient, content, priority, or discharge condition. In that case, “reparative” adds no distinct duty and should be dropped.

Evidence of complicity, succession, a prior undertaking, or knowing obstruction could support additional duties, but these are separate routes. They must not be used to rescue the minimal control-based claim when its own argument fails.

## Significance and unresolved research

If correct, this account allows an institution to cooperate in repair without first settling whether it inherited a predecessor’s blame or financial liability. It could clarify why evidence holders have responsibilities that wealthy outsiders cannot discharge for them, while preserving safeguards for people named in archival records. Its practical contribution would be a structured way to assign access work, not a presumption that every historical claim is valid or every record must be released.
