# A limit on using silence as evidence after conceptual reform

**Probe outcome: NARROW.**

When a reform is defended partly on the ground that it improves deliberation,
agreement or a decline in objections is defeasible evidence for that claim. The
evidence is weakened when defenders have reason to think that affected people
could not state foreseeable objections in a form the process would recognize and
answer. The alternate route need not retain the old word or definition. A reform
may still be justified by independent evidence about its effects, and the
objection route does not confer a veto.

This proposal is limited to the evidential role of silence or agreement in claims
about deliberative success. It does not say that every conceptual reform must
preserve every old category, that every possible objection must be anticipated,
or that a policy fails whenever communication is imperfect. The relevant
objections are those that challenge the reform’s stated reasons or identify
reasonably foreseeable costs within its announced scope. The scope boundary must
itself be open to challenge, though this probe has not supplied a complete
procedure for setting that boundary.

The source packet gives strong neighboring accounts. Haslanger evaluates
conceptual and terminological choices in light of their purposes and political
context. Fricker analyzes how structural gaps in shared interpretive resources
can prevent experience from being understood or included in a decision. Young’s
limited opening excerpt warns that narrow discussion norms can silence groups.
Plunkett and Sundell show that disagreement about concept use can carry
object-level stakes. These connections make the broad route requirement look
like a close application of existing work.

The narrower inference remains plausible: where a procedure’s available forms
make foreseeable objections difficult to state, recognize, or answer, the
absence of objections cannot by itself confirm that the procedure improved
deliberation. The present search does not establish that this inference is new.
The idea should be treated as a possible targeted application, not a novel
principle, until a fuller comparison with deliberative theory and epistemic-
injustice literature is completed.
