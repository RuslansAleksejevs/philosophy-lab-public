# Promises after personal branching: uptake, not automatic inheritance

**Status: NARROW.**

When one promisor branches into psychologically continuous successors, branching does not itself deliver the promised object. But the present source base does not establish that the original promisee-directed obligation transfers to one or more nonconsenting successors. Psychological continuity and practical control identify possible agents who could perform; they do not by themselves explain why those agents owe this promisee anything.

A successor can become answerable to the promisee by expressly taking up the outstanding performance after branching. An individual successor may give a fresh assurance to the promisee. Alternatively, multiple successors may openly join a shared undertaking whose terms identify one performance and specify that its completion discharges that undertaking. Scanlon’s account can explain the promisee-directed duty in the first case; Gilbert’s account may explain a duty grounded in an actually expressed joint commitment in the second. Any limit on burden, release, or discharge must follow the terms and a defensible account of fairness.

This narrower rule is not a theory of inherited promissory responsibility. Without post-branch uptake, the status and bearer of the original claim remain unresolved here. Neither automatic cancellation nor automatic transfer follows from the assigned sources. The earlier proposal’s “qualifying successor” test—continuity, understanding, and control—is therefore insufficient, and its shared burden cap has no settled allocation rule.

The rule concerns only one specified, transferable performance. It does not cover a promise whose point is that the original promisor personally act, a harmful performance, or an arrangement in which one successor’s assent is treated as consent by another. A single delivery discharges a successor undertaking only if the undertaking itself adopts that one delivery as its object; there is no basis here for imposing duplicate performance or for silently merging separate duties.

The surviving point is modest: personal branching need not settle every moral question by settling numerical identity first, but a directed successor duty needs an independent normative basis. In the tested cases, express uptake supplies a possible basis; mere psychological continuation does not. Whether an unperformed original promise remains enforceable against any branch is an open question, not a conclusion of this proposal.
