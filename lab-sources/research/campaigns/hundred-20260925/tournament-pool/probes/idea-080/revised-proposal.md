# Retraction after a person's death: a narrower claim

## Outcome: NARROW

### Revised claim

When an institution learns that an authoritative exclusion rested on false and unjustified grounds but continues to present the exclusion as valid, it has a present duty to correct the record and withdraw the exclusion's current authority. A bare note that the evidence was inaccurate is insufficient if the institution still treats the exclusion as legitimate. An accurate correction may need to name the person and state that the institution wrongfully excluded them.

This claim applies even if the person has died and no living person's interests or conduct are affected. The reason established here is that the institution is still making or maintaining an unjustified authoritative claim. The institution must stop doing so. The argument does **not** establish that this duty is a distinct claim owed to the deceased, or that a remedial claim survives its holder's death independently of welfare, preferences, and capacity to demand repair.

### Argument

1. An authoritative exclusion is both a record and an exercise of institutional power over a person's standing.
2. If the institution knows that the stated grounds were false and unjustified, continuing to mark the exclusion valid is a current, indefensible institutional act.
3. Truthfulness requires correcting the false basis. Institutional integrity requires withdrawing the status judgment the institution can no longer justify; leaving it formally in force while correcting only the evidence would preserve the central false claim.
4. A correction should identify the affected person where needed to specify which decision is withdrawn and what the institution did. Whether this acknowledgment is independently owed to that person after death is not established.
5. The institution can retain an accurate history that the tribunal issued the exclusion while clearly marking it invalid and repudiated. Preservation of the historical event is compatible with withdrawal of its present force.

Thus, the institution has a current duty to correct and withdraw its endorsement. This is a duty about the institution's present record and authority. On the available evidence, it should not be redescribed as a proven posthumous duty owed to the deceased.

### Scope

The claim does not cover every false clerical entry or every neutral historical record. It concerns a currently maintained authoritative exclusion that the institution knows was unjustified. A non-authoritative false accusation may require retraction and perhaps person-specific repair for reasons not settled here. An already repudiated decision retained only as clearly labeled history does not need a second retraction.

### Remaining question

A stronger directed-justice account could hold that an unjustified exercise of authority creates a person-specific claim at the time of the breach and that the claim is neither waived nor discharged by death. That response is coherent as a proposal, but this probe could not defend its persistence principle or show that it adds a reason or remedy beyond truthfulness and institutional integrity. Stemplowska's preference-based account offers a distinct possible route to posthumous mitigation, but it does not establish this broader claim. Corrective-justice literature and the full institutional-apology literature remain insufficiently covered.
