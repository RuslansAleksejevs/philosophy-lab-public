# Sources note: idea-111

Stage: sources-001. This is a bounded source investigation of the selected
proposal “Optional Signals, Unequal Burdens: A Narrow Duty to Supply Cover,”
not a finished essay, independent critique, or completed novelty review.

## Search target

The proposal asks whether someone who accepted a governance role in an optional
disclosure practice may owe a low-burden repair when the ordinary response
pattern makes a legitimate confidential exception identifiable. The assigned
tests make the decisive source questions:

- whether inferential or group privacy literature already covers exposure
  created by other people's predictable responses;
- whether role-based responsibility can add anything to general easy aid; and
- how a limited privacy claim can coexist with ordinary inference, research,
  other people's speech, and reasonable burdens.

The source choices came from those assigned questions. Searches also tried
terms about opt-out signals, refusal patterns, and cover behavior. Those
queries did not return a close philosophical primary text about a group
coordinating truthful refusals to obscure an exception.

## What the sources establish for this comparison

Rumbold and Wilson develop a direct philosophical account of privacy duties
concerning inferences from public information. Their examples include a person
who deliberately publishes genetic data without intending a medically trained
reader to infer a private diagnosis. They argue that the inference or its
dissemination can still wrong the person in some circumstances; they also
recognize limits where inference is unavoidable and where other moral reasons
justify disclosure. This is a close antecedent to the proposal's general
inference-and-confidentiality problem. The portions read do not assign a duty
to co-maintainers to change a response convention or recruit cover refusals.

Loi and Christen distinguish privacy within a group from inferential privacy
of individuals exposed through features shared with a broader set. They reject
an absolute right against inference but leave room for a limited right bounded
by unreasonable costs and limits on others. Their conclusion says that users
of analytics have responsibility to consider the social impact of knowledge
generation. This directly overlaps with the proposal's defeasibility and
responsibility conditions. Their discussion concerns inference and analytics,
not an optional group disclosure routine or a truthful cover refusal.

Rawls's practice account distinguishes judging a practice from judging an
action defined by it. It helps explain how a governance position can acquire
role-specific content, but it does not establish that any particular
disclosure practice is fair or that its administrators owe this proposed
repair. The practice must be justified on its own terms.

Hart's p. 185 passage offers a possible analogue for reciprocal duties arising
from a rule-governed joint enterprise when some participants accept
restrictions and others benefit. The indexed excerpt is too narrow to settle
whether a disclosure co-maintainer fits Hart's conditions. It also points to
a missing specification in this proposal: exactly what restrictions, benefits,
and reciprocal terms make the co-maintainer's role a genuine cooperative
arrangement?

Singer's low-cost prevention principle gives the rival account a direct
starting point: an agent may have a duty to prevent serious harm even without
a special role, when doing so costs little. The excerpt supports testing the
role-removal counterfactual, but does not discuss privacy, disclosure, or
whether cover is an effective intervention.

Hardimon's article appears to be a close primary source for the general theory
of role obligations, but only its metadata was accessible. No claim about its
argument is made here.
