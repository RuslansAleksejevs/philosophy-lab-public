# Independent critique of “What the Envoy Can Still Choose”

## Recommendation: revise, retaining the narrow interpretation

The essay reconstructs the Zi Gao episode as separating an assignment that a minister cannot choose from communicative acts over which an envoy retains some control. Its strongest evidence is the passage's sequence: service is described as inescapable and without choice of business; the ensuing advice then names expressive excess, departure from instructions, and pressing for a settlement as things the intermediary should avoid. This supports a restrained claim about constrained agency in the scene. The essay is also right to deny that faithful transmission vindicates the mission's end.

The main problem is the step from control over an act to responsibility for its contribution. The passage's own reasons are prudential and procedural: exaggeration loses belief and endangers the messenger; pressure and departure from instructions imperil negotiations; a bad settlement cannot be changed. Those reasons show why the envoy's conduct matters causally and to his safety. They do not show that the text treats him as morally answerable for harms caused by the mission, or that it advances an ethic of complicity. The essay repeatedly disclaims that stronger claim, yet its opening question, “responsibility” language, and closing contrast with the “easy excuse” of delegated speech invite precisely that inference.

This is the strongest objection, and it is repairable by defining the essay's use of “responsibility.” A critic-originated distinction may help: an intermediary can be the controllable causal source of an avoidable escalation, and so answerable for competent or faithful performance of a role, without the text assigning moral blame for the mission or its downstream harms. The essay should either defend that limited, role-relative sense of answerability or use “agency,” “control,” and “causal contribution” instead. The first option is more philosophically interesting, but the passage alone cannot establish it as a moral doctrine.

## Argument and rival reading

The argument is clearest when stated as follows: (1) the envoy does not select the mission; (2) the passage nevertheless directs him to regulate how he conveys it; (3) those communicative choices can alter trust and negotiation outcomes; therefore, lack of authority over an end does not erase all control over the means. This is a plausible reading of the translations. It does not establish the further normative conclusion that the envoy is blameworthy, guilty, or obligated to refuse a harmful order. The essay mostly recognizes this boundary and should make it explicit in its thesis and final paragraph.

The strongest rival says the passage is only counsel for self-preservation and stable negotiation. That rival is not defeated by observing that the messenger could exaggerate or urge a settlement: practical advice often identifies controllable conduct without attributing moral responsibility for the broader outcome. Giles and Legge both foreground danger to the intermediary, and the passage's closing appeal to resignation or Destiny reinforces its prudential frame. The essay can still argue that the passage makes the intermediary's causal role visible, but should present this as its own limited reconstruction, not as an implication that the text rejects the “empty instrument” view on moral grounds.

The essay should also identify the target of its closing phrase “easy excuse.” It has not established that anyone actually excuses all delegated speech, and it has not shown that this episode refutes such a general position. A more defensible ending would say that the scene distinguishes control of an assigned end from control of some features of its transmission, while leaving moral permission and blame unresolved.

## Textual accuracy and translation limits

The broad sequence is supported by both supplied translations. Giles says a subject serves his prince “without reference to the act but only to the service”; Legge says “without reference to or choice of the business.” Both then prohibit expressive overflow and departure from instructions, and both warn against urging a settlement. The essay fairly notes the safety-centered rationale and correctly treats Legge's introductory remarks as translator framing rather than ancient evidence.

The proposed three-way taxonomy is less secure than the essay presents it. Giles's note says that “travel beyond your instructions” is literally “urge a settlement,” whereas Legge translates the two as separate clauses. Without the classical text, these renderings cannot establish that the passage itself sharply distinguishes three independent acts. The taxonomy may be useful as the essay's analytic organization, but label it translation-dependent and avoid the categorical claim that the prohibitions are “not interchangeable.” The Chinese source was not available in the checked packet, so no lexical conclusion can be certified.

Two local phrases also need repair. “Changing the order” sounds like changing the sequence of an order; neither checked translation says that. If the intended point is changing the sender's instructions, write that. “Retaliation” overstates the supplied evidence: Giles describes resistance and both sides giving way to passion, and Legge describes quarrels. “Conflict” or “escalation” is safer. These are repairable wording and precision issues, not reasons to set aside the essay.

## Literature positioning and novelty

The essay's novelty claim is appropriately modest in one respect: it does not claim that scholarship has overlooked the Zi Gao scene. The proposed contribution is an interpretation of how unavoidable service and regulated speech sit together. That is a plausible candidate contribution, but the essay has not yet shown that this distinction adds something beyond familiar accounts of constrained agency, role obligations, or prudential political counsel. It should explain the payoff in one or two sentences and distinguish the textual interpretation from the broader conceptual lesson.

The assigned source packet did not include the cited secondary works. I did not verify the claims about Kwek, Lai, Rathnam, or the SEP entry, and this critique should not be treated as confirmation of their coverage. The manuscript itself describes those works as addressing adjacent themes, not the same messenger question. Keep that comparison explicitly provisional unless the author can point to the relevant passages and explain the difference. Do not present “the narrower Zi Gao question” as novel in the literature merely because the cited works discuss different episodes or broader political questions.

## Concrete revision requirements

1. State the thesis in terms of constrained control or causal contribution. If retaining “responsibility,” define it as role-relative answerability and distinguish it from moral blame for the mission's end.
2. Keep the prudential rival central. Explain why the passage supports the limited interpretive distinction even if it does not establish a moral theory; do not claim that personal control alone settles responsibility.
3. Mark the three-part taxonomy as an analytic reading of Giles and Legge. Note Giles's gloss connecting “travel beyond instructions” to urging a settlement, and avoid claiming a Chinese lexical distinction that was not checked.
4. Replace “changing the order” with “departing from the instructions” if that is the intended meaning; replace “retaliation” with the weaker language supported by the translations.
5. Remove or substantiate the “easy excuse” foil. End with the bounded textual claim and keep the question of obedience to an unjust mission open.
6. Keep the originality claim conditional and clarify what the interpretation adds. The current evidence does not establish a literature gap.

No fatal textual defect was found in the narrow reading. The primary weakness is an unresolved inference from pragmatic control to responsibility, compounded by a translation-sensitive taxonomy and an unverified literature comparison. Revision is warranted; setting the candidate aside is not.
