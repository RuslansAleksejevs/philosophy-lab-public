# B source work, sources-B-001

Research began only after the neutral shared question and this stage had been prepared. The sole philosophical input was `research/campaigns/hundred-20260925/debate-02/QUESTION.md`. The question distinguishes receipt/understanding from acceptance and reasonable reliance, and asks whether the promisee has a directed claim. Search work followed that opposition rather than consulting A’s materials.

## Selected primary texts and actual reading

- **David Hume**, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, 3.2.5, “Of the obligation of promises.” The full section (paragraphs 1–15 and notes 77–78) was read from Hume Texts Online’s edited transcription. Exact retrieved HTML bytes are saved as `hume-treatise-3-2-5.html`. Hume argues that promissory obligation depends on conventions and signs that support social cooperation, not a freestanding act of will. His historical conventionalist account does not settle the modern question about a promisee’s directed right. The word “accept” in paragraph 13 should not be treated as a developed theory of uptake.
- **David Owens**, “A Simple Theory of Promising,” *The Philosophical Review* 115(1) (2006), 51–77. The full article was retrieved from Owens’s own site and read through a text extraction of the exact PDF; both PDF and extraction are retained. Owens grounds promising in a recipient’s authority interest and argues that a promise is valid only if accepted. He says silence is often treated as consent when promises are normally welcome, but a recipient can refuse the authority and make the promise invalid. This is the closest direct support for B, while his tacit-acceptance qualification is a limit on a strict communication test.
- **T. M. Scanlon**, “Promises and Contracts,” in Peter Benson (ed.), *The Theory of Contract Law: New Essays* (Cambridge University Press, 2001), 86–117, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511570834.004. A third-party scan of the volume was retrieved; only pp. 86–96 were extracted and read, including Scanlon’s account of due care, loss prevention, assurance, and Principle F. The original full-volume PDF is retained; the chapter extract is labeled as an excerpt. Scanlon’s assurance account is a serious objection to requiring actual reliance: an addressee can have reason to want a stable assurance even without making plans or suffering reliance losses. However, Principle F also requires that the promisor know the addressee wants assurance and intentionally provide it under mutual knowledge. That is closer to uptake than to mere comprehension. This source is about moral promise obligations and contract law; legal enforceability is outside the assigned scope.
- **Abraham Sesshu Roth**, “Intention, Expectation, and Promissory Obligation,” *Ethics* 127(1) (2016), 88–115, DOI 10.1086/687333. Selected publisher text was read via web extraction, with the exact bounded excerpt and a paraphrase saved in `roth-web-excerpt.md`. Roth argues that acceptance helps secure obligation, protects the promisee’s autonomy, and cannot be reduced to hearing or expecting a statement. His own account is stronger than B requires: it ties acceptance to an intention for the promisor to perform and has trouble with accepted assurances where the promisee expects failure. The present opening retains the neutral definition of communicated assent and does not adopt Roth’s intention theory. Direct local retrieval of the publisher and PhilArchive/PhilPapers PDF endpoints returned HTTP 403; the article is therefore excerpt-level, with no exact original bytes locally saved.

## Selection and limits

Owens and Roth were selected because they directly connect acceptance to the promisee’s authority and directed claim; Scanlon supplies the strongest assurance-based challenge; Hume supplies historical context for the role of conventions and public signs. Together they are enough to develop a defensible opening but not enough to establish exhaustive originality or settle all accounts of promising. The novelty assessment is intentionally modest and is preserved in `novelty-gate.md`.
