# The Promisee’s Option: Why Some Promises Bind Before Uptake

A person can promise another a benefit without asking that person to do anything in return. If the promise is clear, voluntary, deliberately addressed to its recipient, and understood, does the recipient already have a claim to performance? I argue that it does when the recipient need not perform a task, accept contact, or enter a relationship, and there is no reason to think the promise is unwanted. The claim is defeasible: the recipient may release it, but owes no reply, monitoring, or exercise of it. Acceptance is needed when performance would enlist or burden the recipient. Reliance may add protections, but it is not the source of the basic claim in this narrow case.

This answer does not infer that the promisor is morally free to withdraw whenever the recipient has no claim. A promise may remain morally significant even if its recipient lacks release authority. The question is who holds that authority. My answer is that an understood, person-directed undertaking normally gives it to the addressee when doing so imposes no meaningful burden.

## 1. What receipt changes

The question separates three things that are easily conflated. Understanding is recognizing that the speaker has undertaken to act. Acceptance is communicating assent to that undertaking. Reliance is reasonably changing one’s plans or acting because of it. None entails either of the others. A recipient may understand a promise and remain silent; may accept without changing plans; or may rely before there is time to answer.

The question also concerns a specific moral position: a claim held by the promisee against the promisor, which the promisee may release. This is stronger than a reason for the promisor to keep their word. It identifies a person who may insist on performance and can waive it. The fact that these ideas are distinct is important. The promisor’s duty to perform does not logically entail that a particular person holds a releaseable claim. But the absence of logical entailment leaves a moral question: what rule should govern a clear promise deliberately made to someone?

In the ordinary no-burden case, the best rule is that the promise itself identifies its claimant. When I say, “I promise to leave the ticket for you,” I do not merely announce a private resolution or forecast my future conduct. I undertake a particular performance to a particular person. If that person understands the undertaking, the communication has succeeded. The recipient’s silence does not make them responsible for my choice; it leaves them free not to exercise the option I have created.

That rule is not a magical power of private will. Hume’s account of promising emphasizes the role of a public convention: a recognizable practice gives certain signs a moral function that a private resolution could not have by itself (Hume, T 3.2.5). The present claim likewise depends on a shared practice in which an understood, person-directed undertaking makes the promisor answerable to the addressee. Hume helps explain why public meaning matters; his account does not settle whether this particular duty is held by its recipient. That further rule must be defended on its own.

## 2. A limited option, not a duty to participate

The strongest reason to require uptake is that a claim gives its holder authority. The promisee may insist on performance and release the promisor. Someone could reasonably object to being placed in that role, even if the promised item is useful. An unwanted gift can be intrusive; a promise can be an unwelcome overture or a way of imposing continued contact. It would be wrong to treat silence as permission in such cases.

But not every claim imposes a task or relationship. A recipient who is offered a ticket they need not collect, answer about, or discuss can simply ignore the undertaking. They do not become responsible for reminding the promisor, checking progress, or declaring a release. The promised performance remains the promisor’s responsibility. The claim gives the recipient one limited option concerning that performance; it imposes no reciprocal duty.

This matters for the autonomy objection. The promisor does not conscript the recipient into carrying out an act. The promisor undertakes the act themself and identifies the person for whose sake it is promised. A recipient’s right to refuse is protected by allowing them to reject or release the standing. Requiring a communicated acceptance in every case protects a different interest: it makes the recipient’s power depend on a further act, even though the recipient has not been asked to do anything. Where that power is not itself meaningfully burdensome, the added condition needs justification.

The boundary must include the standing itself, not only practical chores. If the promisor knows the recipient dislikes being made a promisee, or the circumstances make the promise an unwanted approach, I would require acceptance. The same applies if performance requires the recipient’s cooperation, creates ongoing contact, brings public attention or liability, or otherwise places them under a meaningful burden. “No task” is not enough if the promise still imposes a relationship. The claim is automatic only where the offer is genuinely nonintrusive and the recipient has not rejected it.

This is a defeasible default, not a claim that every recipient welcomes every unsolicited assurance. It assigns the limited option in the ordinary case and lets refusal defeat it. Silence is not construed as acceptance; it does not express a preference. Rather, silence leaves in place the consequence of the promisor’s own deliberate act. The distinction is like the difference between making someone responsible for a task and giving them a benefit they may decline: the latter need not be accepted before it can be available.

## 3. Does the promise itself give the addressee authority?

The strongest opposing view says that self-binding and giving another person a power are separate acts. The speaker can choose to undertake a duty, but that unilateral choice cannot by itself make the recipient the person who controls whether the duty remains in force. On this view, acceptance gives the recipient authority by choice; substantial reliance gives them a special stake; and mutually understood assurance can give them a claim even without practical reliance. Understanding alone does not establish that the recipient wants the authority.

This objection correctly rejects a shortcut: one cannot infer “the recipient has a claim” merely from “the promisor has some duty.” Nor does the promisor’s choice alone prove that the recipient values being a creditor. My argument is instead about the form and direction of the promise. A public undertaking addressed to one person is not simply self-legislation witnessed by that person. It says that this act is being undertaken for them. The practice of promising can therefore assign both the duty and its limited release power as the ordinary consequence of this kind of communication, while still allowing recipient-side circumstances to defeat that consequence.

The recipient’s authority is not an independent prize imposed from outside the promise. It is the recipient-side aspect of a duty undertaken to them. If the promisor’s obligation is genuinely owed to this person, that person’s ability to waive it is a natural part of the relation. If the obligation is instead owed to no one in particular, the theory must explain why the promise was deliberately made to this addressee and how the recipient can release a duty that is not theirs to release.

The opposing view can answer that a duty may be owed to a person without giving them authority to waive it. That is conceptually possible, and sometimes moral duties have no private holder with a power of release. But a promissory obligation is unusual in being intentionally directed. In a no-burden promise, withholding the recipient’s release power does not protect them from an assigned task; it leaves them unable to dismiss a commitment made in their name. A theory that treats the recipient as the person to whom performance is owed, yet denies their power to waive it absent a further act, must explain why that is the more respectful default.

The promisee’s power to release also answers a concern that the status itself might be unwelcome. A clear refusal can end the claim. This does not mean that the recipient must accept first and then perform the work of release. It means the promisor has made the promise, and the recipient can decline the resulting option without owing an explanation. If the situation is one in which even that default standing would impose a meaningful cost, the case falls outside the rule and requires uptake.

## 4. Acceptance, reliance, and assurance

David Owens gives a strong account of the opposing view. His 2006 article distinguishes a communicated undertaking from a prediction and argues that a promise requires acceptance; it also discusses the authority a promisee gains and the difference between refusing a promise at the outset and releasing it later (Owens 2006, 51–54, 68–73). The view explains why acceptance can matter: a promisee may not want the role or the power that comes with it. Owens also recognizes that, in ordinary welcome cases, silence may function as consent. Under the question’s stipulated definition, however, silence is not communicated assent. I do not rely on redescribing it as acceptance. I defend a different default for the narrower no-burden case: uptake is not required because the recipient is given only a usable option and no duty to take it up.

The difference between reliance and the claim to performance also matters. A reasonable change of plans can create a powerful reason to protect the promisee. It may generate duties to warn, prevent loss, or compensate. But a change of plans does not itself tell us whether the promisee’s remedy must be performance, whether a substitute would suffice, or when reliance is substantial enough to create a full releaseable claim. The debate did not settle those thresholds. In the case at issue, the promisor’s undertaking itself explains why this recipient may insist on the promised act; reliance can strengthen the case without being a precondition.

Scanlon’s Principles D and L help distinguish duties to prevent or repair harm from a duty to perform the promised act. Principle L can be satisfied by warning or compensation; that does not necessarily fulfill the promise. Principle F supplies a distinct assurance route. In the pages read here, Scanlon makes it depend on the promisor’s aim to give assurance, knowledge that the recipient wants it, and mutual knowledge of the relevant intentions. When F applies, the recipient has grounds to insist; the recipient’s practical plans need not already have changed (Scanlon 2001, 97–99). This is an important challenge to a strict reliance requirement. It does not establish that bare comprehension is always enough, since its assurance conditions include a mutually known interest in being assured. I accept it as an additional route without treating it as proof of the ticket case.

These distinctions keep the argument modest. A recipient can acquire a claim through acceptance or through some assurance arrangement even where a simple no-burden default does not apply. Reliance can generate separate protections. But the existence of these routes does not show they are exhaustive. In the narrow case, the direct undertaking itself is a reason to assign the claim to the person it addresses.

## 5. What remains after the debate

The exchange sharpened the issue. The uptake advocate withdrew the claim that every reasonable plan change automatically warrants a full performance claim, because the threshold and the remedy remained unclear. The advocate also accepted that Scanlon’s assurance case may generate a claim without practical reliance, and that an understood promise can remain morally significant even when its recipient lacks a claim. The receipt advocate accepted that a no-task promise may still impose an unwanted relational status and withdrew the inference that no claim means the promisor is morally free to withdraw.

Those points rule out two easy arguments. I cannot say that every promise binds because otherwise breaking it is morally costless; the promisor may remain answerable without a promisee-held claim. And I cannot say that “no task” alone proves the recipient welcomes the standing. My position instead is a default rule for promises that are clearly addressed, understood, optional, and nonintrusive. In that case, directedness is supplied by the public undertaking to this person, while the burden limit protects against using silence to enlist someone in a relationship.

The remaining question is normative, not merely verbal: should the promise itself make the addressee the holder of the releaseable claim? I answer yes in this limited class. It treats the promisor’s deliberate choice as morally consequential without assigning the recipient a duty to respond. It also gives the recipient control to refuse the claim rather than requiring them to accept authority before they can waive it. Where the standing or performance would burden them, the default gives way to consent.

This conclusion is a defended position, not a proof that one complete theory of promising has been established. A broader account would need to test whether the proposed boundary can reliably distinguish optional benefits from unwanted relations, and how recipient refusals should be expressed in ambiguous cases. The source reading here was selective. Hume does not settle the contemporary claim question; Owens presents a serious alternative; and Scanlon’s F principle supports a different conditional route. The argument does not establish that the limited receipt rule is new. It establishes only that the burden and authority objections do not, by themselves, show that acceptance or reliance must precede every promisee’s claim.
