# Trust That Cannot Renew Itself

## Abstract

An expert can reasonably decide which objections a nonexpert should ignore. Can the expert also decide which objections to its own continuing authority deserve attention? A blanket prohibition would make useful dependence impossible. A blanket permission would let an authority protect itself from correction. I distinguish the warrant for relying on a source now from the warrant for renewing that reliance. Silence can support renewal only to the extent that the process producing it would behave differently under the relevant failure. A quiet record may therefore confirm reliability, provide no confirmation, or count against it. Where an initial warrant is time limited and monitoring supplies no new support, continued deference can lose its justification without any particular adverse report. The appropriate alternative to unrestricted personal checking is often bounded delegation with discriminating review.

## 1. A reasonable decision with an unreasonable extension

A research association employs a specialist service to assess technically difficult claims. Its members have good grounds for thinking the service more dependable than their own amateur assessments. They also know that many public criticisms are confused. They therefore allow the service to filter complaints as well as answer questions. This arrangement saves attention and improves their judgments.

At each annual meeting, the association asks whether it should renew the arrangement. The service reports that it has encountered no credible evidence of a serious problem with its work. The members are willing to withdraw trust if a credible warning appears. None appears, so they renew. They regard each quiet year as a successful test of the arrangement.

Something could be wrong here even if the initial appointment was justified, most rejected complaints were mistaken, and the members would sincerely reconsider if shown a problem. The service controls which potential problems become recognizable warnings. Their willingness to revise may have no effective connection with its loss of reliability.

But something could also be entirely right. A competent service might use an excellent internal audit. Outsiders might be much worse at detecting its failures. An insistence on personally investigating every complaint would sacrifice the very advantage that justified delegation. Control of a review channel, by itself, proves neither dishonesty nor epistemic failure.

The question is therefore not whether an authority ever judges its own performance. It is what entitles someone to take the resulting record as a reason to continue relying on it.

I defend a limited answer. When continued reliance depends on the absence of adverse signals, that absence must be assessed against the process generating the signals. It cannot renew trust simply because the authority labels the surviving record satisfactory. Renewal may instead rest on an initial warrant that still suffices. But where that warrant has a justified limit, an uninformative record cannot indefinitely replace it.

By a warrant I mean an epistemically adequate basis for a specified degree or form of reliance. I do not assume that reliance requires certainty, that all warrants are consciously articulated, or that a single numerical threshold defines knowledge. The target is a particular transition: from justified dependence at one time to purportedly justified dependence later. The argument concerns agents who have evidence about the relevant possibilities of failure and review. It does not establish that every deceived person is blameworthy.

## 2. What is already known

Three established discussions constrain the argument. Nguyen distinguishes exclusion through missing information from exclusion through manipulated trust. He also explains why agreement selected in advance cannot simply be counted as fresh corroboration, while recognizing the necessity of epistemic dependence and the possibility of appropriate distrust of outsiders. Those insights already rule out identifying healthy inquiry with unrestricted exposure. [Nguyen 2020, §§1–2](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2018.32).

The problem also resembles bootstrapping. A process cannot straightforwardly establish its reliability by judging its own outputs correct on the same unchecked basis. Yet Cohen emphasizes that the problem is not every use of a faculty in establishing something about that faculty. A demand for complete independence would exclude ordinary learning about perception through perception. The present argument likewise concerns a specific failed route to support, not a prohibition on all self-assessment. [Cohen 2005, pp.425–427](https://fitelson.org/seminar/cohen.pdf).

Most directly, Bondy examines inferences from not having heard something, drawing on Goldberg's account of epistemic coverage. Such inferences depend on whether the information would have reached the hearer. Bondy applies this point to information threatening an institution or group: the very interests that make the information significant may make its suppression likely. The claim that filtered silence can be misleading is thus no novelty here. [Bondy 2026, §§3 and 5](https://informallogic.ca/index.php/informal_logic/article/download/9706/6263).

My contribution connects that problem with the renewal of epistemic authority. It distinguishes a currently sufficient basis for trust from supposed additional support supplied by a clean record, shows how different review practices give that record opposite evidential roles, and explains when this difference imposes a limit on continuing delegation. Its central components are familiar; the proposed account concerns their joint consequences for warranted dependence over time.

This question is separable from whether an expert's judgment replaces or supplements a novice's reasons about the subject itself. Dormandy argues that authority need not require abandoning independently supporting reasons, and distinguishes proper authority from weaker epistemic superiority. Either side of that debate can accept that expertise has conditions. The issue here is how the continuing satisfaction of those conditions is assessed. [Dormandy 2018, §§2–4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-017-9913-3).

## 3. Three meanings of a clean record

Consider an authority that is either currently dependable for the relevant reliance, R, or not dependable, F. These are alternatives within a specified domain and interval, not timeless descriptions of its character. P(R) represents confidence that the service meets a specified reliability standard; it is not the percentage of its answers that are true. Let N be the observation that no error notice or adverse review finding has reached the person relying on it. Such a notice need not by itself warrant abandoning the service. Let B include the initial evidence and what is known about the review process. Then ordinary conditionalization gives:

P(R | N, B) / P(F | N, B) = [P(R | B) / P(F | B)] × [P(N | R, B) / P(N | F, B)].

The second bracket expresses what silence adds. The important question is not merely whether a warning is possible under failure. It is how likely silence is under failure compared with its likelihood under continued dependability.

First, suppose a genuinely discriminating review usually detects a loss of dependability and rarely raises a warning otherwise. For illustration, let P(R | B) be 0.8, P(N | R, B) be 0.95, and P(N | F, B) be 0.2. After silence, the probability of dependability is 0.95. The quiet record substantially supports renewal. The underlying review can be technically opaque to the person relying on it.

Second, suppose the final presentation process produces a reassuring report with probability 0.95 in either condition. It may label serious criticisms insignificant when the service is failing, while dismissing genuinely insignificant criticisms when it is dependable. The reported absence of a warning leaves the probability of dependability at 0.8. Silence adds nothing about the contrast between R and F. It need not erase the initial evidence or make immediate reliance unreasonable. It simply fails to do the new evidential work claimed for it.

Third, suppose dependable services candidly report occasional minor errors, while compromised services issue consistently clean reports. Let the probability of no warning be 0.9 under R and 1 under F. With the same prior probability of 0.8, silence lowers confidence in dependability to approximately 0.783. The record looks better precisely because one of the ways things can go badly is a failure to acknowledge problems.

The third case does not imply that every immaculate record is suspicious. Its force depends on justified comparative expectations. Some excellent services almost never err; some dishonest services stage confessions. Without grounds for the relevant likelihoods, one cannot infer corruption from quietness. The case establishes a possibility with a precise explanation: absence need not merely fail to support authority; it can be evidence against it.

Nor does the calculation show that the authority's most recent answer is false. Evidence about a source's standing and evidence about a particular answer are related but distinct. A compromised source can issue many true answers. The association might still have reason to accept some of them while withdrawing the broader policy of automatic reliance.

These examples expose the ambiguity in saying that no defeater has appeared. This can mean that a process capable of reporting failure has remained quiet. It can also mean that no claim survived a selection policy that makes the appearance of a defeater nearly impossible. The observations may sound alike in a meeting's minutes while carrying different information.

Nothing here is a new probability theorem. Its philosophical use is to discipline appeals to revisability. Saying that one would change one's mind if warned does not show that the information one actually receives can discriminate between an authority worth trusting and one that should be abandoned. A commitment to revision specifies a response to evidence; it does not, by itself, justify the mechanism through which evidence becomes available.

## 4. Why an adequate warrant can run out

The uninformative case still seems harmless. If the initial evidence justified trust and nothing has defeated it, why demand fresh support? The response is that not every initial warrant supports an indefinitely extended policy.

Suppose the association has excellent grounds for assigning a probability of 0.99 that its service is dependable at appointment. It also has relevant evidence that a service of this kind has a 0.02 probability per interval of losing that status, conditional on retaining it so far. A loss persists until an intervention; no intervention occurs in the example. These are stipulations about a changing service, not estimates about actual experts. Stipulate also that, conditional on each previous report history, the next report has the same distribution under continued dependability and its loss. Thus neither individual reports nor their sequence supplies information about the state. After t intervals:

P(R at t | initial evidence and uninformative reports) = 0.99 × 0.98^t.

After five intervals the probability is approximately 0.895. Suppose, solely to make the example definite, that the proposed policy claims assurance of at least 0.9 that the service meets the specified reliability standard. The initial appointment could meet that claim; automatic renewal after five intervals cannot meet it on the evidence given. No report identifying a particular error need have appeared.

The calculation does not establish that 0.9 is the right standard for knowledge, action, or every form of reliance. Its significance does not depend on that number: given continuing positive risk of persistent loss, an initially sufficient probability can fall below any positive standard that it originally exceeded. The relevant premise is the evidence-supported expectation of change. Stable cases need not behave this way. The later decline is already implicit in the initial evidence, so a rational initial plan would anticipate reconsideration. This is a limit on indefinite renewal, not a claim that an unforeseen defeater must appear or that rational planning is dynamically inconsistent.

It would be misleading to describe this as confidence deteriorating merely because time has passed. Time matters because processes that bear on dependability operate during it. The same is true when a change in personnel, equipment, incentives, task distribution, or physical condition makes earlier evidence less applicable. If the agent's evidence supports no such change, the argument supplies no automatic expiry date.

A defender of deference can therefore say that ordinary expert trust often retains its warrant for long periods. Agreed. The mistake arises when the limits of the evidence are silently replaced by a rule of continuation until an adverse report is recognized, even though the report-generating process supplies no information about the loss that must be excluded.

This yields a conditional requirement. If a policy of reliance is supported only within certain evidential conditions, the policy must be reconsidered when those conditions cease to be adequately supported. A review channel can maintain them by supplying discriminating evidence. An enduring background warrant can also maintain them. But the absence of recognized trouble, produced by a process known to remain reassuring in the relevant failure, cannot perform that maintenance on its own.

The practical conclusion is initially to reassess the form or degree of reliance. It is not necessarily to abandon the source. An association may rationally choose the best of poor options while acknowledging that its information no longer warrants its former confidence. Whether to act on advice also depends on alternatives and consequences. A failure of epistemic renewal and a requirement to purchase another service are different claims.

## 5. Review need not mean personal rechecking

Someone might object that the account merely relocates the difficulty. The nonexpert cannot judge a technical claim. How can the same person judge whether the authority would report its own failure?

Sometimes they cannot. There is no general escape from epistemic dependence. But the two judgments require different information. A person unable to solve a technical problem may know that audits draw cases at random, that outcomes are checked against records the service cannot alter, or that a separate group receives complaints without the service's permission. Those facts can justify some confidence in the review without enabling an independent verdict on every substantive question.

The review can itself be delegated. What matters is not that it comes from an outsider, or from a human rather than a machine, but that its evidential credentials address the relevant failure. Two nominally independent committees may depend on the same edited case files. One institution can contain a review process whose access to outcomes is protected against the management failure being considered. Organizational separation can help, but it neither defines nor guarantees the required evidential discrimination.

Consider an opaque but audited system. A user cannot follow its first-order reasoning, yet has adequate grounds for believing that a blinded test will identify a specified degradation and that the results reach her unaltered. A quiet audit can support continued reliance. Opacity about the underlying inference need not undermine that support.

Now consider a transparent but self-certifying system. The user can inspect every reason the system chooses to display, including detailed explanations of why every complaint was dismissed. But under the relevant failure the system selects just the same reassuring material. Additional visible explanation does not necessarily improve the record's diagnostic value. Transparency of an argument and evidential adequacy of a review are different achievements.

A third case blocks an overstrong conclusion. During a short expedition, a group may reasonably entrust navigation to its best guide and disregard a preidentified class of distracting amateur objections. That permission would not extend to unforeseen evidence of the guide's incapacitation. Prior evidence may be sufficient for the whole journey, including its modest risks of deterioration, despite there being no usable independent review while underway. The arrangement is closed but bounded. The group need not pretend that the guide's continuing confidence repeatedly reconfirms the original warrant.

These cases suggest a useful division of labor. Much first-order judgment can be delegated. What must remain adequate is the basis for the particular scope and duration of that delegation. Even that basis can depend on other people. The account requires neither universal self-sufficiency nor permanent access to every dissenting opinion.

## 6. Four objections

**The proposal is already contained in ordinary defeasibility.** In one sense, yes. A sufficiently rich account of evidence will count the known operation of a filter, and an evidence-supported change in reliability, among the considerations bearing on trust. The proposal is not a rival to such an account. It makes explicit something that a bare rule of deference until a recognized defeater leaves unspecified.

For example, Lange argues for a defeasible approach to AI authority that retains oversight and responds to domain mismatch, evidence undermining reliability, disagreement and evidence the system may have missed. He also recognizes that curated environments can reinforce dependence. These are substantial antecedents. My narrower point is that the conditions under which one would withhold deference need an account of how information capable of triggering them survives the delegated process. Otherwise a sound conditional response rule can coexist with an unsound continuation policy. [Lange 2025, §§2–3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21043).

Calling this an elaboration rather than a refutation is appropriate. Nevertheless, the elaboration changes concrete verdicts. It distinguishes an opaque system that can earn renewed trust through a good test from an articulate system whose openness is merely conditional on signals it cannot be expected to pass on. It also explains why a time-bounded closed arrangement can be rational. The distinction concerns the adequacy of a policy of dependence, not just a standing attitude of intellectual humility.

**The independence requirement creates an infinite regress.** A reviewer needs a reviewer, and so on. This objection would defeat a demand that every confidence have a prior certificate from a wholly unrelated source. That is not the demand here. The question is whether the particular observation contributes the support attributed to it, given the background actually available.

A standard tested against another can be informative despite relying on perception, memory and background scientific knowledge. The fact that these capacities are shared does not make the test useless. Conversely, adding another institution contributes nothing if the test inherits the precise blind spot under investigation. Relevant independence is local to a possible failure, not absolute independence from one's entire epistemic position.

The account also does not require discharging every skeptical possibility. If initial evidence adequately supports a reliable review process, one can use it without endlessly reopening its foundations. Further review becomes relevant when a warranted concern exceeds what that evidence supports. The challenge is to a specified attempted renewal, not to knowledge as a whole.

**The account gives manipulation too much power.** If a service suppresses reports, does it thereby destroy people's justification, even when they could not know about the suppression? And would that unfairly blame victims?

The strongest conclusions defended here concern people who have evidence about the relevant review policy or failure risk. An agent who knows that a reassuring report is issued irrespective of performance cannot count it as fresh corroboration. An agent who knows that a warrant was limited cannot extend it through that report. The epistemic standing of an unsuspecting person depends on larger disputes about accessible evidence, reliability and social entitlement. Those disputes remain open.

Blame is a further question. A person may lack the capacity or opportunity to repair a poor information environment. Institutions may be responsible for preserving useful channels precisely because individuals cannot. Showing that a procedure fails to provide the support advertised does not assign equal responsibility to everyone caught within it.

**More review can make us worse off.** Critics can flood a system with nonsense, cause false alarms, exhaust competent people and delay urgent action. A channel that passes more criticism may therefore reduce the quality of inquiry.

Exactly. The account assigns no intrinsic epistemic value to the volume of criticism. Its concern is discrimination between relevant states, together with the adequacy of the available warrant. Reducing false alarms can improve review; indiscriminately increasing alarm rates can destroy its informativeness. An appropriately selected sample of cases may be better than an unlimited open forum.

Nor is a source obliged to explain every rejection to every audience at every time. Where review is expensive, the appropriate arrangement may have longer intervals, narrower claims, limited reliance or explicit uncertainty. Reconsideration can consist in reducing a claim to assurance; it need not mean commissioning another audit. The best design depends on information and costs not supplied by the abstract argument. What costs cannot do is turn an observation equally expected under dependability and its loss into favorable evidence. They can justify acting with limited assurance; they cannot manufacture the assurance.

## 7. The limit of reassurance

The alternative to blind trust is often described as thinking for oneself. That description misses a less demanding and more useful possibility: preserve an adequate basis for the scope and duration of thinking through someone else.

An authority can legitimately filter objections. It can sometimes legitimately conduct its own review. A person can even reasonably rely on it during an interval with no effective review at all. What does not follow is that the absence of a recognized warning continually renews the warrant for those arrangements. Whether silence supports trust depends on how silence is produced. Whether old trust remains justified depends on what its original grounds still support.

The important question at renewal is therefore not simply whether any credible criticism has appeared. It is whether the record, understood together with its conditions of production, has earned the epistemic role being assigned to it.
