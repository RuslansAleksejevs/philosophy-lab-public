# What a Correction Must Undo

## Abstract

An institution can withdraw an unreliable claim, notify every reader, and still leave the claim's evidential influence largely intact. Later reports may survive as apparent independent confirmation even though they merely reproduced the withdrawn source. I argue that correction must address a defective route of reliance, not just an item of information. Where an institution has invited reasonable reliance on a presentation of evidence, discovers a material defect in that presentation, and can feasibly address it, its corrective duty extends to the evidential uses that the defect undermines. This requirement neither demands complete historical disclosure nor contaminates every later claim with its origin. It permits targeted warnings, protected provenance and genuinely new verification, while explaining why some perfectly accurate notices fail to correct anything that matters.

## 1. The error that survives its correction

A public testing registry lists three reports that a mineral occurs in a rock sample. Different laboratories signed the reports. The registry's ordinary presentation gives readers reason to treat such listings as separate checks. Later, its staff discover that an earlier report, D, had no evidential basis. They withdraw D, place a clear notice beside its record, and send the notice to every registered reader. No one disputes the notice. No one continues to cite D as an assay.

The three later reports remain. All three copied D's result without conducting another test. The registry knows this, but says nothing about it. A new reader reads and accepts the notice, then sees several laboratories agreeing and takes their agreement as corroboration. An old reader, who has absorbed the retraction, thinks: “The first report failed, but three later reports still support the result.”

Neither reader needs to have forgotten the correction or reasoned perversely. They lack a fact about what the surviving reports did. The institution corrected the status of one item without correcting the appearance of support that the item helped create.

This example concerns a specific kind of invitation. A bare list of references does not invariably promise independent evidence. Readers can be unreasonable in treating it that way. The registry is stipulated to have an established practice that makes the interpretation reasonable: its listings normally distinguish separate tests from republications, and it continued presenting these reports in the former way. The problem arises when that practice supplies a reason for reliance which the institution now knows has failed.

I will defend a condition on adequate correction: an institution must address the materially defective uses of evidence that its own presentation continues to invite. The obligation is to make the affected reliance corrigible, not to ensure that every recipient ends with the true belief. This condition concerns what a correction must communicate, alongside the familiar question of whom it must reach. It also explains why keeping every old document can be insufficient, while a short notice that reveals no sensitive original content can sometimes be enough.

## 2. Dependence is ordinary; disguised dependence is the problem

The possibility of responsible dependence is essential here. A reader cannot reasonably be required to repeat every laboratory test. Hardwig argues that inquiry regularly rests on other people's work which the individual cannot personally check; his account distinguishes the expert's grounds from another person's grounds for trusting that expert. He also emphasizes the dependencies within collaborative research. One need not accept his controversial suggestion that the dependent person's grounds are not evidence for the proposition itself. What matters is that responsible reliance can have a structure which the recipient has not reconstructed firsthand. [Hardwig 1985, pp.335–338, 345–349.](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523)

Nor is there anything novel in observing that repeated testimony can fail to add support. Goldman shows why an additional speaker who reflects another's judgment without discriminating between its truth and falsity supplies no further confirmation once the first judgment is counted. He also stresses that a later judgment can add support through critical checking, even when its route partly passes through the earlier speaker. Complete causal separation is unnecessary. [Goldman 2001, pp.98–102.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x)

These points rule out two easy answers. “Trust nobody's work until you reproduce it” would undo the division of inquiry. “Reject everything descended from an unreliable source” would discard genuine discoveries prompted by that source. The relevant question is narrower: which particular reason for confidence survives?

A report can perform at least three different roles. It may supply a new observation; it may check an earlier inference; or it may transmit a result to a new audience. All can be useful. Transmission helps someone who had not heard the result. But transmission does not, merely by occurring again, give someone who already counted the result another observation. A corrective notice needs to distinguish these roles only where their confusion matters to the reliance it addresses.

The social production of apparently growing evidence is also familiar. Greenberg's study of a biomedical citation network identifies, among other mechanisms, amplification by papers supplying no new relevant data and the conversion of hypotheses into asserted facts through citation. The point I draw from the study is limited: counts of publications can grow without corresponding growth in support. I do not rely on an independent assessment of the medical claim. [Greenberg 2009, abstract.](https://www.bmj.com/content/339/bmj.b2680.abstract)

The issue for correction is what follows after a responsible institution learns that it has helped create that mismatch.

## 3. A notice everyone reads can still be inadequate

Consider two possible histories of the testing registry. In both, the public sees three positive reports and the same notice: “D was not based on a valid assay and has been withdrawn.” In the first history, the later laboratories ran separate tests. In the second, they copied D. In neither history does the notice tell readers how the later reports were produced. Assume that its withdrawal of D provides no separate evidence about the mineral.

A deliberately simple model displays the lost distinction. The three reports are a fixed set, displayed whatever their results; there is no selective publication in this example. Let H be the mineral's presence, with prior probability one half. In the first history, each of the three tests has probability 0.9 of a positive report if H is true and 0.1 if H is false. Their reports are mutually independent conditional on H and conditional on its negation. Three positive reports then yield:

P(H | three positives, separate tests) = 0.9³ / (0.9³ + 0.1³) = 729/730.

In the second history, D's positive or negative result was generated by a fair coin unrelated to H. All three reports copied it. The likelihood of three positives is one half whether H is true or false. Thus:

P(H | three positives, copying) = 1/2.

These are stipulated evidential structures, not measured rates for actual laboratories. They restate a familiar dependence lesson rather than establish a new probability theorem. Their use is to expose a limitation of the notice. The displayed propositions about D and the existence of the later reports can be exactly the same, while those reports sustain radically different inferences.

This does not mean a reader ignorant of the history must somehow choose the posterior that an omniscient reader would choose. She can rationally remain uncertain about the histories and combine their likelihoods. For example, giving the histories equal prior probability, independently of H, yields a posterior of 1229/1730, rather than either limiting value. The institution's failure is not that she lacks omniscience. It is that the institution possesses information defeating a mode of reliance which its presentation continues to recommend, yet supplies a notice that leaves that recommendation in place.

The distinction also separates two failures of retraction. LaCroix, Geil and O'Connor model ways in which false information can persist because people who receive it never receive its retraction. In their model, a recipient of the retraction revises successfully. Their discussion recommends more active communication through central sources and the venues that originally conveyed the falsehood. Those proposals address a real obstacle. [LaCroix, Geil and O'Connor 2021, sections 1–2 and 4.](https://doi.org/10.1086/712817)

The registry case survives the removal of that obstacle. Everyone can receive the notice; the remaining reports can still appear to support the conclusion. Further circulation of the same notice does not supply the missing connection. Transmission succeeds, but the correction leaves its target underspecified.

A simple limitation follows. A correction policy cannot both retain the same positive invitation to treat the reports as separate tests and make that invitation responsible in these two histories, if its public output does not distinguish them. It must disclose a relevant difference, suspend that invitation, or acquire new grounds that remove the difference's significance. This is a constraint on a policy that continues soliciting reliance, not an impossibility theorem about rational belief under uncertainty.

## 4. Why the institution owes more

That the reader would benefit from dependence information does not by itself establish a duty to provide it. Institutions possess indefinitely many useful facts. Why single out this one?

The answer begins with the character of an invitation to rely. When a registry presents three entries as separately obtained observations, it offers readers a way to economize on inquiry. Readers need not establish the provenance of every entry because the presentation performs some of that work for them. The institution receives the practical authority of organizing the evidence; its users accept a corresponding dependence.

The relevant moral premise is modest: someone who reasonably invites others to rely on a representation has a defeasible obligation to correct a material defect in that representation once discovered, when doing so is feasible. Continuing the invitation while withholding an accessible fact that defeats it transfers the cost of the representer's discovery to people who were invited not to undertake that inquiry themselves. The obligation need not be contractual, and it need not depend on fault in the initial mistake. It arises from the relationship created by the invitation and the representer's continuing control over it.

Consider a mapmaker who learns that two marked routes are actually one route displayed twice because of a defective survey. The mapmaker marks the survey record unreliable, but leaves the map offering two routes. That true correction of the source's status does not address a traveller's reasonably induced belief that a backup route exists. The mapmaker still controls a misleading representation of the traveller's options. In the registry, the misrepresented options are evidential: there appear to be several ways for the conclusion to survive the failure of one test.

This is not a claim that correction without blame is a newly discovered moral category. Nascimento and da Silva distinguish corrective responsibility from culpability for inadequate inquiry, including obligations arising after honest error and concerning unsupported or misleading content. My concern is to identify the object of such repair when several statements support one another: what remains owed after the institution has accurately corrected the originally defective item? [Nascimento and da Silva 2026, section 6.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-026-01330-w)

The argument can now be made explicit. First, the institution has supplied a reasonable basis for treating an evidential route as available: agreement among separate checks. Second, it discovers that the route is unavailable in this case. Third, the route remains operative in the institution's presentation after its announcement. Fourth, the defect is material and a suitable correction is feasible. Under the stated moral premise, the institution has not discharged its corrective obligation merely by making a different, true announcement about the original report.

The conclusion does not depend on readers actually being harmed. Suppose the mineral really is present. The copied reports still do not provide the support advertised. Nor does it depend on whether H deserves belief on all available evidence. A fourth laboratory might have demonstrated H beyond reasonable doubt. That could justify H while leaving the registry's representation of three independent tests false or misleading. The appropriate correction might then be “these reports do not constitute three tests; the conclusion is supported by the later independent assay.” Correcting the record need not require withdrawing the conclusion.

There are two reasons to retain this distinction even when the conclusion is well supported elsewhere. The defective representation can be reused after circumstances change, when the fourth test is challenged or its applicability narrows. More fundamentally, the institution undertakes to represent which grounds support its conclusions, not just announce which conclusion it currently accepts. This undertaking gives it no power to make a consideration into evidence by decree. Supplying a good new reason does not turn the old purported reason into a good one.

The obligation is nevertheless limited. It applies to reasonable reliance invited by the institution, to a discovered material defect, and to measures within its practical control. The registry does not thereby acquire a duty to audit every conversation in which someone repeated H. It has a stronger obligation to amend its own live listings than to pursue an untraceable repetition elsewhere. Known recipients and identifiable downstream records can generate additional duties of notification, with their extent governed by foreseeable stakes, feasibility and competing responsibilities.

## 5. What counts as repair

The resulting standard is this:

**Where an institution continues to invite reasonable reliance on an evidential presentation, an adequate correction must either enable recipients to recognize the materially affected grounds or clearly withdraw the invitation to use them in the affected way.**

Adequacy is relative to the audience and uses the institution reasonably addresses. A technical dependency code might inform specialists while failing lay readers who were offered an intelligible account. The institution need not anticipate every eccentric inference. A defect is material here when it makes a difference to the use for which the evidence is offered, rather than merely changing some remote probability by a negligible amount.

“Enable” is not “guarantee.” Some recipients ignore clear notices. Others lack access to a channel despite reasonable attempts to reach them. These are questions of communication and responsibility, not reasons to define successful correction as universal true belief. Here adequacy concerns whether a recipient who encounters and reasonably interprets the correction can identify the limitation it is meant to impose.

The standard concerns specific uses. Merely citing a report as evidence that someone once believed H is different from treating it as a fresh assay supporting H. A retracted report may remain perfectly good evidence for the former claim. A notice that makes this difference clear prevents both excessive reliance and excessive rejection.

Several responses can meet the requirement. The registry might label the three reports: “These reproduce D's result and supply no additional assay.” It might preserve a public link describing that dependence. If disclosure would expose protected information, it might state only: “These entries should not be counted as separate tests; the underlying supporting result has been withdrawn.” If the extent of dependence is uncertain, it may mark a specified group of reports as unverified pending inquiry. The recipients need not be able to reconstruct every historical event. They need enough information to stop using the representation in the way known to be unsupported.

The last option must be distinguished from a routine disclaimer that all information might contain errors. Such a disclaimer gives no guidance about a newly discovered, specific defect and can coexist with the very invitation it supposedly revokes. By contrast, a clear notice that a defined set of entries cannot currently be used as independent confirmations genuinely suspends that use. Whether the institution also ought to complete the promised investigation is a further duty of its ongoing service.

Independent verification offers another remedy, but it needs careful description. Suppose each laboratory now conducts an actual test. This creates new support; it does not retrospectively alter how the earlier reports were produced. The registry can replace its invitation to rely on the old group with a dated invitation to rely on the new tests, while making the transition clear. Quietly changing the old entries would leave earlier decisions and citations misleadingly described. The correction must identify enough of the change for readers to know which reliance has been repaired.

The standard therefore supplies a stopping point as well as a demand. Once the affected use has been clearly withdrawn, or its defect has been made intelligible and the replacement support accurately presented, this particular corrective obligation is discharged. Further historical research may remain valuable, and compensation for earlier losses may still be owed. Neither follows simply from the continued existence of a past error.

This stopping point matters because otherwise correction tends toward two unattractive extremes. One is minimalism: change the false sentence and declare success. The other is archival maximalism: preserve everything because something might someday matter. Neither asks which distinctions are needed for the institution's current invitation to rely.

## 6. Preservation without unrestricted disclosure

The distinction between a source's content and its evidential role makes a limited alternative possible. Imagine that D accidentally revealed a person's protected medical information. Replicating D indefinitely to preserve the history of the error would compound a separate wrong. Yet deleting every trace of the dependence could leave derivative assertions looking independently confirmed.

The institution need not choose between those outcomes. A notice can identify the affected entries and their shared evidential basis without reproducing the protected facts or identifying the person. More detailed evidence can sometimes be retained under appropriately restricted access, so that a qualified reviewer can check the notice's accuracy. In other cases even restricted retention may be impermissible or impractical; a clear withdrawal of the affected evidential use can still be available.

The right amount of preservation is thus relative to the uses the institution continues to invite. If the registry wants readers to distinguish genuinely separate tests from dependent repetitions, it must retain or recover enough information to support that distinction. If it cannot do so, it should stop representing the entries as suitable for that purpose. It does not owe the public every detail of the failed source's history merely because history is epistemically relevant.

This explains both why complete preservation can fail and why limited preservation can succeed. Keeping D in a distant archive, clearly marked as withdrawn, fails if the three surviving reports still appear on the main page as separate checks and no ordinary reader can discover their relation to D. Conversely, a concise dependence warning beside those reports can defeat the relevant inference without keeping D's full text publicly accessible. Preservation is useful insofar as it enables responsible use or correction, not simply because a larger archive contains more truths.

Existing retraction practice already recognizes part of this point. COPE's current guidance calls for prompt, accessible notices linked to the retracted work, and treats retraction as correction of the literature rather than punishment. The proposal here does not claim to invent that practice. It asks when a link to the original is insufficient: namely, when the institution's surviving presentation continues to recommend an evidential use that the notice does not identify as affected. [COPE Council 2025, official guidance preview.](https://doi.org/10.24318/cope.2019.1.4)

There is no guarantee that preserving one public history is always best. A well-designed correction can deliberately reveal less history while communicating more of what readers need to revise their reliance.

## 7. Three objections

**This is merely the instruction not to mislead.** At one level, the objection is right: the moral premise belongs to familiar norms of responsible communication. The proposed contribution is a specification of what must be corrected when the communication concerns an organized body of evidence. An item-based practice can comply with a rule to correct false assertions while leaving the inferential significance of the surviving items unchanged. The paired histories show why an adequate rule must concern the evidential uses the institution invites. The distinction yields opposing judgments about two otherwise plausible repairs: an accessible original retraction may be inadequate, and a terse warning without the original may be adequate. These are not delivered by counting corrected sentences or preserved documents.

**People are responsible for tracing their own sources.** Sometimes they are. A specialist claiming to have assessed three experiments may owe more investigation than a lay reader consulting a trusted registry. But the division of responsibility cannot be settled by placing all undisclosed dependence on the recipient. The institution has undertaken part of the sorting and continues to invite reliance on it. Its control of the presentation and knowledge of the specific defect make it particularly well placed to correct that representation. The duty concerns this advantage and undertaking, not a general exemption of readers from intellectual care. Where the institution never invited the disputed inference, the argument is correspondingly weaker.

**The duty licenses condemnation by ancestry.** That would be a serious mistake. A later researcher may have encountered H through D and then obtained strong new evidence. The existence of that causal history alone does not defeat the new evidence. Genealogical assessment already distinguishes epistemically problematic belief-producing mechanisms from the bare fact of having an origin; Stamatiadis-Bréhier's discussion also explicitly allows that problematic genealogies can produce truths. The present argument needs only a local claim: a specified basis for treating an endorsement as an additional test is absent. It does not infer falsity from origins or authorize wholesale rejection of a field. [Stamatiadis-Bréhier 2023, opening and section 7.](https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174X.2023.2187449)

The last objection also raises a question of power. An institution might use the language of dependence to discredit results it dislikes. Its announcement is itself testimony, subject to assessment and challenge. Where feasible, it should state the basis and scope of the discovered dependency and permit appropriate scrutiny. When it lacks sufficient evidence to classify a report, it should say so. The corrective norm does not make institutional declarations self-validating; an unsupported accusation of dependence would create another misleading evidential presentation.

An institution can properly withdraw its own invitation to rely without acquiring authority to forbid all other people from believing the conclusion. Someone with independent grounds may reasonably continue to believe H. The point of the notice is to separate those grounds from a support route that has failed.

## 8. The proper object of correction

A correction is not simply a new true statement about an old false one. In an organized evidential setting, it is an intervention in the reasons an institution invites people to use. That invitation can survive the withdrawal of the original report, and it can remain defective even if its conclusion happens to be true.

The obligation defended here is limited but consequential. When an institution discovers a material defect in a mode of reliance that its own presentation reasonably solicits, it should make the affected grounds recognizable or withdraw that use. Sometimes this requires following an error into later reports. Sometimes it requires preserving a dependence link. Sometimes a targeted suspension is enough, and sometimes fresh evidence can support a carefully identified replacement. None requires the public retention of every original document.

The aim is neither historical purity nor certainty. It is to let people revise their reliance for the right reason. An institution has not corrected its error while continuing to offer the error's descendants as evidence that the correction can safely be ignored.
