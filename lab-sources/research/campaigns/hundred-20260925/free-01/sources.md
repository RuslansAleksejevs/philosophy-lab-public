## Primary texts actually consulted

1. **John Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” The Journal of Philosophy 82(7) (1985): 335–349, DOI 10.2307/2026523.** Exact PDF saved from the public Paul Maharg reference repository and locally extracted with pdftotext. Reading coverage: excerpts, especially pp.335–338 and 345–349, with intervening material around pp.339–341 partly displayed; tool truncation means the whole paper is not claimed read. Directly relevant: Hardwig distinguishes the expert's reasons from the dependent person's reasons for relying on the expert, emphasizes that a chain of authority must reach evidence, and describes complex research dependence. The manuscript need not accept his controversial exclusion of testimonial grounds from evidence for p. Its claim is that inquiry can properly be delegated while the structure of that delegation still matters. His distinction is a resource, not the new contribution.

2. **Alvin I. Goldman, “Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?” Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 63(1) (2001): 85–110, DOI 10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x.** The Rutgers PDF URL redirected to the department homepage and CiteSeer failed. The actual published article's visible transcription was read through the publicly accessible Scribd page, not its AI-generated page title or summary. Saved web excerpts include pp.85–88 and 95–103, particularly the complete central discussion at pp.98–102. Textual mathematical notation has OCR defects (negation bars can disappear); do not quote its formulas. Central argument read: numbers alone do not supply extra support; a non-discriminating reflector adds none once the original is counted. Even partially autonomous checking may add support; completely separate origin is not required. The essay must credit this and must not claim novelty for the probability lesson.

3. **Travis LaCroix, Anders Geil and Cailin O’Connor, “The Dynamics of Retraction in Epistemic Networks,” Philosophy of Science 88(3) (2021): 415–438, DOI 10.1086/712817.** Exact openly hosted author PDF saved from Cailin O’Connor's site; locally extracted. Reading coverage: abstract and author-version pp.1–7, conclusion pp.26–30, plus final acknowledgment page boundary. Intermediate simulation results, figures and appendix proofs were not closely read. It is an author version with different pagination from the journal; no journal pinpoint citation is inferred from the local page. The models assume recipients of retraction revise successfully; a retraction is normally shared when relevant to an already false belief, and incomplete transmission can explain persistence without irrationality. The conclusion calls for linked notices, active distribution through central communicators and original venues. This is the nearest challenge to novelty. The proposed essay grants those results and prescriptions. It isolates inadequate notice content even under universal receipt, then argues a limited duty to withdraw the evidential use of surviving dependent statements. It neither refutes their model nor purports to predict diffusion empirically.

4. **Steven A. Greenberg, “How citation distortions create unfounded authority: analysis of a citation network,” BMJ 339 (2009): b2680, DOI 10.1136/bmj.b2680.** Reading coverage: full abstract only, as actually returned by BMJ search result and saved in search-001.json; bibliographic/methodological search metadata also saved. Full PDF was forbidden, and PMC presented a bot check. Do not claim the full study or its medical conclusions were independently assessed. The abstract reports citation bias, amplification by papers adding no data, and invention through citation; this establishes a close predecessor for the general problem of apparent evidential growth, not an empirical validation of the hypothetical case.

5. **Alexios Stamatiadis-Bréhier, “Genealogical Undermining for Conspiracy Theories,” Inquiry, online 2023, DOI 10.1080/0020174X.2023.2187449.** Saved excerpts of the open author manuscript from PhilArchive's web representation. Reading coverage: opening pp.1–4; selected passages in section 7, pp.16–18, returned by find. Direct download returned 403. The genealogical argument concerns mechanisms operating without regard for truth; section 7 discusses laundering through reliable sources and acknowledges that problematic genealogies can produce truths. The new essay should not claim that histories first become relevant here, nor commit a genetic fallacy. It addresses a narrow surviving support route, not wholesale condemnation of a person, domain or conclusion.

6. **Dimas Cassimiro Nascimento and Daliton da Silva, “Identical errors, different responsibilities: epistemic duty in AI-assisted scientific writing,” AI and Ethics 6, article 484 (2026), DOI 10.1007/s43681-026-01330-w.** Primary publisher excerpts saved. Read introduction and substantial displayed material through sections 3–4, plus selected passages from 6–7; not the full article or cited references. The article explicitly distinguishes authorship, epistemic and corrective responsibility; correction may be owed for honest error and misleading or unsupported content. This defeats any claim that blame-independent corrective responsibility is new. The essay adds an object and stopping condition for such a duty: the invitation to aggregate dependent surviving claims, limited by repaired support routes rather than sentence truth or retention of all documents.

7. **COPE Council, Retraction Guidelines, version 3, August 2025, DOI 10.24318/cope.2019.1.4.** Read the official page's key points and guidance preview returned in search-002.json, including its change history. Not the full downloadable guideline. The DOI retains 2019 despite the updated version. The official preview recommends prompt freely accessible notices linked to the retracted work and treats retraction as literature correction rather than punishment. A fresh direct web open failed with 403. The essay credits this practice, and does not falsely present preserving linked notices as its invention or assert what the unread PDF says about removal.

## Refined contribution and rejected stronger claims

REJECT: “Dependent repetition is not independent evidence.” Established explicitly by Goldman; no contribution by itself.
REJECT: “Corrections need to reach downstream readers.” Explicitly anticipated by LaCroix et al.
REJECT: “The original false document must always be retained.” Unsupported, harmful in privacy cases, and linked-retraction practice is already established.
REJECT: “A bad origin invalidates every later endorsement.” Fails where later checking supplies genuinely new discrimination; Goldman already distinguishes this.
REJECT: “Correctors owe repair only if culpable.” Contradicted by plausible role-based responsibility and explicitly rejected by Nascimento and da Silva.

RETAIN, conditional on successful argument: a correction is inadequate when an institution knowingly leaves operative a reasonable invitation, arising from its own presentation, to use surviving reports through a support route that the discovered defect defeats. Notice delivery and original retention do not by themselves meet this condition. The duty can be met by (a) enough dependence information to defeat that use, (b) a targeted suspension of the representation, or (c) new warranted support with the change made intelligible. A least-disclosure condition follows: preserve distinctions relevant to permitted evidential use, not necessarily all documents or identifying history. This separates a duty of correction from a general archival maximalism.

The paired-history example will hold fixed every displayed claim, including receipt of a notice, while varying whether remaining reports are copies or independent inspections. The exact arithmetic model is an illustration of information lost, not proof that a reader without the history is irrational, nor proof of the moral premise. A reader may rationally mix over unknown histories; the model-check includes that countercase. The duty instead concerns an institution with access to the defect that continues soliciting reliance as if the missing distinction were settled.

Selection: pursue a complete argument with an explicit non-misleading-reliance premise and bounded proportionality conditions. Originality is relative to the sources and passages actually read. The result may be an original synthesis and sharpening of existing obligations rather than an entirely new category of duty.
