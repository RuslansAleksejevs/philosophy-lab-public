## Assessment and reconstruction

The essay has a credible central diagnosis and a useful practical distinction. It should be revised rather than abandoned. Its strongest claim is presently the limited one: a commitment cannot, merely by invoking its own authority, settle whether it is permissible to dismantle a safeguard on which its justification depended. The more demanding claim that a recognized loss requires repair or a fresh establishment of justification needs a clearer threshold and a clearer account of the kind of rational assessment involved. Originality remains a significant uncertainty, despite the draft's commendably explicit treatment of antecedents.

The argument, charitably reconstructed, is this. Leah's reasons justify a package: sustained translation, resistance to habitual reconsideration, and access to relevant attribution research. Her filter changes that package by eliminating a safeguard. A reason for retaining the original package is not automatically a sufficient reason for retaining the altered package. In particular, the resolution cannot dismiss requests to preserve the very informational arrangement that helped justify resisting reconsideration. Nor can an empty inbox reassure Leah once she knows contrary reports cannot enter it. The appropriate response can concern the management of her intention while leaving its object in place: restore a way to learn about attribution, and continue translating unless the relevant grounds change.

The essay does not claim that all commitments must remain maximally open, that adverse reports must always prompt revision, or that a monitoring intention solves temptation. These limitations are philosophically necessary and generally well handled. The argument also avoids treating an ultimately successful translation as retrospective vindication of Leah's earlier reasoning.

Three merits deserve preservation. First, lines 23–25 separate project reconsideration, monitoring, and maintenance of monitoring; the distinction identifies different possible objects of a practical demand. Second, lines 43–45 distinguish the evidential misuse of silence from the practical misuse of an earlier decision. Either defect could occur without the other. Third, lines 73–85 allow justified ignorance, changed purposes, and ordinary reliance without requiring certainty about every monitor. These are reasons to take the positive proposal seriously, not merely concessions made to save it.

## 1. Lost support does not yet establish the stated rational requirement

**Locations:** lines 11–13; the bold constraint at line 33; the explanation of “materially supports” at line 35; the package argument at lines 39–41; lines 53 and 91.

**Classification:** source-grounded lens applied by AI, plus an independent counterexample. Hawthorne and Stanley distinguish the evaluation of a selected action from its grounds at manuscript pp. 5–6 and 11–12; p. 9 explicitly separates permission to use a relevant consideration from a requirement to use it. Those distinctions motivate the audit here. They do not themselves establish my objection.

The first sentence of the bold constraint denies that the policy's authority is, by itself, sufficient to justify disabling a safeguard. The second says that recognized loss requires either restoration or establishing continued justification. There is a gap between these claims. A consideration may materially improve the case for an option without being necessary for its permissibility. It may also have helped motivate the agent's choice without being needed by the adequate grounds she already possesses. Showing that one proposed justification does not transfer is not yet showing that her existing grounds fail to justify continuing, or that she must undertake an additional justificatory activity.

Test a Leah who initially appreciates two substantial grounds for a protected year. Attribution-specific interest, helped by the bulletin, is one; a separate benefit of finishing a complete translation is another. Each would be sufficient for accepting the year, and Leah already appreciates that fact. The bulletin nevertheless adds materially to her actual case and helps explain her choice. Its loss does not generate a requirement to discover or establish a new justification. The adequate alternative grounds are already in place. Whether deliberately disabling it is sensible will still depend on its costs and benefits; that is a further question. The case is intended to isolate continued justification, not to vindicate an otherwise pointless filter.

The draft partly anticipates this through changed purposes and fresh grounds at lines 41 and 79. But the relevant grounds in this test are neither changed nor newly discovered. Line 35's “relevant difference to the case” does not unambiguously exclude it. The author's stronger reading may be that the safeguard makes a difference to whether this degree of nonreconsideration is warranted, rather than merely contributing weight. If so, say what establishes that difference. Leah's having accepted the year with the safeguard, while not previously accepting it without the safeguard, is not by itself evidence that she lacked sufficient grounds for the latter arrangement.

There is also a distinct question about her actual reasoning. Even if continuing under the altered policy is supported by grounds available to her, Leah might continue solely because she falsely thinks her resolution settles the issue. That would support criticism of her reasoning without showing that the altered policy is itself impermissible. The draft already distinguishes project from policy; it needs the corresponding distinction between a permissible policy and maintaining that policy on an inadequate basis.

**Revision test:** separate three versions of the case: a safeguard is necessary to the agent's adequate grounds; it is substantial but dispensable in light of grounds she already appreciates; or its loss gives her reasonable doubt about whether adequate grounds remain. State what is required in each version. Clarify whether “establishing” means acquiring new reasons, recognizing existing sufficient reasons, or conducting explicit deliberation. The least demanding plausible reading need not require a new assessment every time a supporting consideration disappears. Preserve the weak nontransfer claim even if the stronger requirement needs additional premises.

## 2. Restoring the channel need not restore the position that justified reliance

**Locations:** line 51, “What she first needs is much smaller”; lines 55 and 65–67; the degradation discussion at line 85; the conclusion at lines 95–97.

**Classification:** independent argument prompted by the profile's demand for a small discriminating case. It is not an objection found in the judge evidence.

The decisive reparative example moves too quickly from unblocking the bulletin to restoration of the arrangement's justification. A route for future warnings and coverage of developments during the blocked interval are different things. The draft correctly refuses to infer reassurance from silence during filtering. But that earlier silence does not become informative when the filter is repaired.

Consider two versions of the bulletin. The first gives a current attribution assessment each month, incorporating all relevant findings. The second sends alerts only when new discoveries occur; it does not repeat settled discoveries in subsequent months. Leah knows which kind she receives. In both versions, unblocking it restores precisely the same future delivery function it had before. But if an alert was issued and discarded during the blocked interval, a silent month after reopening the second bulletin leaves Leah without the informational protection she originally relied upon. She may need to recover missed reports, obtain a current assessment, or accept a newly characterized risk. Simply restoring future delivery is insufficient.

This is not a refutation of the carefully worded reference to “adequate access.” Adequacy could include catching up. It is a consequential test of the essay's main example, because the example is what makes repair seem both easy and independent of project reassessment. If the lost interval cannot be reconstructed, prospective repair may leave a practical uncertainty that the earlier arrangement did not contain. The option of justifying persistence without the safeguard remains available, but its application should be acknowledged rather than describing the old justification as restored.

The point also clarifies the role of causal history. A currently open channel can be functioning perfectly while the agent's reasons for relying on the whole policy remain damaged by known earlier exclusion. Conversely, a recently closed channel might not yet create a significant information gap if its last report is sufficiently current. The relevant object of repair is the support needed for the practical policy, not simply a device's open or closed status.

**Revision test:** run the proposed remedy through both the current-assessment bulletin and the one-time-alert bulletin. State when unblocking, recovering missed information, or assessing persistence under residual uncertainty is warranted. Explain whether catching up can itself remain monitoring rather than reopening the project, and identify the condition under which that separation fails. No general theory of information systems is needed; a few precise sentences in the central case would make the defended possibility more convincing.

## 3. The essay needs a sharper account of its gain over the strongest existing view

**Locations:** lines 19–25; the cost–benefit objection and reply at lines 47–59; the planning comparison at line 87; the final claim at line 97.

**Classification:** source-grounded lens applied by AI, together with generic assessment of originality and significance. Hawthorne and Stanley's examination of explanatory direction at pp. 5–6, and their insistence on distinguishing an action's correctness from a reason's admissibility at pp. 11–12, support asking exactly what the proposed explanation adds. The competition-level novelty assessment is this reviewer's, with limited literature coverage.

The draft accurately acknowledges close predecessors. The independent source check confirms that Holton's discussion allows unreflective implementation activity and monitoring while withholding project reconsideration; his note 14 distinguishes temptation-related pressure from genuinely new information. Thus a strong Holton-based rival can say: monitoring is part of rational persistence; maintaining an inexpensive means needed for appropriate monitoring is ordinarily required; restoring the bulletin is such maintenance. It appears to deliver the practical recommendation without the new constraint. [Holton, *Rational Resolve*, manuscript pp. 7–9, especially p. 8 and note 14.](https://web.mit.edu/holton/www/pubs/rational.resolve.a4.pdf)

Anaya is also a closer antecedent than merely the rejection of constant checking. He requires context-appropriate strategies for discovering relevant information and grounds their demands in independently applicable epistemic obligations. The essay distinguishes its practical subject matter, but more work is needed to show what its justification-relative reasoning contributes beyond an application of a familiar requirement to sustain appropriate information access. [Anaya, “‘Absent Contrary Indication,’” §4, especially the discussion of normative defeaters.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-021-00455-0)

The best answer may be the specific misuse of commitment as a veto on maintaining its own conditions. That is more interesting than a third item added to a monitoring taxonomy. However, a distinctive mistake in a person's explanation is not automatically a distinctive rule governing permissible policies. If the author locates the contribution in the structure of Leah's defective reasoning, it should say so and defend why that structure matters. If the contribution is instead a further obligation of repair, the strongest rival should be shown to miss or misclassify a case that the new account handles.

**Revision test:** compare Leah with an agent facing the same current information, costs, warning channel, and available actions, but who never invokes a resolution to dismiss maintenance. Would the practical demand differ, or only the diagnosis of why one agent resists it? Either answer can be defended. The comparison will establish whether the central innovation concerns justificatory inference, normative scope, or merely the practical presentation of an existing requirement. Then give the strongest existing account its own explanation of the translation case before identifying the remaining difference. This asks for a discriminating argument, not a claim to have discovered a new ultimate source of normativity.

The current modest originality language should stay. The author has not established that the proposed distinction is absent from the wider literature on planning, deliberate ignorance, epistemic responsibility, and rational maintenance of intentions. A useful diagnostic extension can be a contribution, but its significance cannot rest on naming three activities that established accounts already allow.
