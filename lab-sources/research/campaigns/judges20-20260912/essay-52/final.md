# Keeping a Way to Change Your Mind

Leah has decided to spend a year translating a neglected writer. The work is slow, and every difficult passage invites the thought that she would be happier doing something else. She knows this about herself. So she resolves not to ask, each working morning, whether the project is worth continuing. She will work on the translation.

That seems sensible. Now suppose that the collection's attribution is subject to continuing research. Leah's interest is specifically in this writer: she wants to understand how this person thought. She has good initial evidence for the attribution, but she also recognizes a serious possibility that the evidence will change. A reliable monthly bulletin provides updates. She accepts a protected year because she expects significant contrary findings to reach her before she devotes much more work to a mistaken attribution. She does not consider a year spent translating just any collection an acceptable substitute. These details matter: the bulletin is part of the support she needs, rather than merely a pleasant addition to an otherwise sufficient case.

Later, protecting her concentration, Leah installs a filter that excludes messages raising attribution doubts. She continues to receive harmless bibliographical news. She knows how the filter works, and knows that it blocks her only practical route to relevant findings. Nevertheless, she regards changing it as the first step toward changing her mind, and changing her mind as precisely what she resolved to resist. No contrary evidence has reached her, she says. There is no reason to reopen anything.

Something has gone wrong before the attribution question is settled. Leah might ultimately complete an excellent translation of an authentic work. She might remain capable of reconsidering if a convincing reason were presented. Her mistake is using a reason for keeping the project settled to dismiss a challenge concerning the conditions under which it may remain settled.

This essay identifies that mistake and distinguishes the responses it can warrant. Its claim is not that losing any useful source of information obliges an agent to investigate. It is that the authority of a resolution does not automatically extend to the arrangements supporting its continued authority. Where those arrangements fail, one must distinguish three questions: whether adequate grounds remain, whether the agent relies on adequate grounds, and what response a recognized shortfall requires. Sometimes the response is to repair the arrangements while continuing the project. Sometimes no repair is required. Sometimes repairing a channel leaves a gap that future openness cannot cure.

The contribution is a diagnosis within an existing account of practical commitment, not a new fundamental norm. Its value lies in locating a particular misuse of commitment and the proper object of its correction. Telling Leah merely to be more open-minded may send her back to comparing hobbies. What she may need is to recover a bulletin.

A settled intention makes action possible without another decision at each step. Having chosen to translate, Leah can order reference books, allocate her mornings and develop a vocabulary. If every preparation awaited a fresh verdict on the value of translation, her choice would have settled very little. Stability also matters when the problem is not how long thought takes but what it predictably becomes. Frustration can make another project look attractive because its difficulties remain distant.

Richard Holton distinguishes weakness of will from acting against one's best judgment. A characteristic failure, on his account, is revising certain intentions too readily. But he expressly permits sensible revision when circumstances change, including changes never specified in advance. Whether persistence is reasonable therefore depends on the tendencies governing revision, not just on whether the intention survives. [Holton, “Intention and Weakness of Will,” manuscript pp. 5–8.](https://web.mit.edu/holton/www/pubs/Weakness.pdf)

In “Rational Resolve,” Holton extends a two-tier strategy associated with Bratman's planning theory: reasonable dispositions against reconsideration can help explain rational persistence. Monitoring, implementation thought and responsiveness to significant new information remain possible. His account already distinguishes resistance from complete closure. [Holton, “Rational Resolve,” manuscript pp. 7–9, especially p. 8 and note 14.](https://web.mit.edu/holton/www/pubs/rational.resolve.a4.pdf)

The further question concerns the reach of a reason to resist reconsideration. To keep its object clear, distinguish Leah's project, her rule about reconsidering it and her informational arrangements. Her project is the translation. Her rule is to leave the choice of project alone during ordinary work unless an appropriate ground for reopening emerges. The bulletin is an arrangement that helps support this rule over the intended period. In the central case, the project and the rule remain fixed while Leah changes the arrangement.

These are not three unrelated items. The merits of the project help explain the rule; the availability of information helps justify how firmly and for how long she applies it. But changing the arrangement need not reopen the choice of project. Restoring a subscription does not require comparing translation with gardening. Nor need reading a brief attribution update involve deciding how the whole year should be spent. The activities may lead to one another, but they are not identical.

Consider another Leah who blocks advertisements for other courses and keeps messages out of her mornings, while leaving the attribution bulletin accessible in the afternoon. She resists distraction without undermining the support for that resistance. The first Leah treats both restrictions as instances of one practice: refusing anything that might lead to a change of mind. That description conceals the different roles of what she excludes. The bulletin was one of the conditions under which she had grounds to let the project guide her without frequent reassessment.

What follows when such a condition disappears? Less follows than might initially seem. A safeguard can improve the case for nonreconsideration without being necessary to its adequacy. Imagine that Leah already appreciates two independent grounds for committing the year. One concerns attribution; the other concerns the value of finishing any sustained translation. She regards either as sufficient. The bulletin adds considerably to the appeal of the arrangement she chooses. Yet losing it need not remove her grounds for the same rule. She can recognize this immediately, without acquiring a new reason or conducting fresh deliberation.

That Leah must not be criticized merely for declining to reconstruct a justification she already possesses. “It helped justify my decision” does not entail “without it my decision would lack justification.” A reduction in support is not always a defeat of support. Nor does a person's having chosen the protected arrangement prove that she could not reasonably accept an unprotected one.

The opening case adds the missing premise. Leah understands the risk of misdirected work to be unacceptable for a protected year without timely information, and has no independently sufficient ground for accepting that risk. The bulletin's loss then affects the adequacy of the grounds on which she relies. Its importance is not defined by a requirement to repair it; it is explained by her purposes, her evidence about uncertainty and the risk she has grounds to accept. If those facts differ, the appropriate response can differ too.

Between the two cases lies a common third one. An agent does not know whether losing the safeguard leaves enough support. She has reason to suspect a shortfall: for example, the arrangement protected against a substantial risk she has not otherwise assessed or accepted. That is a reason to address whether reliance remains appropriate. It need not dictate an immediate inquiry, a particular repair or a complete reassessment. It can be outweighed or postponed by the costs and timing of a response. Mere awareness that more information would have been useful does not by itself create this demand.

The general point has a weak and a stronger part. The weak part concerns an inference: a reason for keeping the project settled is not, simply because it is such a reason, sufficient to refuse maintenance of its supporting arrangements. The stronger part concerns cases with additional grounds: when an agent recognizes that the remaining support is insufficient, she cannot adequately defend unchanged reliance by invoking that same settlement. When she instead has reasonable doubt about sufficiency, she has a defeasible reason to address the doubt. If she already appreciates that adequate support remains, no fresh justificatory exercise is required on this account.

These distinctions also separate the evaluation of a policy from the evaluation of someone's reasoning. Suppose the altered arrangement is supported by reasons Leah possesses, but she continues solely because she thinks any change would betray her resolution. Her answer can be defective even though her policy is permissible. Conversely, someone can choose the appropriate repair for a poor reason. Identifying a bad inference does not show that every action it motivates is unreasonable, or that the agent has only one permissible option.

The specific inference at issue reverses the role of the safeguard. Initially, access to attribution research supports a protected period of work. Later, the protected period is invoked as a sufficient reason to block that access. The original supporting relation does not license its own reversal. Something further must show that the grounds against project reconsideration also speak sufficiently against receiving the bulletin. Otherwise a limited permission is made to protect the loss of a condition for its use.

This reasoning can go wrong independently of Leah's claim about her inbox. Once she knows adverse reports cannot enter, silence no longer reassures her in the way that silence through a dependable channel might. Yet the practical mistake would remain if she conceded that point: “The empty inbox tells me nothing, but my resolution tells me not to change the filter.” In the opening case, she would still be offering a reason that does not answer the recognized problem with her grounds for reliance. No false attribution belief is needed to generate that problem.

Now consider the appropriate remedy. Suppose the bulletin contains a current assessment each month, incorporating all significant earlier findings. Restoring access and reading the current assessment can restore the relevant informational position. If nothing of consequence has changed, Leah may continue translating under the same rule without comparing the project with alternatives. The repair need not include an argument presented to herself. It may be ordinary implementation of something she already understands: these updates must remain available if she is to work in this way.

But reopening the channel is not always enough. Suppose instead that the bulletin sends alerts only when a discovery occurs. It does not repeat earlier discoveries in later issues. During three blocked months, a relevant alert may have been discarded. A quiet month after reopening provides no answer about that interval. Leah may need to recover the missed issues or obtain a current assessment. If neither is possible, future delivery leaves a residual uncertainty that must be included in the case for continued reliance.

The distinction is temporal. A channel can be working perfectly now while its recent history prevents it from supplying the support the policy needs. Restoring its future operation does not retrospectively make its earlier silence informative. Conversely, a newly blocked channel may not yet leave a significant gap if its last assessment is sufficiently current. What needs repair is the agent's relevant informational position, not merely the open or closed status of a device.

Catching up may still be limited monitoring. Leah can retrieve an archive and discover whether there has been an attribution change without first reopening whether she should be a translator. But the separation has limits. An ambiguous or irrecoverable record might require assessing whether the remaining uncertainty is acceptable for this project. If so, some practical reconsideration may be necessary. The claim is that repair can preserve a settled project, not that every defect permits a remedy wholly insulated from its merits.

This yields a distinction between two forms of responsiveness. An agent may be disposed to reconsider if a convincing reason is presented while having eliminated any realistic route through which it could be presented. And she may restore a route for future reasons while leaving a known gap in the information on which present reliance depends. Neither point demands omniscience. Both concern whether the grounds supporting a particular degree of nonreconsideration are still adequate.

There is a close epistemological antecedent. Alfonso Anaya argues that knowledge requires more than an actual absence of undefeated defeaters: agents must be appropriately ready to address them. His account includes context-appropriate strategies for identifying information they ought to have, grounded in independently applicable epistemic obligations. It does not require everyone to check continuously. [Anaya, “‘Absent Contrary Indication,’” §§3–4.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-021-00455-0)

That is a substantial part of the background to the present argument. But a knowledge condition cannot simply be transferred to an intention. It can be reasonable to act without knowing that one's project is best, and acceptable practical risk need not coincide with risk compatible with knowledge. Here the relevant standard is supplied by the grounds for applying a particular rule of nonreconsideration. An agent can accept significant ignorance. The mistake arises when she invokes the rule itself to dismiss a recognized problem with those grounds.

At this point a strong rival explanation is available. Holton's account already permits monitoring and implementation. Add ordinary practical reasons to maintain a useful means, or Anaya's attention to appropriate strategies for acquiring relevant information. Then Leah ought to keep up with the attribution research when her grounds for persistence require it. Repairing a filter is simply part of doing that. Nothing in the present argument shows that this combined explanation must give the wrong recommendation.

I accept that result. The additional claim is explanatory: in one recognizable form of failure, the agent treats the very reason that supports avoiding repeated choice as a veto on sustaining the conditions for avoiding it. Making that inference explicit identifies why her appeal to commitment fails, while distinguishing that failure from insufficient information, ordinary carelessness and a false belief about the project. It also prevents an overreaction. The discovery of defective management does not automatically make the choice of project the right object of reconsideration.

A control case shows the scope of this gain. Suppose another translator has the same information, costs, purposes and blocked bulletin as Leah, but neglects the problem out of laziness. Under the opening assumptions, both have the same reasons to restore adequate access. The resolution does not manufacture an extra duty for Leah. What differs is the attempted justification for resisting the response. Leah presents her resistance as fidelity to a reasonable decision. The other translator simply fails to do something she has reason to do. My diagnosis explains why the first defense fails; it does not predict different permissible policies merely because one agent voices it.

This distinction matters for how practical rationality governs a life over time. Reasons against deliberation are indispensable to a planning agent. Yet their function can be misdescribed so that declining an inquiry always looks like successful self-control. The remedy is not a general instruction to deliberate more. It is to identify which inquiry was settled, on what grounds, and whether those grounds authorize refusing the proposed response to a changed situation. Sometimes a smaller response preserves both the original decision and its rational support.

A different objection is that monitoring cannot remain a smaller response. Temptation does not announce itself honestly. Reading about attribution may offer Leah an excuse to quit. At a frustrating moment, even reliable information can become material for a distorted comparison. Every safeguard can turn into a route for the instability it was supposed to contain.

That possibility is real. A further intention to monitor wisely supplies no guarantee. Alida Liberman argues that pressure against a first-order intention can also become pressure against a higher-order intention not to reconsider it. Adding another level does not automatically block temptation. [Liberman, “Reconsidering Resolutions,” §§4–6.](https://www.jesp.org/paper/36346f73-90e0-81a9-b63b-ec1ee6d50946)

The present claim concerns what needs justification, rather than a psychological mechanism guaranteed to satisfy the demand. A monthly summary might be less destabilizing than a continuous discussion. Leah might receive it outside her frustrating working hours. A trusted reader might identify substantial attribution developments without presenting unrelated alternatives. These are possibilities whose merits depend on the circumstances. They are not empirical prescriptions or exceptions to the need for practical judgment.

Crucially, calling something “maintenance” does not exempt it from reasons against reconsideration. Suppose Leah has good grounds for a settled morning routine that includes avoiding attribution discussions. Altering that routine might threaten its benefits. The fact that the proposed alteration would improve information access does not settle the balance. Its costs, its timing and the justification of the routine must be considered. The distinction between project, rule and arrangement permits separate assessment; it does not predetermine different permissions.

The opening case is narrower. Leah has a cheap, dependable afternoon bulletin whose availability supported her rule. She has no independent grounds for thinking that merely keeping access would destroy the benefits of protected mornings. Her resolution to translate therefore does not answer the case for restoring access. In the contested-routine case, by contrast, reasons against changing the arrangement may be independently adequate. The verdict must remain open until those reasons are assessed, or may already be settled by grounds the agent appreciates.

Any independence required of the warning route is correspondingly limited. It need not be external, emotionally neutral or indifferent to the project. An enthusiastic collaborator might provide it. The relevant limitation is inferential: commitment to the project must not, without further support, suffice to disqualify information from a route whose availability helps warrant the manner of commitment. A report's tendency to make quitting attractive is not itself proof that the report is merely an expression of temptation. Its source, content and relevance still matter.

This can fit a richer view of resolve. Abigail Bruxvoort emphasizes cultivated patterns of desire and deliberation and questions whether a supposedly neutral higher-order comparison can protect us against temptation. [Bruxvoort, “Resolution and Resolve,” especially §3.](https://www.jesp.org/paper/36346f73-90e0-817b-a38d-c4640dffbdb5) Nothing here requires a neutral standpoint. Learning to receive a certain kind of warning without reopening every life choice might itself be an acquired capacity. Such a capacity would support both stable action and the recognition of its limits.

If no workable separation is available, the loss must enter the assessment honestly. Perhaps receiving any useful attribution information would predictably derail the project. A protected year without monitoring might still be reasonable, or Leah might choose a shorter commitment. The argument does not decide between them. It prohibits treating the original decision as a sufficient answer to a recognized shortfall while continuing to rely on support that has disappeared. It does not prohibit rebuilding the case from different grounds or recognizing grounds that were adequate all along.

We also need not preserve every imaginable exception. Someone who chooses a career need not receive a stream of advertisements for other careers. A commitment can reasonably include tolerating hardship, refusing better offers or accepting uncertainty that further information would remove. The question is not whether another consideration could ever favor an alternative. It is whether the informational restriction is covered by the grounds that make this degree of settlement reasonable.

Nor must the original purposes remain fixed forever. Leah may discover that sustained translation has become worthwhile independently of authorship. That can support continuing without the bulletin. The earlier self has no authority, supplied by this argument, to forbid that development. What would fail is the inference that because she remains committed, she must already possess adequate grounds for disregarding the conditions under which she committed herself.

There is a final worry about regress. If Leah must maintain a warning route, must she monitor it? Must she monitor the means of monitoring it? Eventually the entire year would disappear into checking.

The argument does not start from every possible failure. It starts from an actual or reasonably suspected shortfall that the agent can recognize. Leah knows what the filter removes. She need not prove before each working session that the whole causal history of her information is faultless. Ordinary reliance on reasonably dependable arrangements can supply adequate grounds. Evidence of another failure might generate a further reason to respond; its bare possibility is not already a demand to search for it.

The same point limits retrospective repair. Leah need not reconstruct every item ever omitted from her reading. A missed interval matters when it leaves a recognized gap in support relevant to her practical reliance. If a current assessment covers the interval, or if she already has sufficient grounds to tolerate the uncertainty, no further recovery is required on this account. Neither the forward nor the backward demand is a demand for certainty.

Computer planning provides another important antecedent. Veloso, Pollack and Cox distinguish selecting what to monitor, deliberating about detected changes and transforming a plan. Their monitors derive from the rationale for planning decisions, including alternatives not selected. [“Rationale-Based Monitoring for Planning in Dynamic Environments,” pp. 172–173.](https://cdn.aaai.org/AIPS/1998/AIPS98-021.pdf) Selective, rationale-sensitive monitoring is thus already developed work. The present essay does not propose an architecture. It clarifies a practical inference about restricting deliberation and the supporting arrangements to which that restriction may be applied.

Finally, the appropriate response has a time as well as an object. Leah may reasonably finish a paragraph before repairing the filter. A serious and time-sensitive attribution risk may require quicker attention than a minor possibility with no imminent effect on her work. Identifying inadequate support does not remove every other practical reason. It does remove one purportedly sufficient answer: that changing anything would be contrary to the resolution.

If the translation succeeds, its success does not show that this answer was adequate. An unsupported risk can fail to materialize. If a maintained bulletin produces a misleading alert and wastes an afternoon, that does not show the safeguard was unreasonable. These assessments depend on the grounds available and the roles of the arrangements, not on hindsight alone. Likewise, an initially reasonable decision can be managed badly later without having been unreasonable from the start.

Practical commitment needs both settlement and an accurate account of what has been settled. A reason to leave the choice of project alone can coexist with a reason to restore its informational support. That restoration can require recovering a missed interval, and its need disappears when adequate independent grounds are already in place. The central mistake is not simply being insufficiently open. It is allowing the authority of a decision to answer a different question about whether the conditions for relying on that authority still hold. Understanding the difference makes room for a distinctive form of responsible persistence: changing how one keeps a resolution in order to keep it on adequate grounds.
