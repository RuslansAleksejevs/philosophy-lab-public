# Keeping a Way to Change Your Mind

Leah has decided to spend a year translating a neglected writer. The work is slow, and each difficult passage invites the thought that she would be happier doing something else. She knows this about herself. So she resolves not to ask, each working morning, whether the project is worth continuing. She will work on the translation.

That seems sensible. Now suppose that part of the collection may have been wrongly attributed. Leah's interest is specifically in this writer, and she would not choose the same project if most of the work turned out to be somebody else's. At the outset, she has good evidence for the attribution. She also knows that relevant research is continuing. A short bulletin provides reliable updates. Reading it once a month takes little time and seldom unsettles her. Keeping that route open is part of what makes her comfortable committing a year rather than a week.

Later, looking for ways to protect her concentration, Leah sets up a filter that excludes messages raising doubts about the collection. She continues to receive harmless bibliographical news. She knows how the filter works. She also knows that it removes the only practical route by which she would learn of a serious attribution problem in time to redirect her work. But she regards changing the filter as the first step toward changing her mind, and changing her mind as precisely what she resolved to resist. No contrary evidence has reached her, she says. There is no reason to reopen anything.

Something has gone wrong before any question about the attribution is settled. Leah might complete an excellent translation of an entirely authentic work. She might also remain perfectly capable of reconsidering if she were given a convincing reason. The defect lies in her use of commitment to protect an arrangement that prevents such a reason from reaching her.

My claim is that a resolution's authority to restrict reconsideration does not automatically extend to restricting the means of detecting its own exceptions. Where a usable warning route materially contributes to the justification of a persistence policy, knowingly disabling that route calls for repair or a fresh justification of the policy. It need not call for reconsidering the underlying project. Leah can have reason to change how she keeps her resolution while continuing to translate.

This is a limited claim. It does not say that every decision needs a review date, that all information should be welcomed, or that commitments must survive only while they win a continuously repeated comparison with alternatives. The problem is more specific: a plan can borrow its credibility from a safeguard and then invoke that credibility against maintaining the safeguard. I will argue that this is an identifiable failure of practical rationality, and that locating it correctly changes what the agent should do next.

We need first to understand what Leah's resolution gets right. A settled intention makes further action possible without requiring another decision at every step. Having chosen to translate, she can order reference books, allocate her mornings and develop a vocabulary. If these preparations had to await a fresh verdict about the value of translation, the choice would have settled very little. Stability is also useful when the problem is not the cost of thought but what thought predictably becomes. Frustration can make another project look attractive simply because its difficulties remain distant.

Richard Holton distinguishes weakness of will from acting against one's best judgment. On his account, a characteristic failure is revising certain intentions too readily. But he explicitly allows sensible revision when circumstances change, including changes never specified in advance. Persistence and stubbornness must therefore be distinguished by the quality of the tendencies governing revision, not just by whether the intention survives. [Holton, “Intention and Weakness of Will,” manuscript pp. 5–8.](https://web.mit.edu/holton/www/pubs/Weakness.pdf)

In “Rational Resolve,” Holton extends a two-tier strategy associated with Bratman's planning theory: a reasonable disposition to avoid reconsideration can help explain rational persistence. He expressly preserves monitoring, thresholds for reconsideration and responsiveness to new information. His account already contains the resources for distinguishing resistance from complete mental closure. [Holton, “Rational Resolve,” manuscript pp. 7–8, especially note 14.](https://web.mit.edu/holton/www/pubs/rational.resolve.a4.pdf)

So the point cannot be that resolution theorists forgot about exceptions. It concerns a further question. What may the resolution do to the processes through which an exception could become recognizable?

There are three different activities here. Leah can reconsider the project: should she continue translating this writer? She can monitor a relevant condition: has substantial evidence emerged that these works are not by this writer? And she can maintain a means of monitoring: is the bulletin still reaching her? These activities can overlap, but they need not. Restoring a blocked subscription does not require comparing the merits of translation with gardening. Nor does reading a brief report about attribution require reconsidering her entire allocation of the year.

This distinction matters because justification can differ between the activities. The expected instability of repeated project comparison may justify avoiding the first. It need not justify avoiding the second, and it may positively support the third. If a dependable warning route allows Leah to work without daily second thoughts, maintaining it serves the very stability she wants.

Consider a second version of Leah. She blocks advertisements for other courses, declines casual invitations to discuss alternative hobbies, and protects her morning from messages. But the monthly attribution bulletin remains accessible in the afternoon. Both versions resist distraction. Both might work at the same pace and reach the same result. Their arrangements nevertheless have different practical credentials. The second preserves something on which the case for sustained commitment depended. The first removes it while treating the permission to persist as unchanged.

The difference is not that one version has a defeater for the attribution belief and the other lacks it. By hypothesis, neither has received one. The difference is that one knowingly alters the basis on which she was entitled to leave the practical question alone. Notice also that the problem would remain if the filter were installed accidentally and Leah later discovered its effect. Deliberately blinding herself might invite further criticism, but the immediate reason to restore access depends on the damaged arrangement, not on assigning blame for its origin.

The relevant constraint can be stated without assuming that intentions themselves create new reasons for the intended action:

**If maintaining a route for detecting certain exceptions materially supports a policy of nonreconsideration, the authority of that policy does not by itself justify knowingly disabling the route. Once the loss is recognized, continuing with the same policy requires restoring adequate access or establishing that the policy remains justified without it.**

“Materially supports” does important work. The fact that a policy has imaginable exceptions is insufficient. When I choose a sandwich, I need not open an investigation into every discovery that might have altered my choice. Losing a source of marginally useful information need not disturb a reasonable decision. The constraint applies when the availability of the route makes a relevant difference to the case for the degree or duration of nonreconsideration adopted. Leah's bulletin matters in this way. Given the uncertainty and her purposes, she reasonably accepts a year of work with that safeguard; she has not accepted an unconditional year regardless of attribution.

Nor is merely losing information enough. The loss must be known, or supported by considerations the agent can reasonably recognize, before this argument gives her an immediate reason to respond. A wholly unsuspected technical fault need not make her management of the intention irrational by her lights. It may make the arrangement less reliable. Those are different assessments. The present case concerns someone who knows what her filter excludes and nevertheless treats her resolution as a sufficient answer.

Why accept the constraint? Start with what was justified. Leah did not endorse persistence under every possible informational arrangement. She endorsed a practice that combines resistance to familiar discouragement with access to relevant attribution research. The contribution of that access is prospective: it makes her less likely to devote much of the year to a project whose central point, as she understands it, has disappeared. That contribution helps justify declining to perform a fuller assessment every morning.

Disabling the bulletin changes the practice. It removes a benefit from the package whose merits warranted Leah's decision to restrict reconsideration. From the fact that the package was reasonable, nothing follows about the reasonableness of a version missing a material safeguard. She may be able to justify the latter version. Perhaps the cost of monitoring has greatly increased, the risk has diminished, or she has discovered that she values the translation independently of the writer's identity. But those would be further grounds. The mere fact that she resolved to persist supplies none of them.

Her proposed reply also contains a mistake about the significance of silence. No adverse report has reached her. Yet she now knows that adverse reports would not reach her. Silence cannot perform the reassuring role it played when she had reason to trust the route. This does not prove the attribution false or even make it less probable than it was at the outset. Her original evidence might remain strong. It does mean that the absence of a report cannot repair the loss of the arrangement on which her policy depended.

These two points should be kept apart. A person could avoid the mistaken inference from silence and still cling to an inadequately supported policy. Leah might say, accurately, “The empty inbox tells me nothing, but my old resolution tells me to carry on exactly as I am.” Her difficulty then is entirely practical. She is using a reason for maintaining a practice as though it were a reason for maintaining a relevantly altered practice. Explaining the defect does not require finding a false belief about the manuscript.

There is an obvious objection: this is just ordinary reasoning about costs and benefits. If the bulletin is useful enough, Leah should read it. Why attach the point to a theory of intention?

The objection is partly right. I am not proposing a new ultimate source of normativity that competes with the considerations supporting ordinary practical thought. The claim identifies a mistake about where the authority of a commitment applies. In this setting, ordinary reasoning faces a distinctive obstruction: Leah treats the request to repair her information arrangements as a request to reconsider the project, and treats the original intention as a reason to refuse both. Even if all the relevant standards ultimately have a familiar explanation, the misclassification matters. It gives the wrong practical response to the right general concern about instability.

Suppose someone tells Leah that she has been too closed-minded. She might respond by spending a week comparing the project with all her alternatives. She may become less able to finish anything while leaving the faulty filter untouched. What she first needs is much smaller: restore access to the bulletin. The repair addresses the defect without surrendering the benefit of having made a decision. If the bulletin brings a serious challenge, further assessment may follow. If it does not, she can continue under an arrangement whose justification has been restored.

That is a substantive difference in the object of a rational demand. Evidence that a way of persisting is defective is not automatically evidence that the intended action is a bad choice. A person can be right to continue a project, wrong to continue under her present rules of attention, and under no obligation to compare the project afresh with every alternative. Describing her simply as either steadfast or stubborn misses this combination.

The result also distinguishes two senses in which a commitment can remain open. It can leave the agent capable of reconsidering after a reason is presented. Or it can preserve a realistic possibility that an appropriate reason will be presented and admitted. The first can survive after the second disappears. Leah would listen to a convincing attribution argument if one were placed before her under suitable conditions. Yet she knowingly prevents the only available source of such an argument from reaching her. Her remaining ability is insufficient to preserve the specific justification of her arrangement.

This problem has a close epistemological relative. Alfonso Anaya argues that knowledge requires more than an actual absence of undefeated defeaters: agents must be appropriately prepared to address defeaters, and some circumstances require strategies for identifying information they ought to have. His discussion makes clear that sensitivity need not amount to constant active checking. [Anaya, “‘Absent Contrary Indication,’” §§3–4.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-021-00455-0)

The practical constraint defended here does not follow simply by replacing “belief” with “intention” in a condition for knowledge. It is possible to act reasonably without knowing that one's project is best, and permissible practical risks need not match risks compatible with knowledge. The argument instead starts with the particular grounds on which an agent restricts her own reconsideration. It asks whether those grounds survive the restrictions she subsequently imposes on access. This allows substantial, justified ignorance. Its distinctive consequence is a reason to repair the policy's informational support that can arise without a reason to reverse the action it governs.

A more forceful objection is that the proposed separation cannot be maintained. Temptation does not introduce itself honestly. Reading about attribution may give Leah an excuse to quit. Every check becomes another opportunity to transform discouragement into apparently respectable doubt. The bulletin's present innocence gives no guarantee about what it will do when she is frustrated.

No such guarantee is available. There is also no guarantee that an additional intention to monitor sensibly will be obeyed. Alida Liberman's criticism of higher-order intention accounts is relevant here: pressure against the original intention can also become pressure against an intention not to reconsider it. Adding another level does not automatically settle the difficulty. [Liberman, “Reconsidering Resolutions,” §§4–6.](https://www.jesp.org/paper/36346f73-90e0-81a9-b63b-ec1ee6d50946)

The constraint therefore specifies something a policy needs to achieve in the relevant cases, not a psychological mechanism certain to achieve it. A monthly bulletin might be better than a continuous debate. A trusted reader might summarize findings without presenting distracting alternatives. Leah might check information outside the frustrating hours in which she is most inclined to abandon the work. These are possible arrangements, not empirical prescriptions. Their adequacy depends on the person and the circumstances.

The independence they seek is limited. The warning route need not be external to the agent, emotionally neutral or indifferent to the project. A colleague who passionately wants Leah to succeed could provide it. What matters is that the instruction to keep going must not, by itself, be sufficient to disqualify a signal from a source whose availability helped justify keeping going. Otherwise the safeguard depends for its operation on permission from the very policy it is supposed to qualify.

For example, “Do not abandon the project merely because a difficult passage is unpleasant” leaves room for serious attribution evidence. “Treat every report that might make you abandon the project as another expression of that unpleasantness” does not. The second rule classifies a report by its possible practical effect while pretending to classify it by the reliability of its source. A report can make quitting tempting and still reveal a fact that Leah has independent reason to regard as relevant. The desire to protect the project cannot alone resolve that distinction.

This is compatible with developing a more resolute character. Abigail Bruxvoort argues that responding rationally to temptation requires attention to cultivated patterns of desire and deliberation, and questions whether a supposedly neutral higher-order comparison can protect us. [Bruxvoort, “Resolution and Resolve,” especially §3.](https://www.jesp.org/paper/36346f73-90e0-817b-a38d-c4640dffbdb5) Nothing here requires such a neutral standpoint. The capacity at issue might itself be cultivated: learning to hear a specific kind of warning without turning every warning into a referendum on one's life. A disposition to distinguish those activities would improve both stability and responsiveness.

What if no workable separation is available? Perhaps every accessible way of receiving the warning would undermine Leah's concentration so severely that the project would fail. Then the case for a protected year must be assessed with that limitation included. It might still be reasonable to undertake the year without monitoring. Alternatively, Leah might choose a shorter commitment, a different project, or the risk of reconsideration. The constraint does not settle which. It rules out counting a safeguard among the reasons that make the commitment acceptable while dismissing its loss solely because the commitment has already been made.

This limitation is essential. We sometimes reasonably close off opportunities to reconsider. A person who has chosen a career need not subscribe to a constant stream of advertisements for other careers. Attention to every attractive possibility can prevent a life from acquiring shape. Nor must Leah preserve an exception for every consideration that might count against her project. Her decision may legitimately settle how much of some costs she is prepared to bear. A commitment can include accepting foreseeable hardship, passing up better offers or tolerating a risk that later information would have removed.

The question is whether the informational restriction belongs to the commitment as reasonably justified, or whether it is added while continuing to rely on a justification that presupposed its absence. A decision made with eyes open to a certain uncertainty differs from a decision defended by invoking a protection against that uncertainty which the agent has knowingly removed. This difference concerns what the agent has grounds to accept. It does not grant the earlier self a permanent authority to dictate which costs matter.

In particular, Leah need not remain interested in attribution forever. She may discover that the translation itself has become valuable regardless of authorship. That can support a different policy of persistence. But the change needs some basis beyond “I am committed, therefore the conditions that made my commitment reasonable no longer matter.” New purposes can change the relevance of a safeguard. Mere persistence does not show that such a change has occurred.

A further objection threatens a regress. If a monitoring route must remain usable, must Leah monitor the route? Must she then monitor the means of monitoring it? Eventually the effort would consume the year.

The requirement does not begin from the possibility of every failure. It begins from a recognized loss of support for a particular policy. Leah already knows that the filter removes her bulletin. Nothing in the argument requires her to prove, before every working session, that no other part of her environment has failed. She may rely on ordinary, reasonably dependable arrangements without auditing their entire causal history. If evidence of another failure arrives, it can generate a further reason to respond. The indefinite possibility of further reasons is not a present demand to search for all of them.

This is why the proposal concerns adequate access, not an infallible warning system. An occasional delay may leave the justification of a year-long project intact. An unavailable journal may have an adequate substitute. A safeguard can degrade somewhat without ceasing to play its relevant role. When uncertainty about its condition is itself serious enough to undermine reliance, assessment may be required. But the same considerations of cost, timing and purpose that limited the original demand also limit the assessment. There is no instruction to pursue certainty at every level.

Computer planning research offers a useful comparison. Veloso, Pollack and Cox distinguish selecting relevant features to monitor, deliberating about a detected change and transforming a plan. Their monitors draw on reasons for planning decisions, including alternatives not selected. [“Rationale-Based Monitoring for Planning in Dynamic Environments,” pp. 172–173.](https://cdn.aaai.org/AIPS/1998/AIPS98-021.pdf) This is already a developed way to avoid monitoring everything. My proposal neither introduces that architecture nor claims to improve its implementation. It asks what happens to a normative permission to avoid deliberation when exercising that permission undermines the monitoring on which it relied.

The answer is not that every subsequent action becomes irrational. Suppose Leah notices the blocked bulletin just as she reaches the end of a paragraph. It could be perfectly reasonable to finish the paragraph before repairing the filter. The appropriate timing depends on what the delay risks, how much disruption repair causes and how soon the information could matter. Nor does the argument show that her original decision was irrational. If the safeguard was available then, the original decision might have been entirely sound. Its history explains both why stability was warranted and why a later change in the arrangement requires attention.

These distinctions let us diagnose the successful case as well as the disastrous one. If no attribution problem ever arises, the careless Leah's translation can succeed despite the defect in her policy. The defect is not inferred from a counterfactual bad outcome alone. It lies in her already knowing that she has removed a material support for her chosen degree of nonreconsideration, while declining either to replace that support or to assess whether she can reasonably do without it. Finishing the translation does not supply the missing earlier grounds. Success can show that a risk did not materialize without showing that the risk was responsibly accepted.

Conversely, a Leah who preserves the bulletin may receive a misleading report, investigate it and lose some productive time. That does not show that preserving access was a mistake. Whether she managed her intention reasonably depends on the grounds available and the arrangement's expected role, not on whether every exercise of it improves the outcome. The value of settling a question and the value of keeping a warning route open are both assessed under ordinary limits of foresight.

Practical commitment therefore requires a more discriminating ideal than either continual openness or sheer resistance. An agent can protect a settled answer while keeping open a question about the conditions under which that answer may continue to guide her. In the cases considered here, those two activities support one another. Leah's reason to stop asking whether to translate can be a reason to keep receiving the bulletin.

The philosophical gain is a restriction on the reach of an intention's authority. Reasons for leaving a practical question settled do not automatically govern the maintenance of the arrangements that make leaving it settled reasonable. When those arrangements fail, the next rational move can be to repair them while continuing the work. Some changes in how we hold an intention are part of holding it responsibly. A resolution worthy of persistence may require us to protect a way of learning when its persistence has ceased to be warranted.
