# Essay 52: initial question and contribution plan

Philosophical question and proposal below were chosen by this author before this essay's literature search. Provisional title: **Keeping a Way to Change Your Mind**.

## Chosen question

Can a policy of resisting reconsideration be rational when following it predictably damages the agent's ability to discover the circumstances in which reconsideration would be appropriate?

An intention can rationally spare us repeated deliberation. A resolution may also protect us against the predictable effects of temptation on judgment. But a commitment can alter the informational environment from which its own exceptions would have to become recognizable. A person pursuing a demanding project might reject every conversation in which its feasibility could be questioned. A person committed to finishing a physical challenge might treat all discomfort as the kind of temptation the resolution was designed to withstand. These policies do more than decide how to respond to admitted evidence: they shape what will count as evidence in the first place.

The provisional contribution is a conditional constraint on rational nonreconsideration. If an agent relies on there being appropriate exceptions to a policy of persistence, and the policy foreseeably obstructs recognition of those exceptions, the agent must either maintain an adequately independent route for detecting them or give up the claim that the policy has that conditional justification. The proposal concerns a plan's own justification, rather than a general duty to acquire all useful information. A possible stronger result is that some apparent steadfastness defeats itself even when no bad outcome occurs, because its practical justification presupposes access to a distinction its operation erases.

This is not yet a demonstrated novel thesis. Primary-source reading will test whether it adds anything to familiar claims about reasonable reconsideration, resolutions, deliberation costs, and monitoring. The essay must identify the extra inferential step and its limits rather than rebrand ordinary prudence.

## Initial argument and objection plan

1. Distinguish reconsidering whether to act, noticing a defeater, and maintaining the conditions for noticing a defeater.
2. Construct paired cases in which a commitment policy is equally effective at suppressing temptation but differs in its preservation of defeater recognition.
3. Explain what warrants a persistence policy in these cases, then ask whether its own operation removes a necessary condition of that warrant.
4. Defend an appropriately limited requirement of independence: the warning channel need not be neutral about the project, infallible, external, or continuously deliberative.
5. Address monitoring costs, inevitable trade-offs, a regress of monitoring the monitor, and commitments whose point includes refusing certain opportunities or reconsiderations altogether.
6. Clarify whether the requirement is reducible to ordinary prospective cost-benefit reasoning, and what it contributes if it is a specification of planning rationality rather than a new ultimate norm.

## Rejected initial angles

- **A general authority of the past self over the present self.** This would assume rather than explain the temporal authority at issue, and would risk making ordinary changes of mind look morally suspect. Retain the issue as background, not as a premise.
- **All intentions should contain mandatory review dates.** Too crude: time alone need not produce relevant information; automatic review can recreate exactly the instability resolutions address. Appropriate detection can be event-sensitive, and some inexpensive intentions need no special provision.
- **Commitment has value only because reconsideration is costly.** Too narrow as an initial conclusion; resolutions may address motivated changes of judgment even when deliberation consumes little time. Treat this as a rival to examine, not an established claim.
