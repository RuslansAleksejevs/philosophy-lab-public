## Reconstruction and concrete merits

The central argument has four steps. First, content, the reasonableness of earlier action, and permission to impose a consequence answer different questions (lines 13–21). Second, soliciting work under public directions creates a relationship in which the issuer owes attention to the opportunities its guidance supplied (lines 23–29). Third, a reasonable ambiguity complaint therefore places a justificatory burden on retrospective enforcement: show adequate prior opportunity or risk allocation, repair the procedure, or supply some further justification (lines 31–39). Finally, when participants compete, repair must consider how changing one person's eligibility changes the comparison faced by others (lines 45–57). The account deliberately leaves ordinary semantics and the authority to clarify intact.

Several features deserve preservation. The concession at line 15 is particularly effective: even granting determinate narrow content does not settle the practical question. This avoids depending on the weak inference from a speaker's dated private intention to the content of a public announcement. The discussion at lines 35–39 also separates a possible reading from a reasonable investment based on that reading. This makes room for neglected specifications and knowingly assumed uncertainty without automatically blaming any recipient who misunderstood.

The printmaker at line 47 improves the argument substantially. A remedy can change the competition faced by someone who was never excluded. Line 49 then correctly distinguishes incompatible requirements from nested sets of compliant products. That correction prevents an artificial logical paradox from taking the place of the real practical conflict. Lines 59–65 likewise avoid manufacturing opposition to Kukla, Lewis, or Stalnaker. Those concessions should survive revision; the paper does not need a sweeping attack on semantics to have a worthwhile contribution.

These merits fit the assigned profile's concern with identifying the target and preserving distinctions between nearby questions. In Mandelkern and Linzen's [*Do Language Models' Words Refer?*, pp. 1–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.05576), a challenge to one argument against reference is expressly distinguished from a decisive positive argument for reference. Holliday and Mandelkern's [*The Orthologic of Epistemic Modals*, pp. 2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02872) distinguish truth preservation from preservation of rational acceptance and ask what discriminating tests each target supports. ## 1. The normative bridge does not yet settle what an issuer may enforce

**Locations:** lines 25–33, especially “a deficiency in one participant's responsiveness,” “Such treatment misdescribes the basis on which the adverse decision is deserved,” and the two alternatives at line 31; also the statement of the thesis at line 9.

The draft moves between two strengths of conclusion. The modest conclusion says that semantic correctness alone does not settle all normative questions. The stronger conclusion appears to make reasonable access to the requirement, procedural repair, or a further justification a condition of using nonconformity against an entrant. The second does not follow merely from the first. Nor does showing that recipients were responsive to the available guidance establish that the competition must assess their responsiveness to guidance.

Consider the strongest rival within the example. The centre says: “The prize is for a work meeting the original artistic restriction. We fully accept that the photographs were reasonable responses to our inadequate notice. We are responsible for that inadequate notice and owe an appropriate response. But excluding a nonqualifying work does not assess the photographer's blameworthiness or attentiveness.” On this account, an eligibility rule is partly constitutive of the activity being offered. It is not a test of whether entrants exercised their agency badly. The standard may matter even when the work could physically be exhibited and the centre could afford other arrangements. This is therefore a stronger rival than the machine and resource exceptions at line 33.

The essay should not simply accept the rival. An institution's authority to constitute the activity may itself depend on adequately announcing its terms. But that is the contested normative bridge requiring defense. The claim at line 27 that the duty follows from recruiting agency assumes too quickly that a defect in the resulting work is being treated as a defect in the entrant's response. A centre can distinguish these throughout and still insist on the original standard. It might owe compensation, apology, another opportunity, or some combination while retaining permission to exclude from this particular award.

The phrase “independent justification” could already accommodate that answer. If so, say precisely what must be independent of what. A prior legitimate rule plus a finding of actual nonconformity is not a naked semantic fact. Does the rule's legitimacy itself answer the complaint, or does invited reasonable reliance defeat its ordinary authority until more has been done? Without an answer, the constraint risks oscillating between an unobjectionable demand to consider everything relevant and a substantial restriction on enforcement that the argument has not yet earned.

**Revision test:** Give a determinate verdict on a version in which the centre explicitly disavows blame, accepts responsibility for its inadequate guidance, can fund repair, and still claims that the artistic restriction constitutes the prize it offered. State separately whether it may exclude, what it owes the photographer, and whether the second answer changes the first. Then identify the additional premise that yields those answers. This can be done without a complete theory of institutional authority. A carefully delimited answer is enough; simply repeating that meaning cannot settle fairness is not.

**Grounding:** The objection's distinction between defeating an inference and establishing the stronger alternative is profile-grounded. The rival account of eligibility, and the demand to separate blame from enforceability and repair, are this reviewer's general philosophical assessment. They are not attributed to Mandelkern.

## 2. The comparative case needs a specified protected interest and baseline

**Locations:** lines 45–53, especially “adds a constraint,” the printmaker's discarded design at line 47, and “Treating the situation as a collection of separate compensation claims misses the relation”; line 57's proposed evidence of material influence.

The printmaker case shows why cash reimbursement of materials is not automatically adequate. It does not yet establish that an individual-reliance account misses the relevant relation. Such an account can recognize losses of opportunity, disrupted plans, or justified expectations concerning the field of competitors. Each person's protected interest can have relational content. The fact that a remedy for one person affects another then becomes a coordination problem for that account, rather than evidence that a further kind of claim has been discovered.

This matters because the cited reliance discussion is already richer than reimbursement of expenditures. In [Kolodny and Wallace, *Promises and Practices Revisited*, pp. 126–130](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~ngkolodny/PandPR.pdf), the loss-prevention principle is not restricted to out-of-pocket loss, and their discussion of assurance expressly concerns the ability to plan and coordinate activities. These passages do not settle the arts-centre case. They do, however, make it important to confront a version of reliance protection that includes the actual opportunity the invitation induced entrants to pursue.

The draft also needs to specify that opportunity. A common restriction on available techniques, an equal chance of winning, and a reasonable opportunity to present one's strongest eligible work are different goods. The fact that an instruction materially shaped a work does not alone show that the resulting comparison is unfair. Entrants routinely arrive at a competition with different practical constraints. Here, a more promising distinguishing fact may be that the invitation gave each entrant reason to organize work around a supposedly common restriction. The essay should defend that fact's normative significance rather than letting the existence of different production histories do all the work.

The nested readings make the baseline particularly important. Under an explicitly narrow announcement, neither group could use grey. Under an explicitly broad announcement, both could. Both are coherent repaired worlds, but they protect different counterfactual plans. Why should a particular entrant's loss be measured against one rather than the other? The correctness of the original public content might have some bearing on this choice, even though it cannot make a previously reasonable response unreasonable. That possibility links this objection to the next without collapsing them.

**Revision test:** Compare the actual case with one in which the printmaker knowingly chooses a self-imposed restriction under a clearly broad rule, holding the finished artworks and expenditures fixed. Explain the relevant difference without assuming that every different production constraint creates unfairness. Then state what the printmaker was entitled to expect about the common competitive setting, specify a baseline for at least one proposed repair, and explain why that repair protects the interest at stake. Finally, test the proposal against a reliance account that already includes opportunity loss and expectations about others. If no verdict separates them, present the contribution as a useful extension or specification of that account. That can still be philosophically valuable.

**Grounding:** Holding comparison criteria steady and asking for a discriminating case are profile-grounded methodological demands. The competing accounts of reliance and fairness are generic assessment, supported here by an independently checked passage of a source already used by the author. No claim is made that Mandelkern favors one theory of fairness.

## 3. The “correct reading” removal test has an unstable evidential role

**Location:** line 55, beginning “This yields a useful test”; relevant qualifications at lines 15 and 65.

The proposed test asks whether a remedy still seems adequate when we stop calling one reading correct while retaining evidence about what participants could reasonably know and how they acted. There are two importantly different operations this could describe.

If we retain the actual content and its legitimate normative consequences, changing only which label we use, the exercise is a useful check for rhetorical bias. But a changed reaction does not establish that the remedy was unjustified. It shows that the presentation affected a judgment. Conversely, if we exclude the original content from the practical evaluation while holding only ex-ante information and conduct fixed, the exercise may remove a relevant consideration by assumption. Original content could matter to what was offered or to the printmaker's justified expectations about the terms of comparison, without mattering to whether the photographer was blameworthy.

The draft's own three-question distinction makes room for this possibility. Independence of historical reasonableness from a later clarification does not entail independence of the justified remedy from actual content. The ending rightly says that interpretive correctness does not settle the practical result by itself. The proposed diagnostic must not silently test the stronger claim that correctness has no bearing on that result.

**Revision test:** Distinguish a version that changes only evaluative labels from a version that changes the stipulated actual rule while preserving the same earlier audience evidence. In the latter, allow one version's original content to be narrow and the other's broad. Ask separately about prior reasonable conduct and present remedial claims. Explain which answers must stay fixed and which may change. If only the first set must stay fixed, restrict the diagnostic to uses of correctness that purport to discredit earlier reasonable reliance. The test would then have a clear target and would not decide the rival theory of remedial authority in advance.

**Grounding:** This is the most directly profile-grounded objection: a diagnostic needs a stated target and a controlled substitution. The modal paper motivates that methodological demand, not the specific ethical conclusion. No formal semantics, nonclassical logic, or linguistic experiment is required for this normative essay.
