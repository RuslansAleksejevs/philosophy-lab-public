# When an Institution Must Stop Speaking for a Claim

A museum attributes a portrait to the painter Mara Venn. The attribution resulted from careful research. Years later, the researchers have left, but the museum still presents the catalogue as the place to learn its curators’ current assessment. Behind the scenes, something has broken. The archive office preserves research files. A specialist committee assesses questions referred to it. The publications office changes entries after receiving a decision. Nobody is responsible for getting challenges from the archive office to the committee. Each office reasonably assumes that this happens elsewhere.

Suppose the attribution is true. No contrary evidence has yet appeared. The surviving files are excellent. What, if anything, is wrong with the museum’s continued endorsement?

It would be too quick to say that the museum no longer knows who painted the portrait. It would be equally quick to say that everything is epistemically in order until someone discovers an error. These answers overlook a third possibility: the museum can retain good grounds for the attribution while losing its entitlement to offer that attribution as the maintained answer of an ongoing inquiry.

I will defend a limited principle. When an institution invites people to rely on its continuing expert oversight of a question, it must preserve a reasonable route by which a serious challenge could alter its public endorsement, or disclose that the oversight has ended. Losing this route need not justify retracting the proposition. It can instead require retiring the endorsement: preserving the earlier finding while withdrawing the invitation to treat it as an answer for which the institution is currently providing expert oversight.

The distinction matters because testimony does more than move propositions between heads. It also distributes the work of finding out whether those propositions remain worth accepting.

Consider three things the museum might be doing. It might preserve a historical assertion: “Our 2008 catalogue attributed the portrait to Venn.” It might retain a belief: its curators still believe that Venn painted it. Or it might offer a maintained answer: researchers are invited to treat its current catalogue as the institution’s continuing expert response to the attribution question. These activities can coincide, but they need not. A library can preserve the catalogue without endorsing its findings. Curators can retain an attribution while acknowledging that they no longer have the staff to oversee challenges to it. An institution can also oversee a question through a division of labour in which no individual has mastered all the evidence.

That last possibility is indispensable. Hardwig argues that rational dependence on others reaches into expert inquiry itself: research can require contributions that no single participant can reproduce or assess in full. Requiring every curator or catalogue editor to possess the entire case would mistake the problem. The museum needs an adequate division of epistemic labour, rather than a collection of self-sufficient investigators. [Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” pp. 335–340, 345–349](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523).

What is distinctive about maintaining an answer is the form of dependence it invites. The researcher consulting the catalogue may reasonably leave certain tasks to the museum: recognizing a credible attribution challenge, connecting it to the relevant work, and deciding whether the published assessment needs qualification. The researcher need not receive a personal promise that every new discovery will be found. Nevertheless, where the museum presents itself as exercising this continuing role, its word supports a particular allocation of attention. Some checking may reasonably be left undone by the researcher because the institution is positioned to do it.

The argument for a continuity requirement begins with that allocation. First, the museum invites reliance on an activity that extends beyond preserving an earlier verdict. Second, the invitation is epistemically credible only if the museum has reasonable arrangements for performing that activity. Third, those arrangements must connect possible challenges to possible changes in what the museum tells people. A system that can receive a challenge but cannot bring it before anyone able to affect the answer does not provide the oversight on which it invites reliance. Therefore, the museum must maintain the connection or narrow the invitation.

The second step deserves emphasis. A truthful answer can be a poor basis for a particular kind of reliance. A stopped clock sometimes gives the correct time, but this does not license treating it as an instrument that continues to track the time. The museum is unlike a stopped clock in one crucial respect: its earlier research may remain excellent evidence for its claim. Still, that evidence does not by itself establish that the museum continues to perform the role on which a researcher is being invited to depend. The truth and evidential strength of the attribution cannot substitute for an absent activity.

This does not make every current assertion an undertaking to monitor the world. I can tell you what I saw yesterday without assuming responsibility for investigating it tomorrow. Nor does printing “current” on a document automatically create unlimited duties. The relevant question is what the institution gives its audience reasonable grounds to leave to it. A dated report offered as a completed investigation ordinarily licenses less continuing deference than a reference service presented as the institution’s maintained expert assessment. Where conventions are unclear, the institution may need to specify the scope of its role. An audience’s wish for perpetual supervision does not establish that supervision was offered.

The continuity requirement has three connected parts. There must be some reasonable way for relevant challenges to enter the institution’s attention; some competent assessment of their bearing on the maintained answer; and some authorized route from that assessment to a change in the endorsement. The parts may be distributed across many people. No individual need own the whole process. But the institution must own their connection.

“Reasonable” is doing substantive work here. The institution need not discover every unpublished objection, answer every hostile message, or reopen a settled question whenever someone repeats a refuted doubt. Its arrangements should address the credible challenges that its advertised role makes it reasonable to expect it to handle, at an appropriate pace. An annual review can be adequate for one subject and absurd for another. A specialist may refer a challenge to another body. What matters is whether the referral reaches a functioning assessment and can affect the answer. Merely adding another address to a chain of referrals does not discharge the task.

Suppose two museums possess identical evidence and publish the same true attribution. In the first, incoming challenges can reach a qualified curator whose judgment can change the entry. In the second, the route has quietly disappeared. Before any challenge arrives, their evidence for the attribution can be equally good. Their entitlement to present themselves as performing continuing oversight is already different. The difference concerns their capacity to respond under circumstances relevant to the role, rather than the truth of their present answer.

Temporary interruption is compatible with that capacity. A curator need not answer a letter while asleep, and a museum may have a normal delay between receipt and review. The defect in the second museum is that the relevant work has no functioning route under its ordinary arrangements. Waiting will not bring the answer within reach of criticism. Some change in the arrangements is required.

This is an anticipatory requirement. It applies before the institution encounters a reason to reject the attribution. Familiar duties to correct known errors would remain dormant at precisely this point. They cannot by themselves explain why the second museum should act now.

Accounts of testimony that emphasize trust provide part of the explanation. Townsend defends an account on which collective testimony involves a commitment to epistemic trustworthiness, rather than merely the production of reliable sentences. He also distinguishes accepting a group’s invitation to believe it from using its words as evidence. That distinction allows us to see how an old report can retain evidential value even when a particular relation of reliance is no longer available. [Townsend, “The Epistemology of Collective Testimony,” pp. 187–192, 203–208](https://doi.org/10.1515/jso-2019-0044).

But a commitment needs a structure through which it can be fulfilled. An institution may remain exposed to criticism, and may sincerely care about being trustworthy, while lacking the connection that would let a competent objection change its answer. My proposal specifies one condition of credibility for a particular continuing commitment. General willingness to be held accountable does not settle who can notice a challenge, assess it, and act on the result.

The appropriate response to a broken connection can therefore be neither reaffirmation nor retraction. The museum might say: “This attribution records the conclusion of the 2008 investigation. We retain that conclusion, but no longer provide continuing specialist review of it.” This statement does not erase the investigation, declare its evidence defective, or assert that someone else painted the picture. It withdraws a particular reason for leaving further inquiry to the museum.

Retraction literature already makes room for important distinctions in this vicinity. Kukla argues that retraction is a social act whose success cannot be reduced to privately changing one’s mind. Discussing a scientist’s withdrawal of assurance, Kukla also allows that an earlier statement can remain usable as evidence. I therefore do not claim to discover that withdrawal of assurance and loss of evidence come apart. The proposed advance is to identify a prospective institutional condition whose failure can require an explicit change in endorsement even when nobody has changed their mind about the proposition. [Kukla, “The Ethics of Retraction,” pp. 3–6, 13–15](https://www.qeios.com/read/PDR3QV).

There are three serious objections.

The first appeals to memory. People often retain knowledge after forgetting how they acquired it. If an institution can likewise preserve knowledge in records and routines, why demand a continuing connection to possible challenges?

The answer is that the demand does not attach to retaining knowledge as such. The museum need not discard its belief, recover every researcher’s reasons, or recreate its original investigation. It must accurately represent the kind of epistemic work it continues to offer. A retired expert may remember a well-established conclusion perfectly while acknowledging that she no longer follows developments in the field. That acknowledgment does not destroy her knowledge. It can still matter to someone deciding whether to rely on her as a current guide. The proposed institutional disclosure makes the same distinction.

Indeed, forcing a choice between full oversight and total withdrawal would waste knowledge. Institutions would either imply more than they provide or remove valuable findings. Retiring an endorsement permits useful evidence to survive the end of a service. It also avoids suggesting that an unreviewed claim is probably false. The appropriate degree of confidence in the attribution remains a question about the evidence; the disclosure answers a different question about where to place the work of further inquiry.

The second objection is that this is merely an ethical duty to keep promises, rather than an epistemic requirement. If the museum promised oversight, its failure is straightforward. If it did not, perhaps there is no problem.

An explicit promise would make the ethical case easier, but it is not essential. Epistemic roles can be communicated through established practices of presenting and endorsing answers. A researcher can reasonably be invited to defer without anyone uttering a promise. The defect is epistemic in this specific sense: the institution presents itself as a suitable place to locate part of the work of inquiry when its arrangements cannot perform that work. It need not thereby cause anyone financial harm or induce a false belief. Even if everyone luckily reaches the truth, the division of inquiry it offers is defective.

This conclusion leaves room for different theories of justification. An audience unaware of the broken arrangement might still be blameless, rational, or knowledgeable. The institution might itself retain a justified belief. The requirement concerns its entitlement to solicit the specified continuing reliance. It does not require the stronger claim that a procedural failure defeats every belief acquired from its testimony.

The third objection concerns collective responsibility. If each member acts reasonably within a reasonable division of labour, where can the fault be?

The case distinguishes an institution’s failure from a person’s culpability. Each office may have inherited a sensible task. Preservation, assessment and publication are all necessary. Changes of personnel may leave no one aware that the connection between them has vanished. It does not follow that their combined arrangements perform the role the institution continues to claim. Local adequacy does not establish adequacy of the connections between tasks.

No mysterious collective mind is needed to describe the defect. We need only identify the institution that authorizes the endorsement, the practices through which it invites reliance, and the arrangements by which challenges could affect those practices. Its members may initially deserve no blame for the gap. Once discovered, however, the gap identifies something to repair or disclose. The point of locating responsibility at the institutional level is to prevent every office’s reasonable answer—“That is outside our assigned task”—from counting as an adequate answer to the researcher’s question about who, if anyone, is doing the work.

Disclosure itself must reach the practice of endorsement. Quietly changing an internal staffing document while continuing to present the catalogue in the same way would leave the relevant invitation untouched. Here Kukla’s attention to uptake is helpful: normative changes in public speech do not necessarily succeed merely because someone announces them somewhere. The institution must make the changed scope reasonably apparent where it solicits reliance. This requires a credible change in presentation, not a guarantee that every past reader receives a personal notice.

Public context also matters. Almagro, Bordonaba-Plou and Villanueva investigate how public and private settings affect judgments about retraction. Their study does not test the present continuity principle. It does reinforce the methodological reason to avoid assuming that a passing private remark and an institution’s maintained public answer carry identical expectations. [“Retraction in Public Settings,” abstract and §1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-023-04348-3).

The museum example is fictional, and the principle leaves difficult boundaries unsettled: what level of review a role reasonably implies, how much notice retiring it requires, and when a successor institution inherits it. These boundaries should constrain applications rather than encourage a universal duty to keep every assertion under permanent review.

Still, the central distinction is useful. A collective can preserve the fruits of inquiry while ceasing to be an adequate place to leave further inquiry. Its accepted answer may remain true, well-supported and worth learning. What it must give up is the invitation to rely on oversight it no longer provides. Institutions should be able to stop that work. They should also make it possible for others to know when the work has stopped.
