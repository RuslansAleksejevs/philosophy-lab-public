# Independent critique of “Who May Say the Review Is Finished?”

Essay 53; reviewer 1; 12 September 2026 UTC. Assessment: revise and retain the central project. The draft isolates a worthwhile form of organizational participation, but its most distinctive contrast—an unanswered objection versus an objection answered badly—needs a sharper defense before the proposed duty is secure.

The argument deserves a charitable reconstruction. A credible, serious objection can generate a requirement for treatment independently of an organization's assignment chart. Dividing work does not by itself defeat that requirement. Some local completion acts also serve as contributions to an authorization to proceed. A worker who understands that function, knows the concern remains outstanding, and has reasonable alternatives therefore owes something beyond an accurate report of her specialist work. She must preserve a qualification, arrange a suitable transfer, or obtain a disposition. This does not require her to settle the technical question or make her responsible for the whole organizational outcome. The proposed contribution is a bounded specification of what responsible participation in closure requires, not a new general theory of blame.

Several features are particularly good. The common-knowledge stipulation at draft line 7 makes the challenge survive complete factual transparency. Lines 35–41 distinguish moving information from transferring an obligation, and the Monday/Friday/Tuesday example shows why an accepted handoff can still fail to govern the action it should inform. Lines 47–49 limit responsibility through the actual function of the worker's act and reasonable knowledge of that function. The safe-outcome comparison at lines 55–57 locates a possible wrong before injury occurs. The draft also explicitly allows justified risk-taking and does not make the objector's satisfaction necessary. These are substantive distinctions worth preserving. The direct prose generally makes their significance easy to assess.

1. **Clarify which standing closure changes, and supply the bridge to this worker's duty.** The relevant locations are lines 15–31, 53–59, and 75. Line 21 says the objection's standing depends on a moral standard external to institutional rules. Lines 25–27 argue that an allocation of work cannot defeat its force. Yet the account subsequently describes the worker as removing the need for anyone to answer, withdrawing a requirement, and helping terminate an obligation. If the requirement is the external moral requirement, the very argument offered explains why her click cannot terminate it. If it is an internally recognized procedural requirement, the opening case does not clearly establish that such a requirement ever existed: the agency's known rule already allows completion after the three allocated checks, and nobody is assigned the additional question.

The text is not simply oblivious to this distinction: line 59 expressly locates the terminated obligation within the agency's practice. The problem is that the distinction is doing essential work without a stable account of its two sides. Perhaps the worker removes a practical opportunity to secure compliance with a continuing moral duty. Perhaps she invokes an internal permission that lacks moral authority. Perhaps she exercises a legitimate delegated power subject to moral conditions. These explanations need not produce identical duties. In particular, the fact that passengers are still owed treatment by someone does not alone establish that every participant who can delay implementation acquires this particular duty. The change from a single planner to divided workers preserves the objection's force, but it changes who controls what. The argument needs to explain why knowingly supplying this authorization, rather than merely having an opportunity to help, attaches the remaining requirement to this worker.

The strongest repair would identify the continuing moral demand, the institutionally recognized transition, and the premise connecting this agent's contribution to that transition. It can be a modest premise about responsible exercise of a specified power; no comprehensive theory of institutional normativity is required. Then use the same distinctions in the safe-outcome case. The wrong may be leaving a demand unsatisfied while enabling action, rather than extinguishing the demand itself. This is a substantive clarification because it determines the scope of the duty, not just a request to replace a metaphor.

Revision test: compare two invented agencies with the same warning and implementation rule. One explicitly gives all serious new concerns temporary blocking status until disposition; the other never recognizes that status, though its workers know the moral reason for assessment. State whether the same closure duty exists in both, what exactly each worker changes, and why the relevant difference does or does not matter. If the answer depends on a recommended institutional practice, mark that recommendation separately from the diagnosis of what current acts accomplish. The attention to actual institutional effects is profile-informed; the normative grounding objection is general philosophical assessment.

2. **The distinction between no answer and a bad answer threatens to collapse in the stipulated central case.** Compare lines 7, 27 and 59 with lines 67–71. In the opening, all participants know access is unassessed and regard its omission from assigned work as enough to proceed. Later, an explicit supervisor decision to prioritize a small saving is said to end the disappearance-through-an-assignment-gap problem, even though the reason is morally objectionable. That limited claim is sensible: a bad substantive decision and an evasive failure to decide can be different wrongs. But why does the original shared acceptance of the assignment rule not already count as a bad answer? The participants appear to endorse a policy about the very uncertainty at issue: proceed because the concern falls outside the prescribed review. The policy is objectionable, but the supervisor's reason is objectionable too.

One response is that only the supervisor knowingly takes ownership of the outstanding decision. If so, explain what taking ownership consists in and why it is absent from the original, fully informed completion. Merely repeating that an assignment gap is an insufficient reason does not draw the distinction, since insufficient substantive reasons are allowed to constitute answers in line 71. Nor can explicit verbalization alone draw it. The favored positive procedure would then be vulnerable to its own criticism of ceremonial risk acceptance at line 69: an organization could insert the concern's name and a cost-saving formula into each file without changing how anyone considers or owns the decision.

I am not asking the essay to show that an explicit bad answer removes every further duty; it already denies that. I am asking what discriminates the narrower wrong it does remove. This is especially important because the essay intentionally rules out misunderstanding as the explanation. In a case with full common knowledge, institutional acceptance of an assignment-based reason can itself be a perfectly identifiable, though unjustified, practical decision.

Revision test: contrast a supervisor who says that the disclosed access concern will receive no assessment because it was not assigned, with one who says it will receive no assessment to save a small sum. Hold fixed acknowledgment, authority, ability to qualify, attribution of the decision, and the eventual action. Does the first fail the closure duty while the second merely supplies a wrongful disposition? If yes, defend the asymmetry. If both count as dispositions, revise the original case or narrow its diagnosis. If neither does, revise the sufficiency claim at line 71. A further test should distinguish an individually considered bad decision from a template that generates the same sentence automatically. Applying the criticism of empty procedure to the proposed replacement is closely informed by the Cappelen–Deutsch methodological evidence; the diagnosis of these particular cases is my own.

3. **The eight-of-ten extension needs an account of participation that the last-gatekeeper argument does not yet provide.** The principal locations are lines 29–31 and 43–47. In the first case the employee's act actually changes the process from open to closed. In the harder case the duty is defended through lending certification to a clearance even where the worker cannot prevent closure. That is plausible, but the grounds have expanded from control over a status change to responsibility for an authorizing contribution. A person may owe a qualification on such a contribution without having control over whether the review closes. The essay should say which relation is necessary and why.

Lack of a unilateral veto is not itself a counterexample; the draft is right about that. The unsettled question is what counts as lending certification. Each original report is expressly narrow and accurate. If the fact that an institution counts it toward closure suffices, that must also explain the boundary with the measurement-entry case at line 47. The bare label “certification” cannot do the work, and the draft correctly says the label is not decisive. We need enough detail about the practice to tell whether the employee provides an authorization, knowingly supplies evidence used by an authorizer, or expresses endorsement without affecting permission.

Revision test: vary one case across the first, eighth and tenth submitted completion, with the tenth arriving after implementation is already irrevocably authorized. Specify whether the later completion is still a recognized authorization or is retained only as a specialist record. Then specify whether qualifying one contribution preserves an outstanding organizational condition, merely records that this worker did not authorize closure, or changes nothing except her expressed attitude. If the duty applies to several of these, state why; if different duties apply, do not force them under a single claim about control of closure. This test should sharpen the intended boundary with the technician rather than require a worker to guarantee organizational compliance. The objection follows the profile's demand for attention to what a practice actually does, but the proposed distinctions are not claims about Cappelen's own ethics.

The discussion of Kahl at line 73 is a useful acknowledgment, but its originality implication should remain narrow. The retained excerpts in `sources/reading-04.json`, around the marked pages 7, 10 and 19–21, concern not only later reopenability but also a recognizable holder of procedural authority and explicit choices about outstanding risk. The distinction between an existing objection and a future challenge therefore does not by itself establish a new philosophical contribution. The strongest candidate contribution here is the combination of the local worker's prospective obligation, a division of expertise that does not settle the concern, and a handoff whose qualification remains connected to authorization. That combination deserves development even if it specifies familiar negligence or participation duties. Whether it is original is unestablished: neither this review nor the author's bounded search establishes priority across professional handoffs, organizational voice, assurances, or the many-hands literature. I found no basis in the consulted excerpts for declaring the project redundant, and no basis for declaring it unprecedented.

The claim at line 13 that organizations “often” couple local completion with wider clearance is not supported by an empirical survey here. Its frequency is unnecessary to the argument; keep the conditional institutional claim unless evidence is added. There is likewise no need to increase length for its own sake or insert a real disaster case. The controlled example can carry the argument if the three distinctions above survive deliberate variation.

For revision, preserve the transfer/transmission analysis, the distinction between local and collective responsibility, and the protections against blaming workers for unknown system functions. Prioritize the bad-answer contrast, because its failure would change the diagnosis of the central case. Then specify which moral demand persists through which institutional change, and either defend or narrow the redundant-contribution extension. A successful revision should let a skeptical reader classify those cases without relying on whether a sentence merely sounds like taking responsibility. This review recommends continued development; it supplies neither a competition score nor an actual-judge forecast.
