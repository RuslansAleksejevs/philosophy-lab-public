# Question and initial contribution plan

Broad field: aesthetics, attention, interpretation and artistic value. Style: CLEAR.

## Chosen question
When a critic tells an initially unresponsive person to give a difficult work of art more attention, what makes that demand warranted rather than a way to protect the critic's judgment from failure?

## Initial contribution plan
Develop a distinction between judging a work's eventual artistic value and justifying a present request for another person's scarce attention. Argue that expertise can justify a provisional invitation to persevere, but that the invitation loses a certain kind of authority when every disappointing encounter is explained away as a deficiency in the audience. Explore whether a responsible invitation must make some possible failure of the recommended route intelligible in advance, without requiring a recipe for aesthetic appreciation or a guarantee of pleasure. The intended contribution is an account of when first-person failures of appreciation become evidence against a particular critical recommendation, even if they do not refute the work's value.

Test this account against genuinely delayed rewards, transformative works whose appropriate standards emerge only through encounter, unequal expertise, and the risk that an explicit stopping rule simply licenses philistinism. Keep the claims about a critic's particular invitation distinct from claims about a work's universal worth or a duty to make art accessible.

## Rejected preliminary angles
1. A general claim that difficult art excludes people unfairly. Too broad; difficulty can be artistically necessary and opportunity costs alone do not establish injustice.
2. A blanket injunction to encounter a work before reading criticism. Easily defeated by cases where contextual instruction makes perception possible; sequencing alone is not yet a philosophical contribution.
3. The claim that uncertainty about eventual appreciation always defeats perseverance. It assumes precisely what difficult and unfamiliar art calls into question.

## Research needs and live risks
Read primary accounts of taste, the relation of aesthetic judgment to instruction, and aesthetic testimony. Check adjacent discussions of acquaintance and expertise. The proposal may be a local application of familiar defeasible epistemic authority; the argument needs to identify what is distinctive about recommendations that aim to change the very capacities used to assess their success. No claim of priority is made at this stage.
