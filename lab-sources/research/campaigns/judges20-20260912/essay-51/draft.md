# The Last Person Still Looking

Two researchers are transcribing an old register. Their first reading strongly supports a date: 1684. A faint mark could nevertheless be the bottom of a different digit. They have access to an enlarged image that would usually settle this particular uncertainty. Each is willing to spend ten minutes examining it. Neither needs both of them to do so.

Noor stops because Elin is checking. Elin stops because Noor is checking. They remain available to discuss a discovery; they trust one another's eyesight and honesty; they would immediately correct the transcription if either found a mistake. By the afternoon, no correction has arrived.

Something has gone wrong before we discover whether the date is actually 1684. But precisely what? Their initial evidence need not have become weaker. Neither has concealed a finding. Both may be reasonable to think that the other would report a mistake she noticed. The failure concerns the production of a discovery, which their mutual readiness to receive discoveries does not secure.

I will defend a constraint on one familiar reason for ending an investigation: the expectation that someone else will expose a mistake. That expectation must be assessed with the proposed withdrawals from inquiry in effect. It follows that individually adequate reasons for stopping need not justify a group's combined withdrawal. It also follows that learning of a failure in the division of inquiry can call for renewed investigation without calling for an immediate reduction in confidence in the answer. These claims give a more precise account of what remains to be done when everyone agrees, nobody objects, and the evidence already looks good.

## What permission to stop concerns

We need to distinguish three assessments. First, how strongly does the evidence now support the date? Second, would the later absence of a correction supply additional evidence? Third, may a researcher now cease the particular investigative work she was doing?

The questions can have different answers. Noor's first reading can strongly support 1684 even if no further checking occurs. An afternoon without objections will add little if nobody examines anything during the afternoon. And Noor can have a good reason to stop her own inspection if Elin will conduct an equally good one and share the result. Nothing here requires treating every unperformed inquiry as a defeater for belief.

Friedman has shown why norms governing inquiry cannot simply be read off familiar permissions to acquire well-supported beliefs. Inquiry requires decisions about where to direct limited attention. Her account of inquiry-directed instrumental requirements provides an important starting point: how well one's beliefs fit one's evidence and how one should organize the search for evidence are connected questions that require distinct attention. I use that starting point without assuming her complete account of their normative relationship. ([Friedman 2020, §§2–3](https://jfriedmanphilo.github.io/E&Z.pdf).)

“Stopping” will mean withdrawing active effort from a specified inquiry for a specified interval. It need not mean forgetting the question, treating its answer as certain, or refusing to reconsider. Noor can stop scrutinizing the image while remaining ready to receive a message from Elin. This distinction matters because much sensible division of inquiry consists in precisely such withdrawals. A scientist can cease following every development in an adjacent specialty while remaining receptive to a collaborator's warnings.

I will also distinguish possessing a reason for stopping from having an all-things-considered permission to stop. A fire alarm, exhaustion, or an unimportant question may provide a sufficient reason for abandoning an investigation whatever happens to future evidence. My concern is a more specific justification: “This further work is dispensable because correction will remain available.” Its adequacy cannot be established by reasons that justify abandonment on some other basis.

To isolate it, suppose the researchers have a worthwhile aim of resolving the transcription, regard the remaining ambiguity as worth ten minutes' attention, and face no competing demand that makes even one inspection unreasonable. These assumptions do not make both inspections necessary. They make the availability of one an intelligible reason to dispense with the other. Later I will relax the assumptions about reliable checking and a determinate assignment.

## The evidence behind silence

The first part of the problem is already well understood. Silence supports a conclusion only under suitable conditions. Pedersen and Kallestrup develop Goldberg's account by distinguishing the source's coverage of the subject matter, the recipient's receptivity, and sufficient elapsed time without the relevant evidence appearing. Their discussion includes a newspaper whose usual reporter is on holiday without a replacement. The newspaper's general reputation does not secure informative silence during that interval. Their account also accommodates evidence passing through chains of sources. ([Pedersen and Kallestrup 2013, §2](https://www.nikolajpedersen.com/papers/Absence-based%20inference%20-%20preprint.pdf).)

The point applies readily to the register. Elin's honesty supports a conditional about reporting a discovery. Her visual skill supports a conditional about what she would discover by inspecting the image. Neither, alone or together, supports the claim that the inspection will occur. An assessment of silence must include the activity that could have broken it.

A simple numerical illustration separates this from the strength of the initial evidence. Imagine one hundred equally likely cases compatible with the researchers' current information. The initial transcription is correct in ninety. In the other ten, a further inspection would expose the mistake in nine. Assume that the inspection never reports a mistake when there is none. If it occurs, silence leaves ninety correct cases and one undetected incorrect case. If it does not occur, silence leaves the original ninety and ten. The same quiet afternoon has different evidential significance under the two arrangements.

These numbers are illustrative, not an empirical model of paleography or a proposed threshold for justified belief. They show a logical separation. Deciding randomly whether the inspection will occur need not change the present ninety-out-of-a-hundred support for the transcription. It does change the significance of a later absence of correction.

Recent work on algorithmic absence-based inference likewise emphasizes the source of information, the connection to it, and the time allowed for evidence to arrive. Pashevich's discussion also considers how a lack of recorded interaction may reflect a lack of exposure rather than a person's preferences. The general possibility that an information process helps produce the absence on which an inference rests is therefore not new. ([Pashevich 2025, §§2, 4.1.5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-025-00929-x).)

We can nevertheless ask a further question. Suppose a warning process exists now. May we appeal to it as our reason for stopping the activity that sustains it? The retrospective conditions for informative silence tell us which facts matter. We need to carry those facts into the prospective assessment of a decision.

## A condition on withdrawal

Here is the proposed constraint:

**When a withdrawal from inquiry is justified by expected access to corrective evidence, that expectation must be adequately supported for the arrangement that would result from the withdrawal, including the other withdrawals licensed by the same proposal.**

Call this the withdrawal condition. “Adequately supported” concerns the grounds available for expecting correction. It permits fallible expectations. Someone can satisfy the condition while a dependable collaborator, unexpectedly, fails to perform her task. The condition rules out relying on a forecast of correction while assessing a different arrangement from the one the proposal would produce.

Its rationale has three steps. First, a reason of the form “I can dispense with this work because its epistemic function will still be performed” relies on that function's continuing performance. Second, a proposed withdrawal changes the arrangement whose performance matters. Third, evidence of performance under the old arrangement supplies the proposed reason only insofar as it supports performance under the new one. Without that connection, the reason never reaches the action it is supposed to justify.

The last clause, concerning other licensed withdrawals, prevents an important mistake. Suppose both researchers are initially committed to inspecting the image. A coordinator considers two changes separately. If Noor stops and Elin keeps her commitment, correction remains available. If Elin stops and Noor keeps hers, correction remains available. Both assessments are sound. Their combination is not. The coordinator cannot grant both releases by treating the unmodified commitment in each assessment as available in the other.

This does not show that two genuine, unconditional permissions are logically inconsistent. The original permissions were conditional on different backgrounds. The mistake is losing those backgrounds when the recommendations are combined. Inquiry makes this mistake especially easy to overlook because the resource being consumed is often the unrecorded expectation that someone else is paying attention.

The point can be stated without a coordinator. Noor's withdrawal must be assessed in light of what she has reason to expect Elin will do. If Noor knows that Elin has adopted a policy of leaving the inspection to her, Noor cannot justify her own withdrawal by invoking Elin's inspection. If their decisions are independent and each reasonably but falsely believes the other's commitment is fixed, their individual decisions may be excusable or even reasonable relative to their information. That does not make the combined arrangement a successful implementation of their shared plan. We should distinguish a defective arrangement from individual epistemic culpability.

The original opening case leaves room for both possibilities. The sharper version is the coordinator's case: the relevant dependencies are known, and the proposed joint release is assessed all at once. No appeal to hidden bad motives or unavoidable ignorance explains the error.

This is a form of failure to combine individually attractive choices, a familiar family of problems. Dunn's work on epistemic free riding investigates ways in which pursuing individual accuracy can worsen a group's accuracy. His publicly available abstract and author description establish that broad precedent; I do not rely here on a detailed interpretation of the unavailable chapter. ([Dunn 2018](https://sites.google.com/depauw.edu/jeffreydunn/research).) The present argument identifies a particular dependency within that family: one person's release from inquiry draws on another's continuing production of possible corrective evidence. It does not require a theory that maximizes aggregate accuracy or a conflict between anyone's epistemic welfare and anyone else's.

Nor does it require that an entire group continue investigating the same question. Habgood-Coote's account explains how group inquiry can distribute subquestions and even the work of tracking an inquiry's progress. ([Habgood-Coote 2022, §§4–5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-020-00232-5).) The withdrawal condition adds a constraint on changing such a distribution: an activity's contribution must be reassessed when the people providing it are released. Completing an answer and arranging for a live possible error to be discovered are different tasks; the latter can remain unassigned after the former appears complete.

## Why a circle is not the test

It is tempting to diagnose the researchers' mistake simply as circular reliance. But that would produce the wrong general rule.

Consider a team examining three different parts of the register. Noor checks dates, Elin checks names, and Sam checks amounts. They pass their findings around a circular messaging group. Each stops examining matters covered by the others. A discovery can originate at any of three inspections and circulate to everyone who needs it. The cycle helps transmit evidence. It does not prevent its acquisition.

A circle of conditional promises can also support legitimate stopping. Noor promises to resume if Elin receives an alert; Elin promises to resume if Sam receives one; Sam promises to alert both after a scheduled automated comparison. The arrangement may be needlessly elaborate, but the problem cannot be its circular appearance. What matters is whether evidence can enter it under the policy they adopt.

Conversely, an apparently tidy hierarchy can fail. A department relies on its committee, which relies on a specialist, who has been told that the committee has finished checking. A chart with named offices will not reveal whether any inspection remains. The relevant object of assessment is the functioning inquiry, including how assignments are understood and acted upon.

This is why the withdrawal condition does not require an ultimate investigator whose authority is epistemically independent of everybody else. Such a requirement would invite a general regress about how the investigator's competence is known. The necessary independence is local and causal: the activity supplying the expected correction must survive the withdrawals being justified by it. Its operator can rely extensively on training, instruments, testimony, and other people.

The work can also be spread over time. Perhaps either researcher has an adequately high chance of noticing a mistake in the course of another task. Perhaps an agreed random selection gives one person the inspection. Perhaps a reminder reopens the question tomorrow. Each arrangement can qualify if it supplies sufficiently good grounds for expecting the relevant correction at the relevant time. There is no general requirement to name a permanent sentinel.

But merely multiplying potential recipients does not necessarily help. A hundred people who would reliably relay a warning once they had one can still have no route to acquiring the first warning. Arielli has already described a related problem in online sharing: people leave verification to their audience, whose members pass that work onwards in turn. My access to his discussion is limited to primary-text search excerpts, but the precedent is clear. ([Arielli 2018, §5](https://philarchive.org/archive/ARISAS).) The withdrawal condition explains one normative consequence: additional relayers do not warrant additional withdrawals from investigation unless the resulting arrangement supports acquisition as well as transmission.

There can, of course, be genuine strength in numbers. If every participant has some independent chance of encountering relevant evidence, a larger network can improve the prospect of correction. “Someone will notice” can be well supported even when nobody knows who. The defect lies in the unsupported expectation about the resulting process, not in the use of an indefinite pronoun.

## Reopening without immediate doubt

Suppose Noor discovers that nobody examined the enlarged image. What should change?

One answer is her assessment of the afternoon's silence. If she had counted it as independent reassurance, she must remove that reassurance. A second answer is her plan. If the remaining ambiguity is still worth resolving, she can inspect the image or obtain a dependable replacement. But a third response is not automatically required: reducing her confidence below what the initial reading justified.

The fact that nobody performed an independent later check need not be evidence that the initial reading was defective. We can stipulate that the assignment failure arose from a scheduling mistake entirely unrelated to the quality of that reading. Discovering it changes the expected availability of further evidence; it leaves the initial evidential relation intact. In the numerical example, someone who had never counted silence as further evidence may retain the original ninety-out-of-a-hundred confidence.

The important inference is limited. Loss of a particular ground for ceasing inquiry can occur without loss of the grounds for one's current confidence. If no other adequate stopping ground is available and the epistemic aim remains in force, renewed effort is then called for. A theory that responds to every such discovery by lowering confidence directs its correction at the wrong thing. A theory that responds only by recalibrating confidence leaves the inquiry's missing work undone.

The needed repair can be social. Noor does not have to do the inspection herself if she can secure another competent investigator. She need not demand a fresh first-order argument for 1684 from everyone in the group. What she needs is an adequate answer to a practical question about inquiry: which activity will now supply the expected opportunity for correction?

Schwengerer's account of epistemic bystanding makes an illuminating comparison. It explains how a person who can recognize and intervene in another's false or risky belief formation may instead encourage unwarranted confidence through her non-intervention. His discussion includes both community-level possibilities and effects on already held beliefs. ([Schwengerer 2025, §§3–4 and notes 2, 16](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12136-024-00598-z).) The case here can arise earlier: nobody yet has the information that would enable the relevant warning, and everybody's initial confidence can be appropriately calibrated. The missing activity would put someone in a position to correct the mistake. A duty to report discovered problems leaves this prior question open.

This is not a claim that silence never bears on current belief, or that a failed assignment never reveals wider incompetence. In many actual cases both do. It is a claim about what does not follow merely from discovering the failed assignment. The repair should track what has been undermined.

## What the condition does, and what it leaves open

The strongest objection is that the proposed condition is merely instrumental rationality applied to research. Anyone choosing a means must consider whether it will still work after the choice. Why turn a commonplace into a philosophical principle?

The objection is right about the underlying structure. I have not established a new source of normativity alongside familiar requirements governing reasons and means. The contribution is to identify what that structure requires when reasons for withdrawing from inquiry depend on the continued conduct of other inquirers. Three consequences are easy to obscure if we attend only to existing evidence or to the competence of the people involved.

First, the justification of stopping can depend on other people's future investigative conduct even when the current justification of the answer does not. Holding the first-order evidence fixed therefore does not hold fixed the rational assessment of withdrawal. Second, reasons that justify separate withdrawals under different backgrounds do not automatically combine into a reason for everyone to stop. Third, the appropriate response to losing a stopping ground can concern assignments and renewed attention while current confidence remains unchanged. These are consequences of applying familiar rational requirements to a neglected transition, rather than reasons to posit an autonomous foundation for them.

A second objection is that we have described ordinary coordination failure. Indeed we have, in part. There need be no uniquely epistemic logical form to the failure. Yet its epistemic content matters. The retained resource is a capacity to acquire and circulate evidence; the relevant success is timely exposure of a possible error; and the corrective response must distinguish confidence from further inquiry. An account that merely says “coordinate better” leaves those distinctions unspecified. The condition identifies what the coordination must preserve, insofar as correction supplies the reason for withdrawal.

A third objection concerns excessive demands. Must every permissible stopping decision leave someone watching? No. Sometimes the answer has been established as well as the question warrants. Sometimes the expected value of further inquiry is too low, or a deadline makes it unavailable. Sometimes a group reasonably abandons an aim. In each case the stopping decision may stand without an expectation of continuing correction. The withdrawal condition is a constraint on using that expectation as a justification. It does not require every question to be insured against every possible future mistake.

This qualification also limits what the condition says about the last available investigator. If her departure would end coverage, she cannot justify departing by appealing to that coverage. It does not follow that she must stay. She may have other sufficient reasons to leave. Nor does it follow that the person who happens to be last acquires an unlimited responsibility because everybody else departed first. Fair allocation, burdens, and the importance of the remaining inquiry require further argument. The condition exposes an unsatisfied dependency; it does not allocate every resulting duty.

Finally, agents cannot usually inspect an entire organization before relying on it. The condition asks for adequate grounds, not a complete institutional map. Stable practices, reliable assurances about roles, and experience of how an organization handles absences may supply them. The need for reassessment becomes sharper when the proposed withdrawal removes an activity that the reason for withdrawal presupposes. An ordinary reader can reasonably rely on a newspaper's reporting without auditing its newsroom. The editor who releases its last reporter cannot carry over that expectation unchanged.

Confidence, justified silence, and permission to stop are thus connected without being interchangeable. A quiet group can retain good evidence and still have unfinished epistemic work. To determine whether its members may dispense with that work, we must ask what would remain of the inquiry after the proposed departures. A reason to stop can belong to one person precisely because another person will still be looking.
