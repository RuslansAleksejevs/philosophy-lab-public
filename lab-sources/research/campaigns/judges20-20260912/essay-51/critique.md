**Recommendation: REVISE while retaining the central distinction.** The narrowly construed withdrawal condition is plausible, clearly explained, and not refuted here. The main limitations are the size of the demonstrated contribution, an ambiguity between justified expectation and actual coverage, and an insufficiently explicit bridge from losing a reason to stop to being required to resume. These affect the essay's philosophical force more than its prose.

## Fair reconstruction and concrete merits

The essay separates present support for an answer, the evidential value of subsequent silence, and a reason to withdraw investigative effort. Its central argument concerns the third: if another person's continuing correction is what makes my work dispensable, evidence supporting that correction must concern the arrangement my withdrawal would help produce. The coordinator cannot justify releasing both investigators by using the other's unchanged commitment twice. The conclusion is a constraint on a particular justification, not a sufficient condition for all permissible stopping or a duty to preserve every possible source of evidence.

The strongest additional result is that discovering a failed assignment may remove a ground for stopping without weakening the initial evidence. Repair may therefore involve obtaining an inspection rather than reducing confidence below its original justified level. On the charitable reading, any requirement to obtain that inspection remains conditional on an independently operative reason to continue the inquiry.

Several choices make this a worthwhile candidate:

- The numerical example at lines 31–33 is sound on its stated assumptions. After a completed check produces no warning, 90 of the 91 remaining cases are correct; without the check, the original 90 of 100 remain. Nothing in this calculation itself requires a credence or inquiry threshold, and the essay appropriately says so.
- Lines 49–55 avoid presenting conditional permissions as inconsistent unconditional permissions. They also distinguish the coordinator's informed mistake from agents reasonably relying on false information about each other's commitments.
- Lines 63–77 correctly resist identifying the problem with a cycle in a communication diagram. Evidence acquisition and transmission are different functions; an epistemically independent ultimate investigator is unnecessary.
- Lines 83–93 explicitly protect the original evidential baseline. The argument does not infer that bad coordination is always evidence of a bad initial reading.
- The treatment of prior work is unusually candid. In particular, the author does not present missing coverage or deferred verification as discoveries, and does not claim a detailed reading of unavailable Dunn or Arielli texts.

The essay's accessible prose is an asset under the documented profile. Additional notation is unnecessary unless it resolves one of the following problems.

## 1. The application is sound, but its distinctive philosophical yield remains underdemonstrated

**Locations:** lines 37, 43–59 and 97–103, especially the three consequences at line 101. **Severity:** major for significance and originality; not a refutation of the condition.

The response to the instrumental-rationality objection establishes that the condition need not be an autonomous norm. That is a sensible concession. It does not yet establish why this application advances the existing literature enough to bear the essay's argumentative weight.

The nearest supported predecessor is not merely the advice to coordinate better. It is a combined account: inquiry-directed instrumental reasoning of the kind Friedman discusses, together with the coverage conditions developed by Pedersen and Kallestrup. The latter already distinguish reliable tracking, communication, and operation at the relevant time, and explicitly discuss the reporter's absence. Give an ordinary prospective decision model those facts and allow the agent's choice to affect who investigates: it will reject eliminating the only retained means of correction. Preserve the initial evidential relation: it will also preserve the initial credence. Keep the backgrounds of conditional recommendations explicit: it will reject combining the releases. The draft has not shown where this combined predecessor leaves a residual explanatory problem.

This is not a demand for a new foundational source of normativity. A careful derived diagnostic can be an original contribution. But the three consequences are currently close to three restatements of assumptions already supplied to the example. The coordinator's version improves decisiveness by stipulating known dependencies, yet that same stipulation makes the error especially easy for ordinary means–end reasoning to diagnose. The introductory interpersonal version is less trivial but requires more care about information and permissible reliance.

**Revision tests:** Reconstruct the combined predecessor in a short paragraph, with its strongest interpretation, and identify the exact additional work the withdrawal condition performs. A genuine difference in verdict would establish one kind of contribution; a useful unification or previously unarticulated restriction on composing recommendations could establish another. If claiming the latter, develop one difficult transition in enough detail that the restriction settles something the opening case merely illustrates. For example, distinguish a release that disables monitoring from a release that reliably activates a replacement, with the same present evidence and similar verbal assurances. State the assumptions under which the proposed assessment succeeds. Do not merely add another example in which everybody obviously stops looking.

The bounded literature search warrants uncertainty, not a conclusion that this result is already published. An equivalent principle may appear in the unavailable free-riding literature. Conversely, no equivalent principle was established by this review. Keep the difference between a demonstrated useful synthesis and a historical originality claim explicit.

**Judge grounding:** This objection follows the method of Fitelson's Jeffrey review: reconstruct the established alternative and ask what motivates the proposed addition. It is an inference about a relevant reviewing method, not evidence that he would reject applications or demand a new formalism. The assessment of this particular essay's originality remains ordinary philosophical judgment.

## 2. Choose consistently between an evidence-relative reason and actual survival of monitoring

**Locations:** lines 43–45, 53, 71–77 and 107–109. **Severity:** consequential but locally repairable ambiguity.

The official condition concerns adequately supported expectations. Line 45 expressly allows a dependable collaborator unexpectedly to fail. This is an evidence-relative condition on an agent's reason. Yet line 71 calls it necessary that the activity supplying correction survive the withdrawals; line 75 says the resulting arrangement must support acquisition; and line 107 denies a coverage-based justification to the last investigator. Read literally as claims about what actually survives, these are stronger than the official condition.

Consider an investigator with good evidence that a scheduled replacement check has a 99% chance of occurring. Stipulate that this level is adequate for the question and that the small risk is acceptable. She leaves; an unforeseen equipment failure prevents the check. Compare another investigator who knows, before leaving, that the check has been cancelled. Both resulting histories contain no inspection. Their original reasons are different. The draft should say that the first may have a justified reason that fails to achieve its intended result, whereas the second lacks that reason. The mere description of either as the actual last investigator cannot decide the issue.

The draft already has the resources for this answer. The problem is that its causal-survival language sometimes makes an actual property of the arrangement look like a necessary condition for the agent's justification. The same issue arises when a randomized monitoring policy permissibly risks an uncovered interval: evaluate the prospect under the adopted policy before learning its realization, then distinguish that appraisal from assessing the interval once the outcome is known. Do not classify an acceptable risk as impermissible solely because its unfavorable branch occurs.

There is a related precision issue in “including the other withdrawals licensed by the same proposal.” Permission to withdraw is not a prediction that everyone will exercise that permission. The coordinator's joint release does specify a relevant combined change. More general permission policies need an account of expected uptake or coordination. A reliably enforced selection rule can make individual permissions compatible without licensing the simultaneous execution of every available action.

**Revision tests:** State whose information, at which decision time, supports the expectation. Distinguish the proposal or policy being evaluated from its realized outcome. Apply the resulting formulation to (a) an unforeseen failure, (b) a known cancellation, and (c) a coordinated lottery with an explicitly accepted risk. Give separate verdicts about the original reason, actual coverage, and what to do after learning that coverage failed. Then audit the universal-sounding claims at lines 71, 75 and 107 against those verdicts. No claim of sufficiency for stopping is required.

**Judge grounding:** The supplied profile's distinction between actual and expected epistemic success, and its demand to track the strength of claims, motivate this test. That is a methodological analogy to its selected accuracy discussion, not a claim that Fitelson has endorsed this account of inquiry. The particular examples and diagnosis are this review's assessment.

## 3. Losing this reason to stop does not by itself establish a duty to resume

**Locations:** lines 21–23, 83–89 and 101–107. **Severity:** potentially substantive for the advertised reopening result; repair by defending or explicitly conditioning its normative bridge.

Line 87 moves from loss of a ground for ceasing inquiry, with no other adequate stopping ground and an epistemic aim still in force, to renewed effort being called for. The withdrawal condition itself rules out only one justification. It does not establish that every permissible cessation requires an affirmative stopping ground, or that having a worthwhile epistemic aim requires performing every worthwhile means to it.

The opening stipulations say that resolving the ambiguity is worth ten minutes and that no competing demand makes one inspection unreasonable. Neither statement alone makes that inspection required. An action can be reasonable, useful, and worth its cost without being obligatory. Likewise, a means can improve the prospect of achieving an aim without being necessary to achieving it. The essay is careful about these distinctions for reasons to stop; it should be equally careful about reasons to continue.

Friedman's discussion makes the issue sharper rather than removing it. In the independently reread preprint section 3, especially pp. 7–8, she distinguishes norms requiring necessary means, better or best means, and satisfactory means; she also distinguishes wide from narrow scope. The draft need not settle these debates. It must say which limited premise supports its positive reopening claim. The fact that the aim remains in force helps with the option of abandoning the aim, but does not settle how much pursuit that aim requires.

**Revision tests:** Compare three situations: a check is necessary for an independently justified required inquiry; a check offers only a small further improvement to an already adequate investigation; and the group may permissibly relinquish the aim. The proposed withdrawal reason can fail in all three, while what should happen next differs. Explain that difference without deriving an obligation merely from the falsity of the original excuse. If the essay intends only the conditional claim that an already outstanding requirement reappears when an assumed substitute is absent, say so expressly and identify that requirement in the opening case. If it intends a stronger norm, defend it. In either case, preserve the sound point that resumption need not involve lowering confidence below the original baseline.

**Judge grounding:** Tracking the difference between a necessary condition on one reason and a requirement to act fits the profile's concern with exact logical strength. The substantive concern about instrumental norms is independently supported by Friedman and is generic philosophical criticism, not an attributed Fitelson position on the epistemic versus zetetic distinction.
