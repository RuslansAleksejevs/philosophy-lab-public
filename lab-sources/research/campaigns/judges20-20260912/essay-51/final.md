# The Last Person Still Looking

Two researchers are transcribing an old register. Their first reading strongly supports a date: 1684. A faint mark could nevertheless be the bottom of a different digit. An enlarged image would usually settle this residual uncertainty. Both Noor and Elin are preparing to inspect it.

A coordinator considers releasing Noor from the inspection. Elin would complete it and report any correction, so Noor's duplicate effort appears dispensable. The coordinator then considers releasing Elin. Noor would complete the inspection, so Elin's duplicate effort appears dispensable. On those grounds, the coordinator releases both. Nobody examines the image.

The date may be correct. Their original reading may remain good evidence. Each researcher may be honest, competent, and ready to respond to a discovery. The coordinator's reasoning nevertheless fails. Each assessment of a release drew on work that the combined release would remove.

The conditional reasoning is familiar: recommendations established against different backgrounds cannot automatically be combined. Its epistemic application yields a further result worth separating from that logical point. A discovery about who is investigating can defeat a reason for withdrawing from inquiry while leaving rational confidence in the answer unchanged. Where a requirement to complete the inquiry remains outstanding, the appropriate repair is renewed work or a replacement arrangement. It need not be reduced confidence.

I will defend this conditional result by identifying what a justification for withdrawal must track. The proposal is a focused synthesis of established ideas about inquiry and evidence from silence. It is neither a new logic of permission nor a complete theory of when inquiry is obligatory.

## Three assessments

We should distinguish the support for an answer, the evidential significance of silence, and a reason to stop investigating. Noor's first reading can strongly support 1684. A later absence of objections can add evidence if someone has conducted a suitably revealing inspection. And the expectation of that inspection can make Noor's own effort dispensable before its result is available.

These assessments have different objects. The first concerns the answer in light of present information. The second concerns the informational significance of an event: no warning arrived. The third concerns a proposed change in investigative conduct. Assessing that change requires considering what will happen if it is made.

“Withdrawal” here means ceasing a specified investigative activity for a specified interval. It need not involve certainty, refusal to reconsider, or even an intention never to inspect the material again. Noor can stop examining the image while remaining receptive to Elin's findings. Such withdrawal is often central to sensible collaboration.

Friedman's discussion of inquiry-directed instrumental requirements explains why the distribution of attention matters to epistemology. Familiar permissions to acquire supported beliefs do not by themselves tell inquirers how to allocate their effort. She also distinguishes stronger and weaker instrumental principles and questions about their scope. I draw on the need to assess inquiry as activity without assuming that every worthwhile epistemic aim generates an obligation to pursue every useful means. ([Friedman 2020, §§2–3](https://jfriedmanphilo.github.io/E&Z.pdf).)

The target justification is specific: “I may dispense with this work because the relevant opportunity for correction will remain available.” It is only one possible reason for withdrawal. Exhaustion, expense, a sufficiently completed inquiry, or a permissible decision to abandon the question may justify stopping on other grounds. The failure of this particular reason therefore does not by itself establish an obligation to resume. We will need an additional premise for that conclusion.

## What makes silence informative

An honest colleague can reliably report every problem she notices without reliably noticing problems. Her willingness to report supports one conditional; her ability to find a mistake while inspecting supports another. Neither establishes that she will perform the inspection.

The literature on absence-based inference already explains this distinction. Pedersen and Kallestrup develop Goldberg's coverage account by distinguishing reliable tracking and transmission, the recipient's receptivity, and sufficient time without the relevant evidence appearing. Their absent-reporter case shows why a newspaper's usual reputation does not secure informative silence while its reporter is on holiday without a replacement. Their account also accommodates chains through which evidence travels. ([Pedersen and Kallestrup 2013, §2](https://www.nikolajpedersen.com/papers/Absence-based%20inference%20-%20preprint.pdf).)

Consider an illustrative probability model. Among one hundred equally likely possibilities compatible with the researchers' initial information, ninety contain a correct transcription. In the other ten, the enlarged-image inspection would expose nine mistakes. Suppose it produces no false warnings. After a completed inspection yields no warning, ninety of the ninety-one remaining possibilities contain a correct transcription. Without an inspection, silence leaves the original ninety out of one hundred.

The numbers are not claims about paleography or thresholds for justified belief. They make two relations explicit. The warning process affects what later silence supports. Its presence or absence need not affect what the initial reading already supports. In particular, a random administrative decision about whether to run the inspection can be independent of the true date.

Recent work on algorithmic absence-based inference likewise emphasizes the information source, its connection to the recipient, and sufficient time for evidence to arrive. Pashevich also discusses the possibility that a lack of recorded interaction reflects a lack of exposure rather than a lack of interest. The general dependence of an informative absence on its production process is thus an established concern. ([Pashevich 2025, §§2, 4.1.5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-025-00929-x).)

What needs to be carried forward is the assessment of that production process when an inquirer proposes to alter it. A process can currently provide excellent coverage and lose it after a withdrawal. A process currently idle can begin providing coverage because a withdrawal activates a replacement. Present coverage alone settles neither case.

## Forecasting the inquiry after withdrawal

The constraint I propose is this:

**An agent who justifies a withdrawal by expected access to corrective evidence must have adequate grounds, at the decision time, for the relevant forecast under the proposed withdrawal policy. The policy includes its coordination rules and reasonably anticipated uptake.**

Call this the withdrawal condition. Its immediate object is the agent's reason, evaluated against the information available when the decision is made. For a coordinator recommending a joint policy, the relevant forecast concerns that policy's combined effects. For a person deciding whether to depart, it concerns the departure and the responses she has reason to anticipate. Neither assessment is identical to asking how the arrangement happens to perform afterwards.

Two separate judgments belong in the full justification. One concerns the forecast: what grounds support expecting this pattern of investigation, detection, and reporting? The other concerns the inquiry: does the forecasted performance make the withdrawn activity dispensable, given an independently defensible aim, standard, and cost of further work? The withdrawal condition constrains the first judgment. It does not supply the second.

Suppose there are excellent grounds for predicting that a retained check would detect ninety percent of a specified class of errors. One inquiry may reasonably accept an eighty-five percent detection target; another may require ninety-five percent. Holding the forecast fixed, the retained check clears the first target and misses the second. An available additional test might then matter differently to each inquiry. These targets are illustrative assumptions, not recommended universal standards. The point is that excellent grounds for a forecast do not establish that its predicted performance is sufficient. If the target itself is unreasonable, that is a further defect in the stopping justification.

The rationale for the withdrawal condition is modest. A reason that says the work is dispensable because its function will be performed draws on a forecast about continued performance. The proposed action can change that performance. Evidence about the old arrangement supports the reason only insofar as it supports the pertinent forecast about the arrangement to be adopted. Otherwise, the argument evaluates an alternative to the proposed action.

The opening coordinator violates this constraint. The forecast supporting Noor's release retains Elin's inspection; the forecast supporting Elin's release retains Noor's. Neither supports the forecast that correction will be available when both are released. The error is not the conjunction of two unconditional permissions. It is the loss of their different background conditions.

If Noor and Elin instead decide separately, each reasonably but mistakenly believing the other's commitment fixed, their evidence-relative reasons may be sound. Their plan can fail without either committing the coordinator's informed error. Three descriptions must remain distinct: an agent possessed an adequate reason, a joint proposal was adequately supported, and a process actually delivered correction. A favorable answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

## Replacements, lotteries, and circles

A harder comparison shows why the condition cannot simply require retaining the last investigator.

Suppose a reserve system can perform the relevant inspection and reliably send the result. In one arrangement, releasing both human researchers automatically activates it. The coordinator has strong evidence that the trigger works and that the reserve's performance meets the inquiry's independently justified standard. In another arrangement, the same reserve is on standby, but an unreleased researcher must start it. The proposed joint release does not assign that act to anyone.

In both arrangements the human researchers are currently checking, and someone can truthfully say that a competent reserve exists. Yet the forecasts under joint withdrawal differ. The first proposal supplies a supported route to activating the replacement. The second leaves the activation unsupported. A static requirement that someone currently be investigating would miss the first arrangement's strength; a static assurance that a replacement exists would miss the second's weakness.

This comparison assumes that activation, relevant detection, timely reporting, and receipt are all included in the forecast. If the triggering software is unreliable, or the recipient will ignore the report, the first arrangement can fail too. The conclusion concerns a supported substitution, not a privileged status for automation.

Permission policies also need to be distinguished from lists of actions simultaneously executed. A policy can permit either researcher to leave while allowing release only after the other's commitment is acknowledged. A lottery can select which person remains. Neither policy licenses adding together every individually available departure. The object of assessment is the coordinated policy, including what participants are expected to do under it.

Now suppose a replacement has a ninety-nine percent chance of operating, and the remaining risk is acceptable under the justified standard for this inquiry. Strong evidence for that forecast can support withdrawal even if an unforeseen failure occurs in the one-percent branch. Compare a researcher who knows before leaving that the replacement has been cancelled. The actual histories can be equally silent, but the original reasons differ. The first researcher may have acted on an adequate reason; the second cannot appeal to a forecast she knows to be false. Learning afterwards that the first arrangement failed calls for a fresh decision. It does not retrospectively make a permissible risk impermissible.

Circularity is similarly an unreliable shortcut. Noor checks dates, Elin checks names, and Sam checks amounts. They pass findings around a circular messaging group. Each can cease checking matters covered by the others because discoveries can enter the communication process through their remaining inspections. A circle of relaying commitments can also work: each promises to forward a warning to the next person, while Sam additionally receives the result of an independently scheduled comparison. The relevant forecast includes that point of entry.

Conversely, an apparently orderly hierarchy can lack any supported acquisition route. A committee relies on a specialist, who has been released on the assumption that the committee has finished checking. Listing offices does not establish the performance of an inquiry. Nor does multiplying people who would transmit a warning establish that someone will acquire one.

Arielli has described a related problem in online sharing: users leave verification to their audience, whose members pass the task onwards. The primary-text excerpts available in the present search establish that precedent, although I could not consult his full paper. ([Arielli 2018, §5](https://philarchive.org/archive/ARISAS).) The present condition identifies a specific constraint on withdrawal reasoning: a forecast about additional relayers cannot substitute for the needed forecast about acquisition. Yet a large network can supply good grounds when its members really have chances of independently encountering the relevant evidence. “Someone will notice” need not name that person to be warranted.

No general demand for an epistemically independent ultimate investigator follows. A forecast can depend on testimony, training, instruments, and established practices. It must concern what those resources are expected to do under the policy, rather than presume that present conduct continues unchanged. The independence at issue is not freedom from all epistemic reliance.

## Returning to inquiry without reducing confidence

The most important consequence concerns what learning of a failed arrangement requires us to repair. To establish it, we need a precise sequence and an explicit normative premise.

At noon, Noor's total information supports the date with the original ninety-out-of-a-hundred confidence. She has good grounds for expecting a check to be conducted later that afternoon. By an established reporting rule, no result is communicated before five. It is only noon: no evidentially significant interval of silence has passed. She stops her duplicate inspection on the strength of the expected substitute.

At one, she learns that the substitute has been cancelled. The cancellation was selected by a random administrative procedure whose operation she has good grounds to regard as independent of both the date and the quality of the original reading. It reveals no broader problem with the researchers' competence. The cancellation notice contains no information about the image. Noor knows that no check has yet occurred.

Her rational confidence in the date can remain at the initial level. There was no earlier interval that required an increase on the strength of silence, and her new information does not favor either possible date. The account therefore does not rely on her irrationally failing to update. It specifies why the appropriate confidence immediately before and immediately after the cancellation notice is unchanged.

What changes is her forecast of further evidence. To infer a requirement to act from that change, add the following premise: a requirement to complete the residual inquiry remains undefeated, and her own inspection or an adequate substitute is a necessary means of meeting it. This premise is deliberately separate from the withdrawal condition. The case does not derive it merely from curiosity, the usefulness of checking, or a self-imposed preference for extra assurance. It isolates an inquiry in which such a requirement is already in force.

Given that premise, Noor cannot both retain the required inquiry and dispense with all its necessary means. Her original reason for leaving the work to someone else has disappeared. She must restore an adequate route, whether by resuming or arranging a replacement. Her required change concerns conduct, despite no required change in current confidence.

The scope of the result becomes clearer through two contrasts. If the check would merely provide a small optional improvement to an already adequate investigation, the forecast supporting withdrawal can fail without making further work obligatory. If relinquishing the aim is permissible, abandonment may be an appropriate response. The cancellation defeats the same particular stopping reason in all three cases; only the first includes the independent premise needed for obligatory repair.

Even there, the conclusion does not establish a duty on Noor alone if several adequate allocations remain available. The immediate requirement can be to secure an allocation, with fairness and existing responsibilities determining who performs it. The last capable person does not acquire unlimited duties simply because everyone else departed earlier.

A later discovery produces a different evidential sequence. Suppose Noor instead receives no warning at five, reasonably treats that silence as informative, and only afterwards learns that no inspection occurred. In the illustrative model, her appropriate confidence may rise from ninety percent to ninety out of ninety-one and then return to ninety percent. That sequence involves a reduction from her immediately preceding confidence. It supports the weaker conclusion that discovering failed coverage need not reduce confidence below the original baseline. The earlier cancellation case establishes the stronger result: required inquiry repair can occur without any immediate reduction in justified confidence.

This distinction also matters for interpreting actual failures. A cancellation can provide evidence of general carelessness or reveal that the initial report was less reliable than it seemed. Confidence should then respond to that information. The unchanged-confidence case stipulates, and gives the agent grounds for, the relevant independence. It establishes a possibility, not a general rule that administrative failures have no evidential significance.

Schwengerer's account of epistemic bystanding explains how someone who can recognize and intervene in another's false or risky belief formation may instead encourage unwarranted confidence through non-intervention. His discussion also considers communities and already held beliefs. ([Schwengerer 2025, §§3–4 and notes 2, 16](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12136-024-00598-z).) The cancellation case concerns a prior stage: nobody has yet acquired the information that would enable the warning, and current confidence can remain appropriately calibrated. A requirement to pass on discovered problems does not by itself resolve how to provide for their discovery.

## The contribution and its limits

A sophisticated predecessor can combine inquiry-directed instrumental reasoning with established coverage conditions. It would identify tracking, reporting, and receipt as means of obtaining corrective evidence, then assess their expected performance under alternative actions. Such an account can derive the withdrawal condition and the cases developed here. Nothing in the argument shows that it must deliver different verdicts.

The proposed contribution is the explicit connection between this prospective assessment and the location of epistemic repair. The forecast must be indexed to the change being considered; its support must be distinguished from the adequacy of the performance forecast; and both must be distinguished from the realized outcome. Once these distinctions are maintained, the same discovery can leave the answer's evidential support intact while removing the substitution that discharged a requirement to investigate. This identifies when changing who looks is a required epistemic response even though changing what one believes is not.

The combined predecessor would omit this result only if it left that transition implicit. The claim is therefore one of useful synthesis and development, not a newly discovered foundational norm. The replacement comparison supplies a further benefit: it prevents a crude remedy that insists someone must always retain the old activity. A departure can itself activate sufficient new coverage. What warrants withdrawal is the supported forecast under the transition, assessed against an independently justified standard.

There is substantial prior work nearby. Dunn investigates epistemic free riding, including conflicts between individual accuracy pursuit and group accuracy. The publisher abstract and his author description establish this broad precedent; the full chapter was unavailable in the present search. ([Dunn 2018](https://sites.google.com/depauw.edu/jeffreydunn/research).) Habgood-Coote explains how groups can distribute subquestions and the work of tracking inquiry itself. ([Habgood-Coote 2022, §§4–5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-020-00232-5).) An equivalent transition constraint may already appear in the wider literature. The bounded search does not establish historical priority.

The condition is also intentionally limited in practical reach. It supplies neither a universal completion standard nor a requirement to monitor every possible error. An ordinary reader can have adequate grounds for relying on a newspaper without auditing its newsroom. Those grounds can include stable practices, credible assurances about replacements, and past performance. A coordinator who knows that a proposal changes those practices must assess the forecast accordingly. Different information supports different prospective judgments.

The resulting picture accommodates finite inquiry and substantial reliance on others. We can hold an answer responsibly while depending on a division of work to address an outstanding question about it. When that division fails, the epistemic task is to determine what was lost: support for the answer, support supplied by silence, or the expected substitute for further investigation. In the last case, the work may have to resume while the justified answer stays exactly where it was.
