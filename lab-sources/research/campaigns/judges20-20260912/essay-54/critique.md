## Assessment and fair reconstruction

The draft has a sound central diagnostic insight and an appealingly concrete application. It warrants substantial revision because its strongest policy requirement outruns the argument currently supplied for it. The issue is the strength and scope of the conclusion, not whether a bus-dependent petition is a good safeguard.

The argument is best reconstructed as follows. Democratic authority partly rests on citizens' continuing equal standing, not simply on counting their votes once. A policy may damage the practical capacities through which affected citizens could establish and contest that damage. A promised correction procedure is inadequate protection against a specified injury when that procedure would fail under the injury's occurrence. Uncertainty about whether the injury will occur does not remove this conditional inadequacy. Therefore, when the risk is credible and sufficiently serious, officials normally must secure usable correction before exposing citizens to it, subject to an exception for sufficiently weighty competing costs.

The first three claims perform different jobs. Continuing equality supplies an ethical premise. The dependence of petitions on threatened capacities supplies a causal premise. Testing a safeguard under the failure it addresses supplies a criterion of functional adequacy. The last step adds a norm for choosing under uncertainty. The draft is strongest on the causal and functional claims. It needs a more explicit defense of the norm connecting them to permissible policy implementation.

Several merits should survive revision. The two questions at line 21 cleanly separate the likelihood of harm from the availability of repair if harm occurs. The distinction between civic opportunity and preserved political advantage at lines 35–45 blocks an obvious defense of entrenched privilege. Lines 31–33 and 77–81 consider likelihood, costs, inaction and rival access needs; the draft does not simply assume that preserving the status quo is safe. The treatment of evidence production at lines 27 and 51–55 is more informative than a generic call for another public meeting. Finally, line 85 limits the conclusion about authority and compliance instead of making one defective remedy defeat an entire political order.

## 1. Conditional failure does not yet select the precaution rule

**Locations:** lines 7–9; the three-step argument at line 25; lines 29–33; the exception at lines 79–81; the conclusion at line 89.

**Judge grounding:** This objection applies a documented methodological distinction rather than a preferred ethical theory. In *Cluelessness*, Greaves separates assessments of outcomes from decision guidance and distinguishes representing uncertainty from specifying a permissibility rule. The discussion expressly leaves work for the normative connection. That is the relevant discipline here; neither expected-value maximization nor consequentialism is being demanded. See [*Cluelessness*, §§1–2 and 6, especially PDF pp. 12–14](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~mert2255/papers/cluelessness.pdf).

The inference from “this petition would not work if access were impaired” to “a different route must be secured before taking this risk” is not purely a further description of the petition. Even a demonstrably inadequate safeguard may accompany a permissibly undertaken risk. The reason would have to concern the acceptability of taking the risk, not a false claim that the safeguard works. The draft sometimes recognizes this distinction, particularly at line 25, but the initial formulation at line 7 permits proceeding without protection only when providing it would impose comparably grave costs. Line 81 again treats inexpensive protection and serious possible impairment as enough to close off necessity. These formulations appear stronger than line 31's acknowledgment that obligations vary with likelihood.

Consider two stipulated towns with identical potential civic injury, identical modest safeguard costs, and equally reliable evidence about the causal mechanism. In one, serious impairment has a substantial chance of occurring; in the other, the evidence supports a very small chance. The second risk need not be fanciful: a well-understood but rare event could activate the mechanism. Both towns' petitions fail conditional on that injury. Does the same strong protection requirement follow in both? If not, the criterion needs to distinguish the cases. If so, the paper needs to defend why this class of political risk receives such demanding protection even when its probability is very small. Calling one risk “not credible” would obscure the difference between reliable evidence for a low probability and poor evidence for any probability at all.

Nor does pointing out who bears error settle how much precaution is owed. That observation identifies a morally relevant distribution. It does not establish that every modest certain burden on others is justified to prevent every evidenced small chance of serious exclusion. Repeated across public services, individually modest safeguards can also compete for resources. This is not a demand for a numerical threshold, and it does not refute a special protection for citizenship. It identifies the premise that would make that protection do more work than ordinary risk assessment.

There are at least two defensible routes. The author could defend a special standard of fair risk imposition for capacities constitutive of equal citizenship, explaining its priority and defeaters. Or the author could frame the contribution as a constraint on justifications: once an authority needs a remedy to make proceeding acceptable, it cannot count a conditionally unavailable procedure as that remedy. The latter leaves the prior question of acceptable unprotected risk more open. The present draft moves between these routes without explaining how much it claims from each.

**Revision test:** Give verdicts on the two towns while keeping the conditional failure fixed. Add a case in which the safeguard has a real but non-grave cost and the risk remains well evidenced but very small. Explain the relevant moral difference in ordinary language. Then make lines 7, 25, 31, 81 and 89 express the same standard. A successful revision may remain pluralist about moral theory, but must identify the commitment needed for its own conclusion.

## 2. Clarify whether the defect concerns authority, a offered assurance, or both

**Locations:** lines 7–19; the continuing-equality premise at lines 13 and 25; line 65; lines 85–89.

**Judge grounding:** The assigned profile recommends separating the defeat of a particular argument from the defeat of a broader position. Applying that distinction to political authority is an inference by this reviewer, not an independently documented Greaves view about democratic legitimacy. The originality assessment below is ordinary philosophical assessment.

The strongest demonstrated conclusion is that a certain assurance cannot justify taking a certain risk: officials cannot answer an objection by pointing to correction that becomes inaccessible if the objection is right. The draft also seeks a constraint on what democratic authorities may do. Those conclusions need not have identical scope.

Compare two otherwise identical enactments. In the first, officials offer the petition as protection. In the second, officials candidly say that no reliable correction will remain if the feared injury occurs, but maintain that the fair initial decision and its public benefits justify accepting that risk. Removing the defective assurance cannot itself improve residents' standing. Does the proposed requirement apply equally to both enactments? The language about a remedy offered to justify proceeding can suggest a rule triggered by the assurance. The claim that authority must preserve its grounds suggests an independent duty that applies whether an assurance is made or not.

The second interpretation may well be right, and the draft plainly gestures toward it at lines 13 and 25. But a continuing moral duty of political equality does not by itself specify how a breach affects an earlier decision's authority. A decision can be binding for some reasons while being unjust in another respect. It is also possible that a fairly authorized decision imposes an impermissible risk without automatically losing all binding force. Line 85 usefully preserves these possibilities. The difficulty is that the larger language about consuming a justification can appear to establish more than this carefully restricted conclusion.

The paper should state which dependency it defends: continuing standing is a condition of a policy's authority; preserving it is an independent obligation governing the exercise of otherwise existing authority; or promised correctability is one defeasible reason for accepting a risky policy. These alternatives can overlap, but an argument for one should not quietly stand in for an argument for another. On the narrow interpretation, a successful result could be a precise account of why some appeals to future correction fail, with a separate argument for an antecedent duty to preserve the means of complaint.

This clarification also matters to the contribution claim. The safety-alarm analogy already shows that testing protection under the relevant failure condition is not peculiar to democracy. The political addition must be the ethical significance of losing the ability to contest continuing subjection, or a distinctive consequence for the order of risk and proof. Neither a new label nor an application of ordinary safeguard adequacy alone would establish a substantial new philosophical contribution.

The literature positioning is commendably candid. In the independently checked passages, Pettit already requires practical opportunities to challenge decisions, distinguishes forms of control, and describes institutions that reduce the need to mount individual challenges through advance protections. The draft does not misrepresent this overlap. Its strongest potential addition is the particular dependence between political injury, evidence of that injury, and access to correction—not the discovery of preemptive safeguards. See [Pettit, “Depoliticizing Democracy,” §§2.2–2.3, printed pp. 61–63](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1616).

**Revision test:** Give a single answer about whether removing the promise changes the normative verdict in the paired enactments, and explain why. Then specify precisely what is defective: the risk, the justification offered, the exercise of authority, or its binding force. Finish the contribution statement with the exact further inference the paper adds to acknowledged contestatory commitments. It is acceptable to narrow the thesis if that produces a better defended result.

## 3. Usable entry to a remedy is not yet reliable correction through the whole institution

**Locations:** lines 49–59, especially the body able and obliged to address impairment at line 57 and the definition of independence at line 59; lines 65–69.

**Judge grounding:** The demand to distinguish a representation of protection from its practical significance fits the profile at a broad level. The particular objection about institutional incentives and remedial implementation is this reviewer's generic philosophical and institutional assessment. No claim is made that Greaves has publicly endorsed it.

The draft rightly treats complaint initiation, evidence and remedial power as distinct functions. Its examples nevertheless make entry and evidence more concrete than the final step. The committee can accept assisted local submissions and possess legal authority to order a remedy while the same political mechanism that disabled complainants also makes implementation unlikely. Power on paper and a reasoned response do not establish sufficient reliability.

Suppose the town provides home visits and a public advocate, so residents can document lost access without using the closed bus. A legislative committee recognizes the problem and orders interim transport. Implementation still requires a routine appropriation controlled by the governing coalition, whose support becomes more secure as the outlying district loses political access. It repeatedly postpones the appropriation. This is a stipulated countercase, not an empirical claim about a jurisdiction. The original petition dependency has been removed, but a later dependency on the affected citizens' diminished political leverage remains. The failure occurs along the very path that the draft asks us to inspect.

The phrase “able and obliged” could be intended to rule this out. If it means effective power that is likely to be exercised and implemented despite the hypothesized impairment, that is the right direction. But then it is a substantial condition, not something shown simply by naming a committee with formal powers. Line 59's definition of independence from the specifically threatened capacity is useful, yet must be applied to evidence, decision and execution alike. Otherwise the offered protection can appear robust only because the model stops before the last political dependency.

This does not prove that a court, a politically insulated body, or an automatically funded remedy is necessary. Each has its own limitations, and no remedy is infallible. The essay's opposition to a universal judicial solution is reasonable. The needed addition is a criterion that distinguishes an ordinary chance of a mistaken decision, which the draft accepts, from a predictable loss of remedial effectiveness caused by the very political impairment under dispute. The latter repeats the central defect even when the first doorway remains open.

**Revision test:** Follow one complaint all the way through initiation, evidence, judgment, funding and implementation. Explain what prevents the specified threatened impairment from disabling each indispensable step. Show how the arrangement can satisfy this test without guaranteeing favorable judgments or creating an institution beyond democratic control. If the draft intends only to require access to a fair hearing, narrow its language about correction; if it requires an effective remedy, explain what evidence of that effectiveness is needed before proceeding.
