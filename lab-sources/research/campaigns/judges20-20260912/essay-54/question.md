# Question and initial contribution plan

Broad field: political philosophy, legitimacy and disagreement. Style: CLEAR.

## Chosen question

When a democratic decision predictably weakens the people who oppose it as future political participants, can the fact that they enjoyed an equal say before the decision justify the resulting settlement?

## Tentative contribution to investigate

Distinguish the authority to settle a disputed policy now from the authority to make that settlement difficult to contest by disabling its opponents. An equal initial vote may justify an adverse decision without justifying the decision's effects on the losers' continuing capacity to seek correction. The intended contribution is a defended account of when this distinction creates a special justificatory burden, what counts as preserving standing rather than subsidizing political success, and why the account need not forbid irreversible policy or give present minorities a veto.

This is a research hypothesis, not an originality finding. Primary-source reading should test it against democratic theories of continuing control, nondomination, equal political opportunity and legitimate disagreement. A new phrase for an existing principle would not count as the desired contribution. If the literature already supplies the whole argument, narrow or reject the plan and preserve that decision.

## Questions the argument must answer

- Why is ordinary equal voting insufficient in the relevant cases?
- Which losses concern political standing, and which are ordinary losses of policy, wealth, organizational advantage or popularity?
- Is a special burden justified by this decision's effects, or does the proposal merely restate a general duty to maintain democracy?
- How can a workable remedy protect the ability to contest without preserving groups, institutions or the status quo indefinitely?
- Can the principle survive necessary displacement, emergency decisions, and opponents whose preferred policy would itself eliminate equal standing?

## Initial rejected angles

1. **Every legitimate policy must be reversible.** Rejected: many necessary collective choices cannot be undone; reversibility is neither a general possibility nor an obvious requirement of authority.
2. **Reasonable disagreement requires consensual decisions.** Rejected: this gives disagreement an unexplained veto and evades the problem of legitimate settlement.
3. **A majority can never disadvantage an opposing association.** Rejected: associations may benefit from unjust privilege, and fair policy may defeat their purposes without denying members political equality.
4. **Losing minorities must retain a realistic prospect of winning.** Rejected as an initial formulation: equal standing cannot guarantee popularity or eventual victory. The distinction between a chance to contest and a chance to win needs argument.
