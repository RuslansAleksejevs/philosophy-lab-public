# Before You Tell Us to Try Again

A town votes to close the bus service linking an outlying district to its centre. The money will support a service used by more people. The debate has been open, the district's residents have voted, and the majority has a serious case. Some residents nevertheless object that losing the bus will make it much harder to attend public meetings, use the library's internet connection, and organize around common problems. Officials dispute this prediction. They propose to proceed and restore access if experience shows that the residents were right. A substantial petition will trigger reconsideration.

That answer contains a problem distinct from the merits of either bus service. The residents' ability to secure reconsideration may depend on the very capacities they say the policy will remove. If they are right, the promised remedy becomes hardest to obtain when it is most needed. “You can try again” is an inadequate answer when the decision may take away the means of trying.

This essay defends a requirement on democratic decisions under that kind of uncertainty. When a proposed policy poses a credible risk of seriously impairing citizens' political standing, a remedy offered to justify proceeding must remain usable under the hypothesis that the impairment occurs. If the ordinary route to correction depends on the threatened capacities, a sufficiently reliable alternative must be secured before those capacities are put at risk, unless doing so would impose comparably grave costs. The requirement protects the possibility of correcting a political injury without first having to overcome that injury through ordinary political success.

The requirement is limited. It does not make every policy reversible, preserve every opposition organization, or entitle losers to become winners. Its distinctive point concerns the terms on which uncertainty can justify proceeding. An authority may reject an objection to its policy while still having to prepare for the objection's being right.

Democracy's claim on a losing citizen cannot simply be that the majority is correct. Citizens can disagree responsibly about where a bus service belongs, what taxation requires, or which public institutions should survive. Giving the majority authority allows action without requiring agreement about the outcome. An adequate defense of that authority must therefore give the dissenter a reason to accept some decisions she continues to regard as mistaken.

Equal participation can supply such a reason. Others' judgments cannot count only when they converge with one's own. But equality concerns a continuing relationship among citizens. Participation in one decision is not a payment that buys permission to diminish someone's standing in later decisions. Citizens who lose remain entitled to form political judgments, express and associate around them, and bring objections before institutions with power to respond.

These are familiar democratic commitments. Thomas Christiano argues that the public equality grounding democratic authority also limits it, and distinguishes the violation of those grounds from other injustices. His discussion of persistent minorities shows why nominal inclusion may fail to provide meaningful political input. Philip Pettit likewise makes individual contestation central to democracy and defends arrangements that reduce the burden of mounting challenges, including safeguards established in advance. My argument draws on these commitments; it does not introduce them. [Christiano 2011, pp. 2–4, 12–14](https://www.jesp.org/pdf/36346f73-90e0-819f-97d2-cda0dceac43a); [Pettit 2004, §§2.2–2.3](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1616).

There is also substantial work on how ordinary policies reshape politics. Daryl Levinson and Benjamin Sachs show that political entrenchment can arise through changes in substantive policy, without explicit barriers to repeal. They also explain the difficulty of separating objectionable entrenchment from ordinary political change. Gabriel Levine brings democratic consequences into regulatory assessment and recommends triggers for later review. Simply demanding that policymakers consider future participation, or that they schedule a second decision, would repeat those proposals. [Levinson and Sachs 2015, Introduction and Part III](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/political-entrenchment-and-public-law); [Levine 2023, Part II.B, Principles 4–5](https://direct.mit.edu/ajle/article/doi/10.1162/ajle_a_00059/117629/DEMOCRATICALLY-DURABLE-REGULATION).

The question developed here is narrower: what makes an offered path to correction adequate when people disagree about whether the policy will damage political standing? A procedure may be well designed for ordinary circumstances yet fail precisely in the disputed circumstances it is supposed to address. The requirement I defend identifies that dependence and gives it a consequence for when, and in what form, a policy may be imposed.

Consider the town's promise more closely. There are two questions. First, will withdrawing the service seriously impair some residents' political standing? Second, if it does, will those residents retain a usable route to establish the impairment and seek repair? Officials might reasonably answer the first question “probably not” while having to answer the second “no.” Their confidence about the first does not settle the second. A petition procedure that works only when no serious impairment has occurred cannot serve as protection against that impairment.

This is not a demand that the majority adopt its opponents' assessment of the bus service. It is a demand that an assurance be tested in the circumstances for which it is offered. We would not regard an emergency alarm as adequate protection against a power failure merely because it works while power is available. The political case adds something to this ordinary requirement of sensible preparation: the threatened capacity helps justify the authority that asks people to bear the risk.

The reasoning has three steps. First, democratic authority based on equal standing must leave citizens with an adequate opportunity to challenge the terms of their continuing subjection. Otherwise, a present exercise of that authority can consume its own justification. Second, a proposed remedy does not preserve that opportunity if obtaining it requires capacities that the alleged injury would seriously impair. Third, where that possibility is supported by credible evidence, uncertainty about whether the injury will occur cannot itself make the remedy adequate. The appropriate response is to preserve a route to correction that survives the relevant failure, or to supply a justification for proceeding without one that goes beyond the initial vote.

The third step matters most. A demand to prove the injury before protecting the means of challenging it can be self-defeating. Proof may become available only after the service closes. By then, collecting testimony, finding other affected residents, and securing official attention may be much harder. The residents are being asked to wait for evidence while accepting the risk of losing the means to produce and use it. A fair allocation of authority cannot always make the vulnerable party bear that combination of risks.

Why must the authority take precautions before the injury is established? Because waiting is itself a choice about who bears the consequences of error. If officials overestimate the danger and establish an unnecessary alternative route to complaint, they incur its costs. If they underestimate the danger and establish none, they may leave some citizens subject to a policy while seriously impairing their ability to question it. Where a modest safeguard can prevent that second outcome, declining it needs a reason. Merely repeating the majority's optimistic prediction is not enough: the purpose of the safeguard is to address the chance that this prediction is mistaken.

“Credible risk” therefore cannot mean any imaginable bad outcome. A claim should identify the threatened political activity, explain how the proposed policy could obstruct it, and give evidence that existing alternatives may fail. In the bus case, evidence might concern travel times, the absence of affordable substitutes, or reliance on the centre's public facilities. Disliking a policy is insufficient. Nor must officials provide every conceivable safeguard. Their obligations vary with the seriousness and likelihood of impairment, the available means of protection, and their costs. There need not be a single numerical threshold for the clear case to be distinguishable from a speculative complaint.

The relevant assessment must also include the consequences of retaining the existing policy. The new service may improve political access for people whom the old route neglected. Keeping everything unchanged is not presumptively fair. The question is whether a feasible policy can deliver the improvement while securing an adequate route to contest serious impairments, and how its risks compare with the alternatives. This comparison prevents a concern about the losers' standing from turning every current arrangement into an entitlement.

But what is political standing, and how much of it must a policy preserve? A broad answer would make the requirement implausible. Almost any policy changes resources, incentives, or associations, and almost any such change can affect politics. An estate tax might reduce a wealthy family's capacity to finance advertising. A rule ending a professional monopoly might diminish the influence of an association that defended it. If every loss of political advantage called for restoration, existing concentrations of power could protect themselves by invoking democracy.

The object of protection is a fair practical opportunity to act as a citizen, not the ability to reproduce one's previous level of influence. This includes access to the basic means of obtaining relevant public information, communicating objections, associating with others, and invoking procedures that can address one's treatment. The standard is violated by serious obstacles that leave some people dependent on exceptional sacrifice or a patron's goodwill to perform ordinary civic activities while others can perform them as a matter of course. It is not violated simply because fewer people now support their views.

This account does not promise equal influence over every outcome. Nor does it treat any functioning channel as sufficient: an unused complaints address is little comfort to residents who cannot access it or discover what happened to their submissions. The practical question is whether affected citizens retain usable means to participate and challenge on fair terms. What counts as usable depends partly on the demands the polity normally makes of its citizens. A system that requires complex written submissions cannot assess access solely by whether a person remains legally entitled to post a letter.

The wealthy family's lost advertising budget need not impair standing in this sense. Family members may retain ample means to obtain information, associate, vote, and press claims. If a policy does threaten those ordinary means, however, their wealth does not disqualify them from protection. The distinction concerns the activity and obstacle, not whether officials approve of the claimant or expect to benefit from her silence.

Likewise, preserving standing need not preserve an existing organization. An association can lose a subsidy or an unjust privilege while its members retain practical freedom to organize. The remedy need not keep its membership, leaders, or views intact. Conversely, supplying isolated individuals with a formal right to complain may be inadequate where the policy destroys the only affordable means through which they can discover a shared problem and coordinate a response. Association matters because citizens often exercise their rights together, not because any particular association is immortal.

This gives a useful test for a proposed safeguard: can it secure the threatened civic activity without restoring the disputed policy benefit as such? In the town, the answer might be subsidized travel for public business, accessible local meetings, or an effective alternative to visiting the centre. Such arrangements could preserve participation while leaving the ordinary bus service closed. They would not settle whether closing it was wise. They would make it possible to learn and argue about its effects without exposing residents to the particular political risk they raised.

The test does not always produce a separable remedy. Sometimes a facility is so important to a community's political life that closing it and preserving equivalent access cannot be achieved together. In that case, the political cost belongs in the decision about closure. A promise to consider the problem afterward cannot make the conflict disappear. At the other extreme, where adequate alternatives already exist, the requirement calls for demonstrating their adequacy rather than inventing a new institution.

What would an adequate route to correction require? At a minimum, affected people need a way to initiate a claim, obtain or contribute relevant evidence, and secure a reasoned response from a body that can remedy the alleged impairment. The route must be reasonably reliable under the conditions in which it will be needed. None of these functions must be supplied by a court. Depending on the polity, an accessible complaints office, a legislative committee, a public advocate, or a review body might perform them.

In the bus case, simply reducing the number of signatures needed may not help enough. The obstacle could be finding other residents, collecting information, or reaching any official forum. A better arrangement might establish an initial record of access needs, offer a local means of reporting difficulties, provide help in assembling evidence, and give a designated body authority to require interim access while the matter is considered. The relevant support should be available because of a functional obstacle, irrespective of how a person voted. Today's supporter can encounter tomorrow's harm.

A scheduled review can help, but a date on a calendar does not by itself preserve participation. Officials could reconvene after a year, receive testimony almost entirely from beneficiaries, and conclude that the reform is successful. If those harmed have lost the ability to contribute evidence, the review reproduces the difficulty. To address the present problem, the arrangement must connect a later decision to protected means of discovering and presenting the relevant injury. Its timing must also reflect how quickly that injury could obstruct challenge. Sometimes the protection must begin immediately, well before a scheduled general review.

There is an associated limit on what officials may infer from silence. An absence of complaints cannot vindicate a disputed policy without considering whether the policy has impaired the means of complaint. The same evidence is compatible with residents' having adjusted successfully and with their having become less able to make themselves heard. Officials need some basis for distinguishing these possibilities. This does not make silence evidence of injury. It prevents silence from doing justificatory work that depends on an untested assumption about access.

The proposed route cannot guarantee correct decisions. A committee may reject a warranted claim, and a court may misunderstand the evidence. Requiring infallibility would defeat the very possibility of authority under disagreement. What must be preserved is a fair and sufficiently reliable process with genuine remedial capacity. A right to bring a claim differs from a right to win it; a body able and obliged to address established impairment differs from a body permitted only to recommend that someone else consider doing so.

Nor does independence here mean isolation from all democratic control. It means independence from the specific capacity whose possible loss is at issue. A legislative committee may offer such independence by accepting assisted local submissions and ordering an effective remedy, even though elected representatives make the final decision. Conversely, an formally independent court offers little protection if the policy removes the claimant's only affordable route to evidence or representation. The relevant question concerns the whole path to repair, not the prestige of its final institution.

There is a powerful objection to this proposal. People will disagree about fair political opportunity, serious impairment, and adequate precautions just as they disagree about bus services. Why should a critic's account of political standing override a decision made through equal voting? And if some special body must decide whether the precaution is sufficient, what entitles its judgment to prevail?

Jeremy Waldron makes the relevant warning forcefully: disagreements about democracy's conditions do not eliminate the importance of a fair procedure for settling those disagreements. His argument against judicial review is conditional on reasonably functioning institutions, and the failure of those conditions does not automatically justify transferring authority to courts. Comparative institutional judgment remains necessary. [Waldron 2006, pp. 1359–1361, 1399–1402](https://www.humanities.mcmaster.ca/~walucho/3Q3/Waldron.Core%20Case%20Judicial%20Review%20Yale%20LJ.pdf).

My proposal accepts that warning. It neither appoints a final guardian of democracy nor allows any claimant to stop a policy by alleging exclusion. It specifies a responsibility of whichever institutions legitimately make the decision. A legislature can assess the evidence, reject remote risks, compare safeguards, and vote on a policy that includes them. Disagreement about the responsibility's application does not make the responsibility disappear, any more than disagreement about the fairness of an election removes the need to conduct one fairly.

More importantly, the requirement can permit action without first resolving the whole disagreement. Officials may continue to think that losing the bus will not impair standing. Residents may continue to think that it will. If a protected route to repair works on either hypothesis, both sides can assess the policy without settling that prediction in advance. They need not agree that closure is harmless. They need an adequate institutional answer to what happens if it is harmful in the relevant way.

That answer will itself sometimes be contested. There is no procedure beyond all possible challenge. But the claim being assessed is narrower than the original policy dispute: whether a particular means of making and deciding a complaint will remain available under a specified impairment. Some questions about distance, assistance, evidence, and remedial power can be resolved without choosing between comprehensive views about public transport or distributive justice. Where disagreement persists, ordinary legitimate institutions must resolve it as fairly as they can. The proposal identifies what their justification must address; it does not promise a standpoint outside politics.

A second objection is that this gives the losers a special advantage. They received an equal say, lost, and now receive public resources for another attempt. Winners must fund challenges to a policy they already justified. Why is that fair?

Because losing is not the ground of the claim. The ground is exposure to a serious obstacle to ordinary citizenship. The same protection would be due to residents who supported closure, and to beneficiaries of the new route if some further decision placed their access at comparable risk. The resources should support the affected civic functions, with rules that do not require adopting the opposition's views. They should not pay a party to regain office or guarantee another vote on demand. A remedy can be adequate even if, after accessible consideration, the decision remains unchanged.

There will still be political effects. Restoring access may help the opposition organize. But access was never valuable only when its beneficiaries used it to endorse the government's policy. An authority cannot count citizens as equals at enactment and then treat their continued ability to disagree as an unfair subsidy. The appropriate limit is preserving fair participation rather than compensating every political disadvantage. That distinction requires judgment, but it is not made incoherent by the fact that participation has consequences.

A third objection concerns necessary action. Suppose the service must close because continuing it creates an immediate danger, or because its cost would eliminate access for many more people. Suppose no adequate alternative can be provided before closure. Does the requirement demand paralysis?

It does not. Sometimes every feasible option threatens important interests or conditions of agency. Then officials must compare those threats, explain the necessity, limit the duration of impairment where possible, and undertake the earliest feasible repair. The initial vote remains relevant, but it cannot by itself convert an unusable remedy into an adequate one. Proceeding may be justified by urgency and the absence of a less damaging alternative. Those reasons should be stated as such. The enduring fact that someone remains politically disadvantaged should not vanish into a claim that she already had her turn to speak.

That qualification is a limit on the requirement, not a license to substitute convenience for protection. Many public decisions impose modest costs to maintain fair access even when no particular citizen can prove in advance that she will need it. When the proposed safeguard is inexpensive and the threatened impairment serious, necessity is unavailable as a reply. When safeguards would themselves seriously damage other citizens' standing, the comparison becomes harder. The burden is to compare feasible arrangements, including delay and inaction, without assuming that the preferred policy arrives free of its conditions of legitimate implementation.

The account also leaves ordinary irreversible decisions possible. A town can demolish a building after fair deliberation even though later voters cannot reconstruct the same building. Irreversibility of the object differs from inability to contest the decision or its remaining consequences. Citizens may seek compensation, changes to related plans, recognition of an error, or rules preventing repetition. The relevant protection concerns their standing to pursue those claims. It does not promise to restore every lost option.

Nor does the argument automatically defeat an entire government's authority when one policy lacks adequate protection. The immediate conclusion concerns the particular demand that affected people accept that policy on the strength of its democratic pedigree and promised correctability. That justification is incomplete. Other reasons may still require compliance, and other decisions may retain authority. A further argument would be needed to determine when resistance is permissible or when a pattern of impairment undermines the political order as a whole.

The initial bus decision can now be described more accurately. The town has authority to choose among disputed uses of public resources. It does not thereby acquire an unrestricted authority to decide which citizens will retain practical means of challenging what follows. Closure with protected civic access and closure with a petition procedure that may become unusable are different proposals, even if they have the same transport budget. The second cannot borrow the first's claim to remain open to correction.

The contribution is therefore a constraint on the order in which a democracy may expose people to risk and ask them to establish injury. Where a decision credibly threatens the capacities needed to challenge its effects, sufficient means of raising and deciding that challenge must normally be secured first. This can support experimentation and reform: a polity need not achieve certainty before acting if it protects an effective route for discovering and addressing its mistakes. But it must test that route in the world where its critics are right. Asking citizens to try again carries a responsibility to preserve their ability to do so.
