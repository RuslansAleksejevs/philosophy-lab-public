## Central change: what the argument establishes

Both reviewers correctly distinguish a remedy's conditional inadequacy from the permissibility of accepting an unprotected risk. The final explicitly gives up the original apparent rule that every credible serious threat normally requires an advance safeguard unless competing costs are comparably grave. Neither the conditional failure of a petition nor the importance of political equality establishes that decision rule.

The defended claim now concerns the justificatory weight of a corrective assurance. A route advertised as protection against political impairment must be assessed under that impairment, across every indispensable step. If the injury disables it, the route cannot supply the protection claimed. Officials must assess the proposal without that claimed protection or change the route. Advance preparation is required by this argument where acceptable implementation depends on retaining that prospect of correction. This is an explicitly conditional ordering requirement.

This narrowing is a substantive revision, not a restatement of the original strength. It leaves some otherwise identical policy risks permissible on independently sufficient grounds. It does not establish a complete theory of risk, remove every decision's binding force, or justify disobedience.

## Response to first review

1. **Conditional failure versus precaution rule — accepted.** The final separates the normative evaluation of risk from functional adequacy. Its paired towns keep the injury and defective petition fixed while varying an evidenced probability from substantial to one in ten thousand. Both assurances fail; the argument does not impose the same precaution duty. Substantial risk with negligible opportunity cost supports protection through an expressly additional proportional-care premise. In the rare-risk case with certain access costs elsewhere, omission can be defensible. Credibility of evidence is no longer used as a proxy for probability, and no grave-cost exception is presented as exhaustive. The budget and risk-reducing-reform cases make opportunity costs and the total baseline explicit.

2. **Authority, assurance and conduct — accepted, with a narrower retained conclusion.** The final's two councils have identical substantive obligations. Removing the misleading assurance does not improve residents' access or erase independently owed duties. It changes whether officials also offer a defective reason. The text distinguishes a failed reason for accepting an exercise of authority, possible impermissibility when other reasons are insufficient, and the further questions of binding force, resistance and regime-wide legitimacy. The broad phrase suggesting that a mere risk necessarily consumes all authority has been removed.

3. **Whole remedial route — accepted.** The final traces a complaint through discovery, initiation, evidence, adjudication, funding, execution and monitoring. A panel's formal power and a favorable ruling are insufficient when fresh appropriation requires the diminished group's political pressure. The illustrative arrangement provides resources and authority in advance, but is expressly conditional on actual reliability and compliance. The final applies the impairment hypothesis to implementation rather than stopping at a fair hearing. It distinguishes ordinary residual institutional error from the specific dependence that was previously left unexamined.

## Response to second review

1. **Invalidating assurance does not establish a particular alternative — accepted.** The final preserves the intermediate possibilities: protection, independent justification of residual risk, redesign, postponement, or rejection. It does not infer a prohibition from a failed assurance. It answers the two-council test identically to the first review's paired enactments.

2. **Probability, baseline and competing protections — accepted.** Substantial risk with negligible protection costs, evidenced small risk with competing access burdens, a fixed budget, and a reform lowering background exclusion all receive separate treatment. The shared verdict is about the assurance's lack of protection against the particular injury. The practical verdict can differ. This is deliberate scope discipline rather than a numerical risk threshold that the argument cannot defend. I do not adopt expected-value maximization or any other complete decision theory on the strength of a judge simulation.

3. **Distinct contribution and private-remedy comparison — accepted in part.** The final acknowledges that requiring destroyed records to claim compensation has the same general structure. It does not claim a novel universal principle of remedial design. The proposed contribution is a specification of democratic justification: assess correction using the political circumstances produced by the hypothesized injury, including evidence production and implementation; derive the timing requirement only where justification relies on that correction. It explains how both a scheduled review and a record of favorable complaint decisions can miss the dependence. The contribution remains potentially modest and its originality unproved, but the application exposes a definite gap in a familiar argument for accepting political experimentation.

4. **Pettit's institutional conclusion — clarification accepted; substantive agreement not presumed.** The final explicitly notes that Pettit argues for contestation bodies distinct from elected forums responsible for the decision. It does not attribute its narrower functional test, or its allowance that a democratically controlled body may sometimes qualify, to Pettit. I retain the narrower test because independence of organizational identity alone cannot establish independence from a threatened capacity. This is a warranted limitation of the essay's inference, not a claimed refutation of Pettit's wider theory.
