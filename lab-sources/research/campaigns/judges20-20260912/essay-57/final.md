# What a Corrected Number Leaves Out

Two clocks agree after correction. Has a theory passed a test, or has a technician completed a task? The same sentence can report either achievement. It can even report both. What decides the matter is what would have happened to the reported agreement if the clocks had behaved differently.

This question arises inside successful measurement. A corrected value can accurately describe the quantity scientists intended to measure while withholding evidence about why their instruments behaved as they did. The mistake is to treat the warrant for assigning that value as a warrant for every explanatory use of it. I will defend a more discriminating requirement: when corrected agreement is offered as evidence between explanations, the available evidence must preserve a difference those explanations make to the probabilities of measurement outcomes. Whether an actual result favors one explanation then depends on its likelihood under each. Often the relevant information survives in the corrected result. Sometimes it survives only when correction records are included and correctly associated with their results. Recognizing this second possibility allows us to defend correction without granting its products unrestricted evidential authority.

The proposal concerns a specific inferential move, not a general account of explanation. Suppose an explanation says that changing a condition changes an instrument's response in a certain way. A rival predicts a different response. Scientists process the responses and obtain agreement. Before using that agreement to favor the first explanation, they must establish that the observed agreement is more likely under the first explanation than under the rival, conditional on the same available background evidence and the specified protocol. Agreement can be possible under both and still favor one. The relevant object of scrutiny is the procedure that actually generated the evidence, including how its corrections were selected.

Clocks make the issue vivid because the same physical difference can be an inconvenience for one task and an opportunity for another. Atomic clocks at different gravitational potentials have different rates when compared. In a 2010 experiment, C. W. Chou and colleagues raised one optical clock by 33 centimetres and measured a change in its rate. They also interpreted the frequency change as a measurement of height: the resulting estimate, 37 ± 15 centimetres, agreed with the independently established change. Their experiment connected a test of relativistic behavior with the possibility of measuring geographical differences through clocks. [Chou et al., pp. 1631–1632](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192720)

For a common time standard, such differences require a different treatment. Jisun Lee and colleagues describe evaluating gravitational corrections for frequency standards at KRISS, including a clock contributing to the calibration of International Atomic Time. They use surveying and models of gravitational potential to determine the relevant correction. Their work exemplifies the effort needed to relate a local clock to a shared reference. [Lee et al., abstract and introduction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04943)

Nothing here suggests that these researchers mistook corrected agreement for an independent discovery, or discarded the evidence they needed. Their practices motivate the philosophical problem. When we want clocks to provide a common reference, a rate difference is something to compensate for. When we want to investigate the relation between gravity and clock rates, a rate difference is something to explain. Calling it a correction in the first inquiry cannot settle its evidential role in the second.

The terminology matters. The International Vocabulary of Metrology distinguishes the intended quantity, the instrument's indication, calibration, correction and adjustment. Calibration establishes relations used to obtain measurement results from indications. Correction compensates for an estimated systematic effect. Adjustment changes a measuring system so that it supplies prescribed indications. These activities can occur together, but they should not be conflated. In particular, calibration does not simply mean forcing instruments to agree. [JCGM 200:2012, entries 2.3, 2.39, 2.53, 3.11 and 4.1](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/2071204/JCGM_200_2012.pdf)

Eran Tal's account of calibration treats the relation between instrument indications and measurement outcomes as a matter of constructing and testing models. His account of time standardization similarly gives a central role to the interaction between adjustments to models and adjustments to instruments. Both positions make room for theory to contribute to reliable measurement. My question grants that possibility: once a result is reliable for its intended quantity, what further work is needed to use its agreement with other results as explanatory evidence? [Tal 2017, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsa.2017.09.001); [Tal 2016, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/axu037)

We can isolate that further work with a small example. Imagine a measurement service that reports the value an object has at a reference setting. An environmental condition changes the instrument's response. In one possible situation, its readings at the reference setting and an altered setting are 100 and 101. In another, they are 100 and 102. The reference value is truly 100 in both situations; what differs is the response to the changed condition. These are arbitrary numbers in an idealized example, not numerical claims about atomic clocks. For now, set aside random error.

The service wants observations at either setting to yield a correct report of the reference value. One way to proceed is to apply a correction established independently of this pair of observations. Suppose the rule says to subtract one unit at the altered setting. In the first situation the corrected readings are 100 and 100. In the second they are 100 and 101. The correction has not erased the difference between the situations. It has moved the point at which the difference appears. Agreement after this fixed correction supports the response of one unit over the response of two, provided the other assumptions needed for the comparison are defensible.

This is why the bare complaint that a theory was used to correct its own test data is insufficient. A theory can help specify a transformation under which a rival's discrepancy remains visible. If a fixed correction subtracts what the accepted theory predicts, a deviation from that prediction can remain as a nonzero residual. The test may still have weaknesses, but the presence of the theory in the calculation does not establish them. We need to ask what the calculation does to the rival's predicted observations.

Now change the service's procedure. It estimates the response from the observed difference between the two readings and subtracts that estimated difference at the altered setting. It therefore subtracts one in the first situation and two in the second. The corrected pair is 100 and 100 either way. Assume the reference reading is accurate and the procedure is intended only to report the reference value. It succeeds completely at that task. Yet the agreement of its two corrected results cannot discriminate between the two responses. More digits after the decimal point would not repair this failure. Neither would repeating the same procedure indefinitely in the two idealized situations: every pair would still be made to agree.

This contrast is more precise than a contrast between theoretical and observational results. Both procedures involve assumptions. Both can produce true reports of the reference value. What differs is their treatment of the very variation at issue. In the first, the correction is held fixed when the response changes. In the second, the correction changes with the response and absorbs it. A report saying merely that an environmental correction was applied leaves this epistemic difference unresolved.

The second service need not be useless for explanatory inquiry. Let it retain the amount subtracted. Its records then distinguish “reference value 100; correction one” from “reference value 100; correction two.” The response difference remains available. Someone investigating it can examine that part of the record, together with the setting and the procedure used to estimate the correction. Successful standardization has transferred the evidential burden from the corrected agreement alone to a record that also identifies the correction required to obtain it.

That transfer is the central point. We sometimes describe corrected results as if they were the empirical core, surrounded by administrative information about how they were obtained. The example reverses those roles. Relative to the question about the environmental response, the corrected value is uninformative and the correction amount is the decisive empirical result. Its conventional classification as a correction does not demote it epistemically. Conversely, its being recorded does not automatically make it good evidence: we still need grounds for trusting how it was obtained. Nor will the correction always discriminate on its own. Suppose a reference value X is equally likely to be zero or one under either of two hypotheses. Under the first, a fitted correction C equals X; under the second, C equals 1−X. The altered reading is X+C, so correction produces agreement at X under either hypothesis. Both X and C separately have the same distribution under the rivals. Their pairing distinguishes the rivals perfectly: equality supports the first, inequality the second. Keeping separate summaries of results and corrections can therefore destroy evidence even when each summary is accurate. The general lesson concerns the joint record, including the associations between entries.

The argument can be stated without the numbers. Hold fixed the competing explanations, the specified protocol and the available background evidence on which both probability assessments are conditioned. Follow each explanation's predicted observations through the actual procedure for generating the reported evidence. Include any fitting or adjustment that the procedure performs. If the explanations yield the same distribution of retained outcomes, those outcomes provide no likelihood advantage to either explanation. For a deterministic example, this is simply the fact that both yield the same outcome. An earlier record may have distinguished them, but its evidential advantage does not pass into a later record that removes the distinction.

The qualification about the actual procedure is essential. We must not freeze an estimated correction at its observed value when, under the alternative, the estimation procedure would have produced a different value. That would test a procedure different from the one used. Equally, we must not imagine that a fixed correction would have been freely adjusted to accommodate every possible response. That would manufacture circularity where none existed. The question is what the specified procedure would have done, not what a sufficiently resourceful scientist could have done instead.

Three achievements must be kept separate. Different outcome distributions give a procedure some capacity to discriminate; they need not make the actual observation discriminatory. Suppose three outcomes have probabilities (.5, .4, .1) under one explanation and (.5, .1, .4) under another. The first outcome supplies no likelihood advantage despite the different distributions. By contrast, an observed agreement with probabilities .9 and .2 favors the first explanation by a likelihood ratio of 4.5, though either allows it. Finally, preserving some discriminatory capacity is weaker than preserving all relevant information: statistical sufficiency demands more than the condition defended here. None of these achievements alone guarantees a good explanation. Distinct predictions may be separated by far less than the experimental uncertainty. Rival causes may have been overlooked. The readings may be unreliable. A measured dependence may fail to identify its mechanism. The condition rules out one particular leap: from agreement that the processing guarantees across the relevant alternatives to evidence favoring one of them. It does not solve all the remaining problems of scientific inference.

Random error does not undo the point, but it changes what must be compared. Two explanations can permit the same reported value while making it more likely under one than the other. Merely noting that both can produce agreement would therefore be too weak an objection. We need the probability of the retained results under the whole procedure, including estimation and selection. In practical cases this may require an analytical calculation or simulation. The noiseless example shows the clearest limit: a response removed exactly by a freely estimated correction leaves no discrepancy at all in the corrected pair. A noisy case requires checking which differences remain and at what sensitivity.

A natural objection is that this is ordinary statistical sufficiency. Much of its mathematical basis is indeed familiar. Fisher described data reduction as retaining information relevant to the parameters under investigation, and his criterion of sufficiency asked whether a statistic left additional relevant information elsewhere in the sample. The idea that compression can lose information is not a philosophical discovery. [Fisher, §§2 and 4](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.1922.0009)

What matters here is where the inferential target changes. A measurement service can reasonably compress its records to report a reference value. Someone then treats the precision and agreement of those reports as support for an explanation of responses to changing conditions. The warrant acquired for the first target does not authorize that transfer. The proposal is to make the transfer an explicit point of assessment: inspect the correction procedure against the explanatory contrast, and locate the retained evidence that bears the burden. This applies even where the measurement itself is entirely correct. It is an application and development of a familiar statistical insight, not a competing mathematical theory.

A second objection is that philosophers of evidence already demand attention to processing histories. Nora Mills Boyd's account of enriched evidence explicitly includes records of data collection and processing, together with information about their provenance and transformation. The general importance of retaining such information, including for reusing evidence, is therefore already well articulated. [Boyd, definition of enriched evidence](https://doi.org/10.1086/697747)

The present argument is a worked application of these established concerns, not a claim that changing inferential targets is a new idea. Its concrete diagnostic distinguishes a fixed correction that leaves a testable residual from a fitted correction that requires additional evidence, and identifies an easily missed preservation decision: retain associations as well as values. It also yields a limited prescription rather than a demand to preserve every stage. In the numerical example, the reference value, correction amount, settings and applicable rule suffice to recover the contrast. Saving additional duplicates of the corrected pair adds nothing. Saving only the software, without the fitted correction or the data needed to recompute it, does not suffice: an algorithm does not supply a missing empirical input. These are adequate records in the stipulated examples, not a derivation of a general minimum archive. Which record matters is determined by the contrast, not by whether the record appears earlier or later in a processing sequence.

Nor must useful evidence always take the form of recoverable uncorrected readings. Imagine a bath held at a constant temperature by an automatic cooling system. Within the stipulated example, two sources release different amounts of heat at the maintained temperature, and the controller removes the corresponding amounts while keeping the measured temperature the same. The steady temperature alone does not distinguish the heat sources. Records of the controller's cooling action may do so, given a defensible account of the other heat exchanges. There need never have been a large temperature rise to record and later reconstruct: control prevented it. The relevant difference appears in what was done to secure stability. The target here is heat release under the maintained conditions. Control may change the source itself: two mechanisms could release equal heat at that temperature while diverging as an uncontrolled bath warms. Their identical controller histories would not distinguish that counterfactual behavior. Inferences about it require additional causal assumptions or experiments. The analogy carries over the demand to propagate rival predictions through the actual procedure; it does not identify physical intervention with numerical subtraction.

This is not a proposal for a new kind of calorimeter. It identifies the same epistemic structure in a different setting. Whether stability is secured by transforming numbers or by intervening in a physical system, the resulting stability does not by itself tell us how much work was needed to secure it. A policy of retaining every available temperature reading could preserve an excellent record of a constant temperature while missing the evidence about heat release. The appropriate record follows the route by which the relevant variation was displaced.

There is a deeper objection. Perhaps the service never promised to measure environmental responsiveness or heat release. Why criticize a good measurement for failing to answer a different question? The objection is correct about the original service and mistaken about the subsequent inference. No laboratory is obliged to make every result useful for every purpose. A reference value can be valuable without preserving all information about the conditions from which it was inferred. The requirement applies when someone offers that result as evidence for a contrast it may no longer preserve.

This distinction limits both blame and obligation. If a later investigator introduces an unforeseen question and the earlier service retained no suitable record, the investigator has encountered an evidential limit. It does not follow that the earlier measurement was defective. If, however, the service was designed to provide evidence for a stated explanatory question, its designers should establish that the evidence made available can discriminate the relevant alternatives. If a published argument claims that the actual result favors one, its authors owe the corresponding likelihood comparison. A good answer might identify residuals, a separately estimated correction, or a new experiment. It might instead narrow the conclusion.

The relevant boundary is the available evidential arrangement, not a particular file or institution. A measurement service can publish corrected values while another reliable custodian retains the fitted corrections, provided investigators can access the needed associations and assess their provenance. Duplication is unnecessary; a promise that someone kept unspecified records is insufficient. This allocates responsibility without requiring universal preservation: a service promising explanatory usefulness must arrange access to the needed evidence, and an investigator reusing its results must establish that the evidence available supports the new inference. If an unforeseen use cannot be supported, the appropriate response may be to limit the claim rather than blame the original service.

Stating the intended quantity more carefully helps but cannot discharge this obligation by itself. Consider two services that both correctly report the reference value as 100, with the same stated uncertainty and the same conditions of application. One retains the fitted environmental response; the other discards it. They agree about what they measured and about the accuracy of the assigned value. They nevertheless provide different resources for answering the explanatory question. The meaning of the headline report does not determine the evidential reach of the available records.

This also distinguishes the problem from a familiar worry about extending calibration beyond an established range. Mahmoud Jalloh examines whether instruments that agree in their shared range are measuring the same quantity when one is extended further; his stated response gives dimensional analysis an evidential role. My example grants that the intended quantity is measured correctly throughout the range at issue. The loss arises when a successful result is recruited to discriminate explanations of a variation that its production removed. Better assurance that the quantity was correctly identified would not, on its own, restore the lost information. [Jalloh, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-024-00600-w)

One might still object that scientists can recover support for an explanation from the independent success of the correction method. A method that repeatedly works in new situations may acquire substantial credibility. That is compatible with the argument. Suppose an environmental correction estimated elsewhere predicts the response of a new instrument without being refitted. Its successful performance is a new constraint, like the fixed correction in the first version of the example. Alternatively, records of fitted corrections across instruments may reveal a stable pattern an explanation predicts. In either case the support comes from a contrast that survived somewhere in the evidence. It does not come from treating an automatically produced agreement as if it were an additional independent achievement.

The same qualification applies to simplicity. Two accounts may fit a set of corrected results equally well, while one requires a complicated collection of adjustments and the other requires one well-supported rule. There can be reasons to prefer the latter. But to assess that difference, we need the rules or adjustments, or some trustworthy report of them. A file containing only the common corrected values conceals precisely what the simplicity argument needs. If we already know the histories, the file may remind us of the conclusion; it does not contain all the evidence for it.

The proposal therefore requires no return to innocent observation. The correction record is itself measured, processed and interpreted. Its uncertainty and dependence on assumptions must be assessed in turn. The demand is local: retain or identify evidence sensitive to the particular alternatives being compared. It is not a demand for evidence independent of every theory. Background assumptions may remain fixed where there are grounds for doing so. When an alternative challenges those assumptions, the evidential assessment must widen accordingly. There is no single archive that guarantees usefulness for every possible future theory.

A modest practical consequence follows. When presenting corrected agreement as explanatory evidence, report enough to answer three connected questions: what distribution of outcomes each rival predicts; what the procedure, including fitting or control, does to those predictions; and whether the actual evidence available, with its needed associations, favors one rival. For prospective design, the weaker question is whether any obtainable outcome could discriminate them. Sometimes a short statement that the correction was fixed from independent observations will answer much of this. Sometimes the answer requires the fitted coefficients or controller history. Sometimes it reveals that the proposed test has no discriminatory force.

This requirement gives precision its proper scope. Reducing uncertainty around a reported quantity is an achievement. Preserving evidence about the processes that produced its indications is another. A measurement can improve along the first dimension while remaining limited along the second. Conversely, a seemingly secondary record of corrections can carry the strongest evidence for the response we want to understand. The task of philosophy here is to keep those achievements distinct enough that one does not silently stand in for the other.

The scope of the contribution is correspondingly limited. The underlying concerns about model dependence, sufficiency and evidential provenance are established. Several closest philosophical works were available for this inquiry only in abstracts or excerpts, so the literature does not support a claim of historical priority. The contribution offered here is a worked philosophical application with a practical diagnostic, not a new theorem or a demonstrated historical first. It explains why retaining fitted values without their associations can fail, why records held elsewhere can suffice, and why control histories answer questions about controlled conditions without automatically answering questions about uncontrolled behavior. These sharpen the assessment of particular inferences; their broader originality and significance remain open.

Two clocks agree after correction. We should welcome the agreement when a common reference is what we need. To learn why the clocks behaved as they did, we must also know whether a different behavior could have survived the route to agreement—and, if it was absorbed, whether the record tells us what absorbed it.
