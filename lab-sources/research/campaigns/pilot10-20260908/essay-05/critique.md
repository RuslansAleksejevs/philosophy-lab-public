# Critique of “What Wrongful Ignorance Cannot Settle”

2026-09-08. Separate AI critic context: `/root/critic_05`. Status: REVISE. This is criticism of this essay alone, not a truth, novelty, eligibility or competition-readiness certificate. The manuscript and the author's source notes remain unchanged.

The essay has a coherent, useful core: an institution's duty to respond to uncertainty can survive personnel changes without supplying evidence about the uncertain history. Its best prospective contribution concerns the interaction between institution-directed relief and incompatible claims to one object. The current draft does not yet establish the distinctiveness or full strength of that contribution. Its principal difficulties are a close existing precedent for the relational restriction, an insufficient argument for transferring decision authority, and an ambiguity between past ownership and present title.

## 1. Strongest reconstruction

The central argument can be reconstructed without treating it as a demand to believe victims regardless of evidence:

1. Some requirements to prove a claim are justified only within an arrangement that protects a fair opportunity to supply the evidence.
2. An institution that breaches its corresponding obligation cannot continue relying, without further justification or accommodation, on the evidential failure it helped create.
3. Replacement of personnel neither repairs the opportunity nor changes the relevant institutional arrangement. The restriction therefore continues without inherited personal blame.
4. This reason concerns the institution's treatment of a claimant. Where the institution equally impairs two incompatible claims, it provides no distinction that selects one claimant over the other.
5. Accordingly, remedy and historical verdict must be separated. A response can recognize an institutional obligation while withholding judgment about which claimant's history is correct.

Premises 1–3 support a credible conditional restriction. Premise 4 supports a symmetry result in the stipulated paired case. Premise 5 follows if historical certification means an evidence-responsive finding. However, a further conclusion—that the museum must surrender unilateral decision authority—requires premises beyond these. The draft sometimes treats that further conclusion as already obtained.

The distinctions among personal blame, evidence and institutional responsibility are background. The essay acknowledges this appropriately. Its potentially valuable work is to identify what fails when a two-party remedy is applied to a single asset with multiple claimants, and to explain what adequate institutional repair then requires.

## 2. Originality: the closest predecessor needs direct engagement

Porat and Stein already distinguish a procedural remedy against a responsible litigant from compensation for evidence lost by a stranger to the original dispute (1999 draft, introduction, p. 7; example at pp. 24–25). Their fairness discussion allocates error risk by responsibility, and their caveat resists factually extravagant outcomes (§VI(b)–(c), pp. 74–79). Their proposal therefore should not be cast as an indiscriminate invitation to believe claimants. [Primary draft](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2db3b43m/qt2db3b43m.pdf).

An even closer concrete antecedent is **Smith v. Atkinson**, 771 So. 2d 429 (Ala. 2000). The opinion presumes that a plaintiff would have prevailed against a manufacturer when determining the liability of the separate evidence custodian. It directs the presumption against the custodian; it does not thereby award recovery from the manufacturer. The conclusion expressly distinguishes remedies available against an opposing litigant from the third-party case. See §III(C), pp. 434–436, and §IV, p. 438. [Primary opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/alabama/supreme-court/2000/1980433-1.html).

These sources do not establish that the museum analysis has already been made. They do make the draft's claim that a relational limit is “neglected” too strong without further qualification. Incorporate a short acknowledgment of the nearby structure, then say precisely what the essay adds: perhaps the fact that the custodian is simultaneously a claimant and controller of the disputed object, so ordinary separation into an underlying lawsuit and a compensatory lawsuit cannot settle its use. That would be a narrower contribution with a clearer problem to solve. Merely restating the two-party limitation in museum vocabulary is insufficient.

The source search was limited. It neither proves duplication of the complete argument nor establishes originality of the proposed extension.

## 3. The logical contradiction targets a weak opponent

Section 4 asks us to accept each family's exclusive historical ownership as true, and exposes the contradiction. A serious defender of burden shifting need not accept that instruction. She can hold that a presumption is a context-sensitive practical rule: Alder is accommodated in its relation with the museum; Birch is accommodated in its relation with the museum; a joint proceeding determines how incompatible demands are reconciled. No one believes both histories. Indeed, the draft's monetary example already demonstrates that pairwise remedial duties can coexist without contradictory historical beliefs.

The subsequent discussion recognizes the practical issue but mostly says that “we need a reason” for imposing a disadvantage on Birch. That is correct, yet too easy unless it confronts a serious candidate reason. Consider this opponent: the institution must reduce the evidential disadvantage it imposed; when several parties claim a scarce object, the extent of each party's impairment should affect that party's remedial priority. Such a view need not mistake remedy for truth and need not hold Birch blameworthy.

The symmetric case answers this view only when impairment, protected opportunities and remedial interests are relevantly equal. Test an asymmetric variant. The museum destroys Alder's uniquely decisive archive in breach of a specific undertaking, while Birch retains nearly all its evidence and loses only a redundant fragment. Their surviving historical claims happen to be equally credible. Does the unequal damage provide a reason for unequal remedial priority? If so, responsibility for evidential loss can affect practical comparisons between innocent claimants. If not, the essay needs an argument explaining why their separate repair claims cannot matter to that comparison.

This is a challenge to the breadth of the principle, not a demonstrated refutation of its narrower reading. The phrase “solely in virtue of that reason” can absorb the objection by classifying every distributive consideration as additional. But then the author should identify what kind of inference is prohibited. Distinguish a change in historical evidential standing, a new remedial priority, and a reduction in the practical chance of receiving the object. They are not interchangeable meanings of “disadvantaging.”

Precise revision: begin with the defensible symmetric proposition—an equally applicable reparative reason cannot by itself select one of two otherwise relevantly equal claimants. Then defend any wider restriction separately. Do not infer a general ban on burden-shifting effects from a demonstration about accepting contradictory histories.

## 4. Lost justification does not by itself establish lost authority

The first principle removes one sufficient reason for closing a claim. It does not establish that a particular decision-maker has lost the authority to close it on adequate grounds. A person can possess authority while having given an inadequate justification on one occasion. Section 5 moves too rapidly from defective reliance on missing records to a duty to fund an independent process and surrender unilateral control.

Consider trustees who disclose the breach, admit the need for accommodation, provide a fair hearing under relaxed proof requirements, publicly explain their decision, and offer appropriate compensation for evidential loss. Suppose they are competent, have no personal stake, and their procedure is cheaper and more accessible than the available external tribunal. The existing principles do not show why their institutional affiliation itself makes this response inadequate. The draft wisely stipulates conscientious successors; that stipulation makes it harder to derive mandatory displacement merely from predecessor misconduct.

There is also an ambiguity about what authority the museum originally had. It may be able to refuse voluntary transfer, but that is not the same as possessing legitimate final jurisdiction over other people's title. If an independent adjudicator was already available, participation in that adjudication need not involve any newly lost authority. If none was available and the museum had a specially recognized power of final determination, that institutional arrangement needs to be stated and defended.

Precise revision: separate three positions: the museum's ordinary possession; its ability to reject a request in negotiation; and a legitimate power to bind claimants through final adjudication. Specify which one changes. Either soften the external-process recommendation to one possible adequate remedy, or add and defend a premise such as this: when the institution controls the only practically available route to assessment and its breach has disabled that route, adequate repair includes access to a procedure that the institution cannot defeat by invoking the disabled requirement. That premise supports external access or review without automatically proving the need to transfer every substantive decision power.

The limitation should be expressed consistently. The abstract-like introduction says a remedy *may* require surrender of control; §5's “should therefore” sounds much more general. The conditional claim is better supported.

## 5. Historical ownership and present title need separate treatment

The essay sometimes treats defeating a competing ownership claim as if it necessarily amounted to refuting that person's history. But a remedy can create a new entitlement without determining the old one. Suppose both families authorize a binding fair lottery, Alder wins, and the award gives Alder present title. Birch can now be excluded as a current owner even if Birch was the historical owner. This does not turn the museum's wrongdoing into historical evidence. The authority of the agreed procedure constitutes the new entitlement.

The draft allows agreed transfers and lotteries, so this distinction is available within its own resources. However, phrases such as “the museum may have lost a defense without either family having acquired a verdict against the other” and “treating Birch's competing ownership claim as defeated” need temporal and normative precision. Is the prohibition against deciding which family owned the painting before destruction, against creating a new present title, or against deciding either matter solely on the reparative presumption? Only the third formulation accommodates the permitted practical resolutions without ambiguity.

Precise revision: explicitly distinguish historical ownership, a claim to institutional repair, and present entitlement generated by an authorized settlement or adjudication. A valid new title can have effects against others; its authority needs justification, while its existence does not certify the prior history. This also improves the promising final passage about accurately describing a remedial decision. The account should prevent false historical descriptions while permitting clear statements of a genuinely binding present outcome.

## 6. What wrongfulness adds, and what the principle leaves unresolved

The dependence argument is strongest where the institution's proof requirement and preservation obligation were a justified package. It is less obvious that all protected evidential opportunities have that structure. A public custodian might owe preservation for historical understanding while a separate tribunal's ordinary proof rule is independently justified. Loss of the archive can create a reparative duty without rendering every application of that proof rule illegitimate. The draft requires a relevant connection, but causal relevance to proving the claim is not yet justificatory dependence of the rule on the institution's performance.

Clarify whether the principle requires the stronger connection. Otherwise a phrase introduced to state the conclusion—“protected opportunity”—may conceal the premise needed to support it. A helpful pair would contrast an institution that induces exclusive reliance on its register with a custodian whose preservation duty is real but does not ground the claimant's burden in the underlying dispute.

The contrast with innocent loss also depends on the terms of the arrangement. If an institution guarantees an evidential opportunity, unavoidable fire may leave it owing a substitute just as wrongful destruction does. If it promises reasonable care and fairly allocates residual accident risk, the two cases differ. The draft partly recognizes this, but should explain that fault establishes responsibility for a loss outside the accepted allocation of risk; it is not a general reason why identical missing evidence has different informational significance.

Finally, “adequate substitute” and “independent justification for closure” do substantial work. They should remain flexible, but not so flexible that every outcome satisfies the principle after being given another name. Supply one determinate application and one limit. For example, a token hearing followed by unchanged reliance on the destroyed record is inadequate; a reasoned decision under an independently justified, accessible procedure can be final even though the institution must still fund compensation for the distinct evidential loss. Whether the draft accepts that latter possibility would clarify its actual commitment.

## 7. Source accuracy, significance and revision priorities

The checked attributions are generally careful. Rosen's Ed discussion supports the restricted claim about derivative culpability, and the draft correctly identifies the 1999 notes rather than substituting a later publication. [Rosen, §III](https://www.princeton.edu/~grosen/ODFF/ODFF.html). Harman's abstract and opening support the stated disagreement about moral ignorance; I did not independently re-read her full argument. [Harman, pp. 443–444](https://www.princeton.edu/~eharman/DoesMoralIgnoranceExculpate.pdf).

Reiff explicitly distinguishes institutional and successor duties from personal guilt, including indirect financial effects on present people. The draft does not pretend that his broader asset-based theory is its own premise. [Reiff, §§2.4–2.5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/josp.12555). Boult's discussion supports attention to epistemic relations and the non-neutrality of resources; it does not independently establish the museum's required procedure. The manuscript-specific application must be argued here. His publication metadata are correct. [Boult, §3 and publication details](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02410-z). Friedman's repository introduction supports the modest use made of it, and the essay discloses its limited reading. [Repository text](https://repository.law.umich.edu/facarticles/167/).

The question is significant: institutions can wrongly use evidential requirements even when their present officers reason conscientiously, and a remedy can harm other claimants if its basis is misdescribed. The essay is clear enough to make those concerns visible. Its current contribution is nevertheless largely a conditional constraint and an agenda for selecting remedies. It should not claim a general decision theory, a newly discovered distinction between belief and action, or a demonstrated right to displace institutional control.

The highest-value revisions are:

1. Engage the close relational precedents and state the remaining multi-claimant problem precisely.
2. Replace the weak contradiction target with a serious account of practical presumptions; test unequal evidential impairment.
3. Distinguish an inadequate reason for closure from absence of authority, and defend the additional condition for external review or transferred control.
4. Separate historical ownership, remedial duties and present title; adjust the language about defeating another claimant accordingly.
5. Give the dependence condition and the adequacy/closure exceptions enough content to rule a concrete response in and another out.

A narrower essay that establishes these distinctions and defends one constrained institutional consequence would be stronger than retaining the present breadth. I recommend substantive revision, preserving the promising core and the unresolved disagreements rather than claiming the argument settled.
