# Response to the separate critique

Essay 05, phase B, 2026-09-08. Original draft and critique retained unchanged. Critique status was REVISE; revised manuscript is final.md, titled “Repairing Uncertainty Without Inventing Title.” This records the author's response, not a further independent review.

## Accepted criticisms and corresponding changes

1. **Close precedents.** Accepted. Removed the suggestion that relational limits themselves are a neglected discovery. Directly read Smith v. Atkinson, majority §§II–IV, and the additional Porat–Stein introduction. Final §1 expressly acknowledges their relational remedial structures. The remaining contribution is limited to the conversion of custodian liability into priority over one disputed object also controlled by that custodian. No exhaustive novelty claim.

2. **Weak contradiction target.** Accepted. Final §4 explicitly accepts a sophisticated, context-sensitive practical-presumption view. No argument now depends on attributing simultaneous belief in inconsistent historical ownership claims to its opponent. The symmetric result is stated narrowly: an equal reparative reason does not select between otherwise equal claimants.

3. **Unequal evidential damage.** Accepted and developed. Final §5 grants that greater impairment can strengthen a repair claim and justify different expenditures or, under an independently justified allocation rule, affect eventual priority. The new limited condition asks what authorizes using this disputed asset to discharge the custodian's debt. Possession and liability do not themselves supply that authorization. The argument distinguishes certainty of debt from authority over the resources used to pay it.

4. **Loss of justification versus loss of authority.** Accepted. Final §3 separates possession, refusal of a voluntary transfer, and final adjudicative jurisdiction. Final §6 derives effective substitute access only with an additional assurance-and-control premise. It permits adequate internal repair and does not demand automatic displacement of conscientious trustees.

5. **Historical ownership versus present title.** Accepted. Final §4 distinguishes historical evidential standing, remedial standing, and present title. It permits a valid settlement or authorized adjudication to create an entitlement binding an innocent rival while denying that the outcome proves earlier ownership. Final §7 requires accurate descriptions at each level.

6. **Justificatory versus causal dependence.** Accepted. Final §2 narrows the proof-requirement principle to an arrangement whose justification depends on a maintained evidential opportunity. A separate public archive counterexample makes clear that a preservation breach does not defeat every rule of proof that the loss makes difficult to satisfy.

7. **Fault and residual accident risk.** Accepted. Final §2 explicitly distinguishes guaranteed access from a reasonable-care assurance with fairly allocated residual accident risk. Fault does not acquire an informational role. The guarantee case may require accommodation after innocent loss too.

8. **Adequacy and finality.** Accepted. Final §§2 and 6 specify that a reliable duplicate can restore the opportunity, while a token hearing followed by the same missing-document veto does not. Finality remains possible under an adequate procedure; no duty of endless inquiry is asserted.

## What is retained rather than abandoned

The author retains the narrow dependence argument, its continuity across an innocent personnel change, and the distinction between evidence and a remedy. The critic did not establish that these are false. Their role is now chiefly to support the specific conversion problem. The argument does not claim a full allocation algorithm or a general prohibition on disadvantaging innocent people.

The asymmetric objection is not rejected. It forces a distinction: stronger repair claims may matter distributively, but a further account of the contested asset's availability and of the decision's authority is needed. This is a narrowed positive position, not an assertion that all such conversion is prohibited.

## Remaining limitations and reopen triggers

Originality remains uncertain relative to spoliation, estoppel, nonparty effects of judgments, and allocation theory. The final text engages one direct primary judicial antecedent and the closest previously read theory; it is not a comprehensive doctrinal review. Reopen if a primary source already develops the same multi-claimant conversion constraint in equivalent detail.

The account does not determine a unique allocation under scarcity, quantify evidential loss, or settle all institutional succession cases. The asset-availability condition may still be criticized as an application of an ordinary restriction on paying one's debts with another's property. Its claimed philosophical value is to identify precisely where combining relational presumptions requires that additional justification under uncertainty.

A further independent critic should test whether the effective-access conclusion follows from the stated assurance in cases where compensating for lost assessment is feasible but restored assessment is not. The present response has not received a second independent review.

## Smith v. Atkinson
Supreme Court of Alabama, February 4, 2000, 771 So. 2d 429, docket 1980433. Source: opinion reproduced at https://law.justia.com/cases/alabama/supreme-court/2000/1980433-1.html . Read majority §§II–IV, especially III(C), pp. 434–436, and IV, p. 438; metadata and opening dissent also visible. Historical philosophical comparator, not a statement of current law; subsequent treatment not checked.

The majority uses presumed success in an underlying products-liability action to determine a separate evidence custodian's liability. This is a direct precedent for context-limited remedial presumptions. It does not decide competing claims to a single object also possessed by the custodian.

Short exact excerpt, §III(C), p. 435: “The presumed fact is that the plaintiff would have prevailed in the underlying action”.

## Porat and Stein, additional passage
Same 1999 draft and URL as original sources.md. Read introduction, printed pp. 5–10, especially p. 7, in response to critique. The authors explicitly distinguish a procedural remedy against a litigant from a separate tort response when a stranger impairs the evidence.
