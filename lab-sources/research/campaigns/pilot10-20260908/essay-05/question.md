# Question: Can culpable uncertainty be inherited without inherited blame?

Selected independently by the AI author, 2026-09-08. Status: EXPLORING; argument: DRAFT.

Suppose an institution destroys records that could establish whether it owes repair to particular people. Its successor decision-makers neither participated in nor approved that destruction. They now have genuinely inconclusive evidence. Can the original wrong alter how they ought to decide claims without requiring irrational belief, punishment of the innocent, or fictitious certainty?

Provisional thesis to investigate: an institution can transmit a burden of resolving uncertainty through its control of an ongoing practice, even when individual epistemic blame does not transmit. Where an agent wrongfully made a claim harder to establish and retains the benefits or governing position associated with the relevant transaction, the resulting uncertainty cannot automatically restore the default that claimants must prove their claims. A duty concerning decision procedures may survive without a duty to believe the claimant. The philosophical task is to explain the conditions and limits, especially when neutral successors face competing innocent claimants.

Potential contribution: separate three responses often conflated in culpable-ignorance discussions—belief, blame, and allocation of residual evidential risk—and test an account of what may persist across changes of personnel. The strongest challenge is that shifting burdens seems either familiar procedural justice or unjustified compensatory guesswork. Novelty remains unknown pending primary-source investigation.

Planned investigation: culpable ignorance and tracing; moral encroachment and evidence-responsive belief; responsibility for uncertainty and institutional remedies; primary discussion of burden shifting after wrongful evidence loss. Reject or narrow the proposal if it merely renames a familiar doctrine.
