## What the literature changes

The broad thought that manipulated approval cannot simply legitimate its cause is familiar. It is explicitly within Chwang's discussion, and the autonomy literature provides several nearby analyses. The essay must not present that broad thought as a discovery. Equally, later endorsement's causal dependence on a transformation is not, by itself, an adequate objection. The sources rule out using causal independence or an unchanged authentic self as an unexplained requirement.

The working contribution is now a narrower combination: distinguish a person's authority to determine what happens to her next from an intervener's claim that her later approval discharges a complaint about the earlier intervention. Argue for an impediment to the second inference when the intervention actively disables assessment of that very complaint. Show that this impediment neither entails preferring the person's former values nor licenses an unwanted restorative intervention. The target is a particular justificatory inference, not a global verdict that the transformed person lacks autonomy.

The contrast is made concrete by separating (a) approving present characteristics, (b) refusing further alteration, and (c) releasing a claim against their producer. An initial version of the question spoke of an effective possibility of negative ratification. That formulation will be narrowed in drafting: a merely counterfactual blocker that never operates raises a separate problem. The defended core will concern actual interference with the evaluation on which a purported waiver rests. The theoretical mechanism is a selective interruption of reasons, not an assumption that strong commitment or deterministic causation destroys agency.

## Intended argument and discriminatory cases

The central comparison will hold benefits and new preferences fixed while varying whether the subject's assessment of an outstanding complaint was protected from, or disabled by, the original intervention. A two-stage case will prevent the intervention's overall benefits from obscuring a separately targeted change to the capacity to object. The contemplated defence of the narrow constraint is that a claim's protection cannot be discharged by the claimant's inability to exercise it, when the putative debtor created that inability and appeals to its product as a waiver.

A second distinction will test the moral consequences: impaired ability to waive a complaint does not remove the person's bodily protections or make a second intervention permissible. Refusal to reverse the first procedure may therefore remain decisive even while a purported release of responsibility fails. Independent emergency justification, valid prior permission, later recovery of the relevant capacity, and ordinary education are scope conditions or potential exceptions to discuss rather than dismiss.

## Strongest nearby objections to address

- All preferences have formative causes; the proposal must identify an actual impediment to assessment, not disapprove a genealogy or particular values.
- Frankfurt-style cases challenge a blanket requirement that consent have alternative possibilities. The core thesis will not assert that requirement.
- Distinguishing retention from exculpation may be a familiar distinction between desires and consent. The argument must address explicit, informed words of waiver as well as mere satisfaction.
- A defect restricted to one normative use may still threaten present consent. The account must explain the asymmetry through the different claims being exercised, not grant blanket competency by stipulation.
- A debtor's conduct cannot invalidate an otherwise autonomous release merely because the release is convenient for the debtor. It is the active disabling of assessment, not benefit, influence, intent or causal dependence alone, that matters.
