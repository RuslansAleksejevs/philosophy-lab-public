# Keeping the Change, Keeping the Claim

A person can want to keep the benefits of something another person had no right to do. The harder case arises when those benefits include changes to her own preferences. If she now welcomes her changed life, must respecting her mean accepting that its producer has been released from responsibility? If her welcome was manipulated, must respecting her mean restoring the person she used to be?

Both inferences can fail. But simply observing that her assessment was manipulated does not tell us which normative powers she retains. Someone who cannot evaluate the merits of a claim may nevertheless competently release it under a general policy of generosity. And someone unable to release a claim does not thereby lose protection against another intervention.

I argue that the relevant distinction is between assessing a claim's merits and controlling its settlement. Manipulation defeats a purported release when it displaces the agency through which the holder settles that claim, not merely when it affects her evaluation of the underlying wrong. This yields a limited account of what must survive for release to be effective. It also explains how preserving a transformed life can be compatible with preserving an undischarged claim, without ranking whole earlier and later personalities.

## 1. A determinate claim after a transformation

Consider an imagined procedure, **Opening**, which enables Mara to enjoy music in ways previously unavailable to her. She develops a demanding musical practice and valuable friendships. She understands how these interests arose, reflects intelligently on them, and wants them to continue. Assume that Opening was added to a procedure she authorized, without the additional permission it required. The clinic also charged an additional £1,000. Under the agreed terms, an unauthorized addition carries an obligation to refund that fee. The clinic acknowledges that it owes Mara £1,000 unless she releases this obligation. Keeping the beneficial effects does not, under those terms, forfeit her refund.

Fixing this claim matters. The question is whether the repayment obligation has been extinguished by Mara's response. It is not whether she has forgiven the clinic, whether it remains blameworthy, or whether her present satisfaction changes the original act's permissibility. Those questions can have different answers. Nor need we determine the entire compensation owed for an unauthorized intervention. One definite, ordinarily waivable claim is enough.

Alongside Opening, the clinic caused **Closing**, a durable psychological alteration. Closing is an enduring effect of a completed procedure; the clinic is not administering continuing doses or operating a remote switch. It selectively interferes with Mara's thinking about the clinic. Two forms of Closing will be important.

In **Merits Block**, Mara cannot assess whether the clinic's explanation excuses its conduct or diminishes the seriousness of the wrong. Relevant considerations are interrupted before she can weigh them. Nevertheless, she understands that the clinic owes her £1,000, that retaining the claim does not require reversing Opening, and that releasing it extinguishes the obligation. Her ability to decide what to do with an acknowledged claim remains intact.

In **Settlement Block**, the interference goes further. Mara initially settles on reserving the claim for later consideration. When she tries to apply that policy, Closing replaces the intention to reserve it with an intention to release it. The mechanism operates in this actual sequence. Her understanding of the words remains, but her judgment or policy about disposing of this claim cannot govern the resulting declaration. No independently authorized delegation supplies another route to settlement. She sincerely announces that the clinic owes her nothing.

These are philosophical constructions, not descriptions of available technology. They distinguish an impediment to evaluating a wrong from an impediment to deciding what to do with a right. The distinction would be hidden by treating every unwelcome influence as equally destructive of agency.

Now suppose Mara says, “Leave me as I am; keep the money.” The first clause concerns another proposed intervention upon her. The second purports to settle the repayment obligation. It is tempting either to accept both as the voice of the present Mara or to discount both as the voice of a manipulated Mara. That treats authority as a property bestowed on a whole personality. The better approach is to ask which agency and which protections each clause involves.

## 2. What releasing a claim requires

Appreciating an outcome does not amount to releasing its producer. I can value an exhibition's effect on my career while objecting that a friend exhibited my unfinished painting without permission. I can also release a claim despite disliking the outcome, because reconciliation matters more to me. Even informed appreciation and release come apart.

Chwang's defence of subsequent consent already distinguishes consent from gratitude and connects it with relinquishing demands for compensation. His discussion of brainwashing also recognizes that defective causal formation can undermine particular instances of subsequent consent without discrediting the whole category. My concern is therefore not to discover that distinction, but to identify the relevant impediment when a release and a transformed person's present choices come apart. [Chwang 2009, §§3.1–3.2, 5.2.](https://spot.colorado.edu/~chwang/chwang-eric-a-defense-of.pdf)

A release need not follow an adjudication. A creditor may say, “Perhaps your excuses are poor, and perhaps I have a stronger case than I realize. I am giving up the claim anyway.” Requiring her to understand all its merits would make a power intended for her use conditional on an inquiry she has reason to avoid. It would particularly obstruct generosity toward undeserving recipients.

What is necessary is more modest. The holder must adequately understand the disposition she is making: for example, that a claim may still be outstanding and that she is relinquishing it, rather than merely reporting that she feels content or declining to demand payment today. And this disposition must proceed through a practical basis that actually governs her settlement: her deliberation, her policy about disposing of claims, or a delegation she adequately understands and authorizes. Call this *settlement control*.

These conditions are necessary, not a complete sufficient account of release. Coercion, deception, special duties, and the scope of the right may create further difficulties. Nor does “her policy” mean a policy she invented unaided or has reflected upon exhaustively. Ordinary commitments acquired through family life, education, or recent transformation can govern an action. The relevant failure occurs when such a practical basis is displaced in settling this claim, rather than functioning as the basis on which she settles it.

The distinction has a familiar analogue. Someone may be unable to calculate what a complicated contract is worth while remaining able to appoint an adviser who will decide whether to relinquish it. Conversely, someone who can explain every provision might have her decision overridden and the resulting signature produced without her control. Intellectual grasp of a transaction's merits and agency in disposing of it are different requirements.

Why connect release to settlement control? A releasable claim gives its holder some authority over whether the obligation continues. The holder need not always exercise that authority wisely, but a debtor cannot substitute its judgment that release would be beneficial. Otherwise the right would reserve no relevant decision to its holder. Release must consequently do more than resemble the response she might have produced: it must involve her disposition of the claim through a route competent to settle it.

This argument does not define all manipulation as absence of control. It explains why a specific loss of control matters to a specific normative effect. Destroying a creditor's records may impair her capacity to prove a debt without extinguishing it. Forcing her hand to sign may produce a conventional release without exercising her power. Settlement Block interferes internally rather than mechanically, but the reserved decision is likewise displaced. The fact that the resulting words express a sincere current intention is insufficient when the process has actually replaced her governing decision about that very disposition.

## 3. The objection that improves the criterion

Merits Block reveals why an indiscriminate appeal to interference would be too strong. Suppose Mara has independently adopted a general policy of releasing personal claims to give others an undeserved fresh start. She understands its scope and chooses to apply it to the clinic. Closing still prevents her from assessing the clinic's excuses. Yet those excuses are irrelevant to the basis on which she chooses release. Her declaration can exercise settlement control even though some relevant first-order reasoning remains impaired.

The release is not made effective by the clinic's entitlement to induce Merits Block; it has no such entitlement. It is made possible by Mara's authority to settle a claim without adjudicating its merits. The impairment and an adequate exercise of authority can coexist. The same holds if Mara knowingly entrusts settlement to an independent representative and retains sufficient control over that delegation. Her inability to decide the underlying dispute need not deprive her of the ability to choose how it should be decided.

These possibilities qualify the objection to manufactured approval. Actual interruption of complaint-related thinking is not enough to defeat every release given while the interruption occurs. Neither is a causal contribution to Mara's overall psychological state. We must identify the route through which this release is exercised and determine whether interference displaced its governance of the disposition.

Settlement Block supplies the contrasting case. Mara's reservation policy is not merely one consideration outweighed by generosity. The mechanism replaces its application with release before that policy can determine what she does. There is no separate decision to waive irrespective of merits or to delegate settlement. The resulting declaration therefore cannot, by her authority alone, fully discharge the repayment obligation. The conclusion depends on the failure of settlement control, not on the mere presence of psychological alteration.

A critic may ask whether the contrast simply builds the verdict into the case. A thought experiment inevitably specifies the features an account deems relevant. What would be objectionable is to describe Closing merely as “invalidating consent” and infer invalidity. The distinction here instead concerns two independently intelligible activities: judging whether one's complaint is justified and directing the disposition of a claim whose existence one accepts. Generous release and delegation show that the first can fail while the second survives. The replacement of an actual reservation policy explains why the second fails in the severe case. These features constrain the verdict rather than merely restating it.

There will be harder cases. Perhaps an independent policy and a manipulative process both contribute to the final declaration. The fact that Mara would have released the claim anyway does not establish that she exercised settlement control in doing so. Conversely, a manipulative influence need not defeat control simply because it made release more likely. A full account of mixed and overdetermined agency would require more than these cases provide. The definite result concerns actual displacement of the governing route, with no adequate alternative route exercising the power.

The account also does not require metaphysically open alternatives at the instant of release. Pettigrew accepts a Frankfurt-style case in which preferences form through free agency although coercion would have produced them otherwise; his assessment concerns the actual formation. That distinction supports separating an operative obstruction from a device that never intervenes. [Pettigrew 2026, §4.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phpr.70131)

If a hidden device would have displaced Mara's decision but remained inactive because she competently chose release, the present argument does not show that her release fails. The device's presence may be wrongful on other grounds. There is also an evidential problem: approval alone cannot show whether it resulted from adequate settlement control or from an operative override. But uncertainty about a valid release and the invalidity of an overridden release are different problems.

## 4. Whose interference matters?

The clinic caused Closing in the original case, and its attempt to benefit from Closing makes the problem especially vivid. But that history is not essential to the defect in release. Imagine that an unrelated third party caused precisely the same Settlement Block. Or suppose it arose as an unforeseeable side effect for which the clinic was not blameworthy. If the same displacement occurs, with the same absence of another adequate route, Mara's declaration still fails to exercise the power needed to discharge the claim.

The clinic's own interference can add a wrong and strengthen its duties of repair. It does not supply the basic explanation of the declaration's defect. That explanation concerns Mara's exercise of authority now. Correspondingly, a clinic that wrongfully produced Merits Block can still receive an effective release if Mara exercises unimpaired settlement control through a generous policy. Wrongdoers can benefit from their victims' competent magnanimity. Preventing every such benefit would constrain the victim's authority in order to punish the recipient.

This separates two questions that can otherwise be confused: whether the claimant has effectively exercised her power, and whether a recipient is entitled to rely on the available evidence that she has. An innocent clinic may reasonably believe it received a release when an unknown third party actually displaced Mara's control. Its ignorance can matter to blame or reliance, without manufacturing the missing exercise. Conversely, a guilty clinic may receive an effective release without its earlier conduct thereby becoming justified.

Causal dependence on change is also insufficient. Varelius argues that self-validating neuroenhancement can be autonomous and compares it with ordinary beneficial changes that shape our assessments of them. His conclusion preserves the possibility that particular interventions undermine autonomy. The relevant question here is therefore not whether Opening helped create Mara's evaluative capacities, but whether those capacities govern settlement or are displaced. [Varelius 2020, directness discussion and Conclusion.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11019-019-09905-7)

A new forgiving disposition can govern an effective release. The absence of an earlier disposition favouring release is no objection. Equally, there need not have been an earlier refusal for a present claim to survive. The clinic's outstanding obligation and the present exercise of settlement control matter; an untouched former self need not preside over the transaction.

## 5. The precise authority of a present refusal

What follows if Settlement Block defeats Mara's release? Consider first the version in which her judgment about retaining Opening is intact. She knows the advantages and burdens of further alteration and competently refuses it. Her refusal can remain authoritative while her attempted release fails. The objects of the decisions and the capacities exercised differ. That is not a novel general theory of competence; its importance is that it blocks the demand to credit or discredit the transformed person as a whole.

Now extend Closing so that it also disables her decision about removing Closing itself. Here we cannot infer an authoritative refusal merely from the words “Leave me alone.” But neither can we infer permission to intervene from the defect in her decision. Another intervention needs additional justification. It must take account of her present interests, bodily protections, the risks involved, and any relevant authority to act on her behalf. The defect in release supplies none of these automatically.

These are different results. The first preserves a competent present exercise of authority alongside a defective one. The second preserves protections when a relevant power cannot be exercised competently. Confusing them would exaggerate what the argument establishes. A damaged capacity does not itself yield a valid veto; it also does not dissolve the moral constraints upon those who would act.

The structure is visible outside the technology case. Suppose someone wrongfully uses a child's money to obtain an instrument for her. The child enjoys it but cannot competently release the resulting financial claim. Her cheerful response need not settle the obligation. Yet it does not follow that the instrument should be taken away. Its role in her present life remains a consideration in deciding how to repair the wrong. Preserving a benefit and preserving a claim are compatible without pretending that the child has exercised a financial power she lacks.

The analogy concerns that compatibility, not a general comparison between a transformed adult and a child. Mara may have gained sophistication and independence through Opening. Davis's account of subsequent consent makes room for later authority when greater competence enables comparisons unavailable earlier. Gains in competence can matter without settling whether control over one particular disposition remains impaired. [Davis 2004, §5, pp. 282–285.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7587650_Precedent_Autonomy_and_Subsequent_Consent)

Nor does denying a release make Mara's response normatively inert. Her wish to avoid hearings, repeated apologies, or contact with the clinic may remain well understood and important. She need not identify as a victim or cultivate resentment. An obligation can remain undischarged while its holder does not presently wish to pursue it. That difference may affect when and how repayment should be offered, even though it does not by itself extinguish the obligation.

Bajaj and Tomlin argue, in the context of coercion, that consent can weaken duties without fully removing them, and that permissibility should be distinguished from complete validity. My argument neither establishes their extension to manipulation nor requires an all-or-nothing theory of normative significance. It targets the clinic's claim to a full release from the specified obligation. [Bajaj and Tomlin 2024, §§II, VI.1.](https://academic.oup.com/pq/article/74/3/709/7287044)

## 6. Authorization and repair

Voluntary self-transformation requires three further distinctions. Permission to undergo a psychological change, advance release of a particular claim, and delegation of settlement authority are different acts. Mara might permit a reduction of her inclination to revisit grievances while expressly retaining the right to repayment. Just as permission for sedation does not authorize every declaration made while sedated, permission for Closing does not automatically make later engineered releases effective.

If Mara already competently released the £1,000 claim, it is no longer outstanding, and the later declaration is unnecessary. If she competently delegated its settlement, an authorized representative may supply the relevant disposition. But if she only permitted Closing, we must still determine whether an outstanding claim was subsequently released through adequate settlement control. The permissibility of producing an impairment does not entail the efficacy of every act performed under it.

There may be limits to which powers one can surrender or delegate. Nothing here resolves those limits. The narrower point is about scope: authorization of change is not automatically authorization of settlement. Nor can later engineered endorsement establish that prior permission covered the mechanism whose permission is disputed.

Repair need not restore Mara's former preferences. Pugh emphasizes that a person can take ownership of a manipulated desire while retaining objections to its causal history. This makes room for preserving valued interests while restoring a capacity needed to decide a different question. [Pugh 2020, chapter 3, §2.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556856/)

Suppose an adjustment can restore settlement control without undoing Opening. If appropriately authorized and proportionate, it may be an attractive remedy: Mara keeps her musical life and gains a route to determine whether to retain, release, or delegate the claim. If she then releases it, the continued influence of Opening does not disqualify her. Restoration of agency need not be restoration of a former identity.

But the persisting repayment claim alone does not establish a duty or permission to perform that adjustment. Repayment itself may be available without any further alteration; a risky corrective procedure may be disproportionate. What other remedies are owed depends on the other wrongs and circumstances. Where no permissible route to restored control exists, the inability to obtain a release does not authorize manufacturing the conditions for one.

The completed-procedure stipulation matters here. If the clinic instead maintains Closing through ongoing unauthorized suppression, stopping that suppression may be part of ending a present wrong. It would be misleading to describe every cessation as a fresh intrusion needing authorization in the same way as another operation. Even then, the manner of stopping may involve risks that require attention. The need to distinguish cessation from a new corrective act prevents the language of non-interference from protecting continuing interference.

Finally, an emergency can independently justify intervention. Preserving bodily protections does not rule out every action without present consent. The point is that its justification must be supplied: it cannot be inferred from defective release, and it cannot be replaced by approval produced through the defect.

## 7. What the distinctions add

The claim that manufactured approval is suspect is familiar. So is the distinction between appreciating a result and approving its origin. The contribution here is to identify an overly broad inference within that familiar diagnosis. The inability to evaluate a claim's merits need not prevent its holder from competently disposing of it. Unconditional release and authorized delegation establish that point. What matters to full discharge is control over the disposition, not an intact capacity to adjudicate every underlying consideration.

This is compatible with broader frameworks rather than a purported refutation of them. Enoch's contrastive account makes consent's normative significance depend on the relevant contrast and antecedent entitlements. Applied here, it helps explain why retaining Opening rather than reversing it does not settle whether the clinic keeps the fee rather than repays it. My further question concerns the exercise of agency once the latter transaction is specified. That application is an inference, not a claim that Enoch discusses Closing. [Enoch 2024, §3.2, pp. 7–8.](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/phimp/article/3169/galley/3023/download/)

The resulting account is deliberately limited. It does not supply a general measure of autonomy, a clinical test for manipulation, or a complete account of mixed agency. It provides a distinction with consequences on both sides: interference with adjudication need not defeat release, while interference that actually displaces settlement control cannot secure full discharge merely through sincere assent. Failure of discharge then leaves the question of a new intervention to be justified separately.

We should therefore neither enthrone the former person nor give the producer of a transformed life authority over its moral interpretation. The present person may have reason to keep the change, authority to settle some matters, and continuing protections in matters she cannot settle. Where an appropriately authorized repair can restore settlement control while preserving what she values, it offers a way to return the disputed decision to her without requiring her to become someone else.
