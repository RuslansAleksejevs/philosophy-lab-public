# When Restrictions Sustain Their Own Reasons

## 1. The next accurate verdict

A licensing authority suspends Mara from operating a city's movable bridges. A sound assessment has revealed that she cannot reliably carry out a newly required emergency procedure. The requirement is appropriate, the assessment is accurate, and immediate suspension is justified. At that moment, no safe training facility is available.

Several weeks later, supervised practice becomes possible. The authority, which controls access to the relevant equipment, nevertheless admits only currently certified operators. Mara cannot regain proficiency through ordinary work because she is suspended, and she cannot obtain supervised practice because she is uncertified. At each review she fails a fair assessment. Every verdict correctly records her present inability. The authority explains its decisions clearly and would immediately reinstate anyone who passed. It also knows that supervised practice would give Mara a reasonable chance of passing without exposing the public to the risks that justified suspension.

Something has gone wrong, but identifying a false belief will not locate it. Mara really is unready. Nor need her complaint be that the original suspension was unjust. The authority's mistake is to treat the question whether she may operate a bridge today as settling whether it must help make a different answer possible tomorrow.

This essay defends a duty of *reopening*. When an institution maintains a conditional restriction, substantially obstructs the ordinary routes to meeting its release condition, and controls a proportionate means of reopening such a route, it owes the restricted person that opportunity. It may maintain the restriction while providing the opportunity. What it cannot do is treat the condition the opportunity would address as, by itself, a sufficient reason for withholding it.

The claim concerns the administration of restrictive power, not an unlimited entitlement to acquire any qualification. Its importance lies in identifying a way that accurate, transparent, regularly reviewed decisions can still entrench an unjustified relationship. An institution can respond impeccably to the evidence presented at its door while preventing the development of the evidence that could release someone from its control.

The bridge case is a stipulated example. Its purpose is to isolate a normative structure, not to describe a particular licensing system. The initial restriction, relevant safety standard and feasibility of supervised practice are granted so that neither prejudice nor a dispute about engineering does the argumentative work.

## 2. What confirmation can establish

The danger of judgments producing their own apparent vindication is familiar. Merton's account of self-fulfilling prophecy already explains how responses to a social judgment can produce confirming behavior, including through institutional exclusion (1948, pp. 195–197). King and Mertens explain how fulfilled predictions can conceal mistakes and inhibit accountability; their discussion explicitly includes cases in which participants recognize reflexivity (2023, §§3.3–3.5). De Bruin identifies credibility deficits that become accurate through the effects of treating people as less credible (2025, abstract). These are established starting points. [Merton](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4609267), [King and Mertens](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10677-022-10359-9), [de Bruin](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2025.10052).

There are, however, three different questions to which a subsequent observation might be relevant.

First, does the disqualifying condition obtain now? If an institution's treatment has actually diminished a person's skill, a reliable test can establish that the skill is diminished. Causal involvement does not make the measurement worthless. Treating all institutionally influenced evidence as contaminated would also discredit the evidence by which we recognize successful teaching.

Second, was the original judgment warranted when it was made? Later evidence cannot alter which reasons were then available. An induced failure is especially poor vindication of a prediction about what someone would have done without the intervention. But not every judgment makes that counterfactual claim. Mara's original assessment concerned her actual readiness, and its warrant is stipulated to be sound.

Third, is the institution entitled to continue administering the restriction in its present form? Present incapacity is relevant to this question, but it does not settle it. A restriction is a course of conduct with consequences for the person's future abilities and opportunities. Whether it is justified depends partly on what other courses remain available to the institution.

The third question is easily compressed into the first. At a review, the authority asks whether Mara satisfies the standard. Since she does not, it renews the restriction. If every review uses that procedure, the authority may see an indefinitely renewable permission. Yet the very circumstances that make renewal necessary may be circumstances it has a duty to change.

This is not an objection to valid inference. From Mara's present inability and an appropriate safety rule, immediate suspension may follow. The failure is practical: that conclusion answers only one of the decisions the institution faces. It does not answer whether to open the simulator, arrange equivalent instruction, or amend a rule that blocks safe preparation. Calling each renewal correct leaves these additional decisions unevaluated.

Nor must this pattern start with a falsehood that becomes true. It can start with a true condition that a justified intervention helps keep true. The distinction matters because an account framed exclusively around exposing an original error invites the authority to reply that no such error occurred. Reopening can be owed even when that reply is correct.

## 3. From an open hearing to an open route

Consider two variations. In the first, Mara retains the required competence, but suspended operators are denied access to the only recognized assessment. Restoring access would allow existing competence to become institutionally visible. In the second, suspension has prevented the practice through which she could acquire or recover the competence. A new assessment, however carefully administered, merely registers its continuing absence.

The first problem concerns the production of evidence of an existing qualification. The second concerns the production of the qualification itself. A system can remedy the first while leaving the second intact. It can provide unlimited hearings and flawless examinations while arranging that each examination must end badly.

The distinction sharpens rather than displaces existing defenses of recourse. Venkatasubramanian and Alfano ground recourse in extended agency and trust; they distinguish correcting records from changing attributes, consider representatives and burdens, and acknowledge structural reform (2020, §§1, 4–5). Vredenburgh grounds a right to explanation in informed self-advocacy, while explicitly distinguishing informational from material requirements (2022, §§3–4; accepted manuscript p. 8, n. 19). The present proposal specifies one source of a material obligation and who bears it. [Venkatasubramanian and Alfano](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372876), [Vredenburgh](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopp.12262).

The proposal is not that every person receiving an adverse decision must be made able to reverse it. Some requirements cannot reasonably be met; some exclusions track permanent limitations; some requested opportunities would put others at unacceptable risk. Rather, a restricted person has a distinctive complaint where the body invoking a release criterion also controls an avoidable obstacle to meeting it, and that obstacle belongs to the restrictive arrangement itself.

This connection distinguishes Mara from someone who simply asks a licensing authority to finance an entirely new career. Mara is already subject to an ongoing intervention. The authority is not merely encountering a difficulty in her circumstances; it is maintaining a rule that blocks an otherwise available response to the very difficulty on which its continuing intervention depends.

Reopening therefore has two objects. Sometimes it requires a way to demonstrate a capacity. Sometimes it requires a way to develop or recover it. In neither case does it require a favorable verdict. A route can be fair and effective even though the person fails after taking it. Its effectiveness consists in offering a serious prospect of satisfying the relevant criterion through reasonable participation, not in guaranteeing that the criterion will be satisfied.

## 4. Why the restricting institution owes the route

The duty rests on three premises.

The first is that an institution exercising conditional restrictive authority must justify the avoidable burdens of the arrangement it maintains. A permission to restrict for a protective purpose is bounded by that purpose and by competing interests. It is not a general permission to let every consequence of restriction fall wherever it happens to fall. This is why the necessity of preventing unsupervised bridge operation does not, without further argument, justify denying safe supervised practice.

The second is that the relevant comparison includes feasible courses of action over time. It cannot be limited to immediate release and unchanged restriction. In Mara's case, immediate release is dangerous. Continued suspension with supervised practice can nevertheless be safer for the public and less burdensome to her than indefinite suspension without practice. The more defensible alternative need not eliminate today's restriction. It may instead make its continued necessity less likely.

The third is that an institution cannot discharge the burden of justifying its choice among those courses merely by pointing to a condition that distinguishes none of them. Mara's present lack of proficiency is common to suspension with practice and suspension without practice. It explains why both options exclude immediate unsupervised work. It does not explain choosing the latter option. A reason for withholding practice must bear on the prospect, cost, safety or fairness of providing practice, or on another relevant consideration distinguishing the options.

Together these premises yield a limited but demanding conclusion. Where a reasonable reopening route exists, and there is no adequate countervailing reason, the restricting institution must provide or enable it. A declaration that its present assessment is accurate does not discharge this duty.

The first premise carries substantive moral weight. Why accept it? Consider what denying it would authorize. An institution could initially restrict someone for a limited purpose, foreseeably eliminate the ordinary means of escaping the restriction, and then treat the resulting dependence as sufficient warrant for doing nothing. The initial justification would expand into authority to control whether its own terminating condition could ever be met. That additional power has not been justified by the reason that licensed the initial restriction.

The person can reasonably accept the public's claim to protection while objecting to this expansion. Mara need not claim that her interests outweigh safety. She can demand an explanation of why a safe opportunity, within the authority's control and proportionate to the interests involved, is withheld. The public's interest in safe bridges answers why she cannot presently operate one. It supplies no answer to the distinct demand.

This argument also explains why the duty is directed at the restricting institution. Its practical arrangement creates or sustains the relevant blockage, and its authority controls whether that blockage remains. Assigning the duty to an unrelated benefactor would allow the institution to externalize the cost of administering its own power. Other bodies may assist, and the authority may discharge its duty by arranging their assistance. But the restricted person should not have to depend on their voluntary generosity while the authority treats an avoidable consequence of its regime as her private problem.

No inference from mere causation to unlimited compensation is involved. An institution that closes an unsafe bridge may cause a shop to lose customers, without thereby owing restoration of every lost sale. That case lacks the crucial relation: the authority is not repeatedly imposing a conditional restriction on the shop while obstructing the route to satisfying its own release criterion. Causal contribution matters here because of the particular continuing power it accompanies.

We can state the resulting principle precisely enough to guide judgment:

*An institution maintaining a significant, conditional restriction owes a reasonable opportunity to meet its legitimate release criterion when the restrictive arrangement substantially obstructs ordinary routes to meeting or demonstrating that criterion, the institution controls a practicable means of reopening such a route, and providing it would not impose disproportionate costs or defeat the legitimate protective purpose.*

The duty is to enable a route, or a comparably effective alternative, and subsequently to respond to what happens along it. A person may decline to participate. The institution must not force improvement on her merely because it would make its administration easier.

## 5. The right comparison

A natural objection says that the principle covertly privileges the person Mara would have been without institutional interference. But no such person needs to be identified. Her abilities before suspension were themselves formed through training, workplace arrangements and licensing practices. There is no reason to regard whatever survives an imagined removal of those influences as her authentic capacity.

The relevant comparison is prospective and practical. Starting from her actual situation, what routes can now reasonably be made available? Reopening asks the authority to compare continued obstruction with a feasible alteration, not to reconstruct a causally pristine life. Ortmann's analysis of performativity emphasizes that continuing, withdrawing or replacing an influential model can each affect its target (2025, §6.2). Here too, an institution's continuing arrangement is a choice with effects, not a neutral baseline against which only changes require justification. [Ortmann](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-025-00651-7).

This approach accommodates both loss and foregone development. If suspension eroded an established skill, supervised practice might restore it. If suspension prevented learning a new procedure, practice might create a skill she never previously possessed. The difference can matter to the strength and extent of her claim, but it does not determine whether there is a claim at all. The crucial question is whether the authority can justify maintaining its own avoidable obstacle to meeting the criterion it invokes.

The comparison also prevents an excessively narrow assessment of proportionality. Suppose opening the simulator requires expenditure. The authority cannot establish that this expenditure is excessive merely by noting that another renewal notice is cheaper. Renewal without practice carries a foreseeable prospect of prolonged exclusion. The relevant alternatives include those continuing burdens as well as the immediate budgetary costs. Otherwise the institution could always make reopening appear wasteful by counting the costs of assistance and treating the costs of prolonged restriction as given.

This does not settle every trade-off in Mara's favor. Another claimant may need the simulator for an urgent task. Equivalent training elsewhere might be readily available to her at modest cost. A proposed intervention might have too little prospect of success to justify its expense. These are reasons about the alternatives. They are not repetitions of the fact that she presently fails the standard.

A useful diagnostic question follows: *Would the institution's stated reason for denying the proposed route apply with the same force to a person for whom that route was both safe and likely to overcome the disqualification?* If so, the reason may explain restriction while failing to explain obstruction of recovery. This is a test for an unanswered question, not a decision procedure. Present incapacity can also provide evidence that a particular course is unsafe or futile; when it does, it bears on the additional question and must be considered.

For example, “Mara is currently unqualified” is insufficient to refuse ordinary supervised practice. “The particular deficit makes this simulator unsafe even under supervision, and no safe modification is available” can be sufficient. The latter statement adds a claim about the proposed route. That claim requires evidence and is open to challenge, but it does not become circular simply because it concerns the same underlying deficit.

## 6. What reopening does not require

The strongest objection is that a duty to assist can be smuggled into the phrase “proportionate means.” If an intervention has already been judged proportionate, have we not assumed that it should occur?

Proportionality is indeed a substantive assessment, not an automatic calculation. The argument does not generate assistance from descriptive facts alone. Its contribution is to identify what must be assessed, whose responsibility assessment and provision are, and which apparently complete justifications leave that responsibility unanswered. An institution must assess the continuing restrictive arrangement against feasible routes out of it; it cannot assign all justificatory weight to the accuracy of today's verdict. Even where people disagree about the eventual allocation, that is a consequential constraint on their reasoning.

Nor does the argument grant Mara priority over everyone with an equally serious need. A directed duty arising from an institution's exercise of power can coexist with broader duties of fair distribution. If meeting it would deprive others of basic protections, provision may be postponed, modified or refused. Such a refusal must acknowledge the continuing burden and justify the allocation. The authority cannot instead reclassify Mara's exclusion as simply the natural result of her incompetence. Whether it must offer another form of assistance or compensation then depends on further moral facts; the reopening principle alone does not decide.

A second objection insists that only a previous wrong could ground a special duty of repair. Since the suspension was justified, the authority owes nothing beyond accurate review.

This confuses corrective liability with the responsibilities of administering a continuing measure. An emergency evacuation can be justified while leaving the evacuating authority responsible for arranging a safe return when conditions permit. That responsibility need not be punishment for having evacuated people. Similarly, a licensing authority can owe a route toward reinstatement as part of properly administering a justified suspension. Its refusal to reopen may be the first wrong in the history.

The evacuation comparison does not establish that every restriction must eventually be lifted. If the danger remains and no proportionate route back exists, the duty can be satisfied by a serious inquiry that establishes those facts and by whatever alternatives other principles require. It establishes only that the absence of an original wrong cannot, by itself, exempt the authority from later enabling responsibilities.

A third objection is that providing practice manufactures favorable evidence. Why is this less objectionable than manufacturing evidence that confirms an adverse judgment?

The answer depends on the legitimate object of assessment. If the question is whether Mara can operate a bridge safely after training, successful training supplies exactly the kind of changed condition that matters. If the question is whether she already possessed a skill last month, training cannot establish that. There is nothing objectionable in making a present qualification true, provided the means are permissible and the assessment asks the appropriate question. Teaching someone the procedure is different from coaching her to imitate competence on an unrepresentative test.

A restriction's release criterion must therefore be justified independently of the institution's interest in vindicating its past decisions. A condition that says “demonstrate proficiency without any help ever provided during suspension” needs its own rationale. Where independent performance is necessary, training followed by independent assessment can still be appropriate. A rule excluding every influence of assistance would confuse the ability to act independently with the ability to acquire that ability unaided.

A fourth objection concerns uncertainty. Institutions may not know whether their restriction is sustaining the deficit or whether assistance would work. Demanding proof of causation could make reopening depend on evidence the restricted person cannot obtain; demanding assistance whenever someone alleges an effect could overwhelm the institution.

A proportionate response can be staged. A credible account of how the regime blocks preparation, together with a plausible, safe alternative, gives the institution a reason to investigate. It need not already prove that the individual would succeed. Where a limited trial can clarify both the causal effect and the prospect of recovery at tolerable cost, that trial can itself be the required route. Where useful inquiry would be unacceptably risky, uncertainty can justify continued restriction. What uncertainty cannot do is transform knowledge that the person fails today into knowledge that no reasonable opportunity could matter.

The scope remains deliberately bounded. The principle does not supply a universal probability threshold, a complete allocation theory or a guarantee of restoration. It does identify an evidential asymmetry: evidence sufficient for immediate precaution may be insufficient for refusing a safe inquiry into how the need for precaution could end. Treating those thresholds as identical gives restrictive institutions an avoidable advantage over the people who must seek release from them.

## 7. A different object of review

An institution can be correct about the person before it and wrong about what it owes that person. The gap is especially clear when its own restrictive arrangement sustains the condition its accurate verdict records. Neither exposing falsehood nor repeating a fair test necessarily closes that gap.

The appropriate object of review is therefore larger than the verdict. It includes the institution's continuing control over the routes by which a different verdict could become warranted. In the bridge case, retaining a sound safety standard and reopening supervised practice are compatible obligations. Mara need not be released before she qualifies. She must have a justified answer to the question of why the authority prevents her from trying to qualify through a safe, proportionate route.

A system of conditional restriction becomes answerable in this fuller sense when it must justify the obstacles it maintains as well as the judgments it makes. The next accurate verdict can justify caution today. It cannot, on its own, justify closing the route to a different tomorrow.
