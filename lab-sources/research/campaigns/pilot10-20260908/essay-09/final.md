# When Restrictions Sustain Their Own Reasons

## 1. The next accurate verdict

A licensing authority suspends Mara from operating a city's movable bridges. A sound assessment shows that she cannot reliably perform a newly required emergency procedure. The requirement is appropriate, the assessment is accurate, and immediate suspension is justified. At that moment, no safe training facility is available.

Several weeks later, supervised practice becomes possible. The authority nevertheless admits only currently certified operators to the relevant equipment. Mara cannot acquire the missing proficiency through ordinary work because she is suspended, and she cannot obtain supervised practice because she is uncertified. At each review she fails a fair assessment. Every verdict correctly records her present inability. The authority explains its decisions clearly and would reinstate anyone who passed. It also knows that supervised practice would give Mara a reasonable chance of passing without exposing the public to the risks that justified suspension.

Something has gone wrong, but identifying a false belief will not locate it. Mara really is unready. Nor must she establish that the original suspension was unjust. The authority has treated the question whether she may operate a bridge today as settling whether it must make a different answer possible tomorrow.

The example isolates a general problem: an institution can sustain the condition that supports its own continuing restriction. Accurate assessment and conscientious review can coexist with avoidable control over whether the condition could ever be overcome. What responsibility follows from that control?

I defend a bounded duty of *reopening*. An institution exercising significant restrictive authority must not retain avoidable control over the routes to satisfying its own release criterion without a justification for that additional control. Where its arrangement forecloses otherwise permissible preparation or demonstration, it must remove the obstacle or arrange a reasonable substitute, subject to independently defensible limits of safety, feasibility and fair allocation. This responsibility does not by itself settle who must pay the ordinary costs of becoming qualified.

The argument offers a general explanation and boundary for a familiar kind of enabling responsibility. It does not claim to discover that institutions can create confirming behavior, that hearings need material support, or that justified restriction can coexist with owed assistance. Its distinctive target is the inference from an accurate present verdict to the permissibility of leaving the surrounding arrangement unchanged.

The bridge case is stipulated. It describes no actual licensing system. Its initial judgment and safety standard are granted so that neither prejudice nor a dispute about engineering supplies the answer.

## 2. Confirmation, review and their antecedents

Merton already explains how responses to a social judgment can produce its apparent confirmation, including through institutional exclusion (1948, pp. 195–197). King and Mertens analyze self-fulfilling predictions, suppressed error signals and impediments to accountability, including cases without a process mistake (2023, §§2.5, 3.3–3.5). De Bruin examines credibility deficits made accurate through social mechanisms, including effects on investment in skills (2025, §3.2). These accounts establish that discovering feedback is not yet a new normative contribution. [Merton](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4609267), [King and Mertens](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10677-022-10359-9), [de Bruin](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2025.10052).

A particularly close antecedent appears in the United Kingdom Supreme Court's 2014 *Haney* judgment. Paragraphs 36–39 recognize reasonable opportunities for rehabilitation and for demonstrating reduced danger, distinguish facilities preceding review from review itself, and separate the enabling duty from an entitlement to immediate release. The reasoning invokes rehabilitation and the scheme of Article 5. Paragraph 60 also rejects an obligation to maximize provision. This historical argument already exhibits the coexistence of continued restriction and owed enabling assistance. [*Haney*, §§36–39, 60](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2014_0036_judgment_9a6017c043.pdf).

That antecedent leaves a philosophical question. Detention involves exceptional control over bodily liberty and can carry an express rehabilitative purpose. A licensing authority might accept the prison duty yet deny being a provider of vocational education. We need an argument about restrictive authority that identifies when an analogous responsibility arises without importing either a prison's purposes or the full strength of a prisoner's claim to liberty.

It helps first to distinguish three objects of judgment. One is the person's present condition. If treatment has diminished a skill, a reliable test can establish that it is diminished. Causal involvement does not make the measurement worthless. Successful teaching also produces the competence that subsequent assessments register.

A second object is the warrant for an earlier decision. Later evidence cannot change which reasons were available when that decision was made. But Mara need not reopen that issue: her initial assessment was sound. A restrictive arrangement can keep a true condition true without turning an original falsehood into truth.

The third object is the institution's present arrangement. Whether Mara may operate today and whether the authority may deny supervised practice are different practical questions. Her incapacity can justify today's suspension while leaving the second question unanswered. The point is not that incapacity becomes irrelevant whenever it is institutionally sustained. It is that evidence supporting one decision need not justify every policy surrounding that decision.

An open hearing can therefore be insufficient in two ways. Someone may possess a qualification but lack an accepted way to demonstrate it. Alternatively, the surrounding regime may prevent acquiring the qualification itself. In the latter case, a flawless examination merely records the continuing absence. The responsibility of review must sometimes extend upstream to the conditions under which a different verdict could become warranted.

## 3. Two powers requiring justification

The normative argument begins with a limit on the authority under discussion. Its subjects have a substantial interest in an activity, liberty or public good, and a claim to access on justifiable terms. The institution restricts that access for a conditional reason, such as a person's present inability to act safely. It must justify its exercise of power to the affected people. This is a substantive moral premise, not a definition of every adverse decision.

Within such a relationship, distinguish the power to enforce a qualification from the power to control the permissible means of obtaining it. They are often combined, but the justification for the first need not justify the second. The public's interest in safe bridges supports requiring competence. It does not automatically support a rule reserving safe practice for people who are already competent.

Compare two regimes. Both forbid unqualified independent operation. One permits supervised preparation followed by the same independent assessment; the other prevents it. Suppose the former can be administered with equivalent protection for the public and without disproportionate burdens on others. The subject can accept the safety standard while rejecting the latter regime. That objection asks for neither a lower standard nor immediate release. It contests an additional exercise of control for which the original safety reason is insufficient.

Why should the objection generate a duty, rather than merely a request for generosity? Because the authority is itself blocking an otherwise permissible response to the condition it invokes. The baseline is not a world in which an unrelated benefactor declines to improve someone's prospects. It is a relationship in which one party restricts an activity and also decides whether the other may use a feasible means of meeting its condition. Leaving that means wholly discretionary would give the authority power to prolong the restriction independently of the reason for imposing it.

The principle underlying the objection is this: conditional authority over an important interest does not include discretionary authority to foreclose the permissible ways of bringing its condition to an end. Foreclosure requires its own justification. This principle respects both parties' claims. Others retain protection against unsafe conduct; the restricted person retains a claim against avoidable institutional obstacles to becoming safe. Treating the latter claim as optional charity would make the practical reach of the restriction depend on the authority's willingness to let its terminating condition become achievable.

The objection can require more than deleting a rule. Suppose the authority justifiably prohibits unsupervised preparation, but safe preparation requires a supervisor. It cannot claim to have relinquished control merely by allowing Mara into an empty room where she remains forbidden to practice. To reopen the route, it must permit a competent external supervisor, coordinate access to an existing service, or reasonably arrange provision. Which response is required depends on the feasible alternatives. The duty is to discharge its responsibility for the institutional obstacle; it need not deliver the service through its own employees.

This does not derive positive assistance from the logical inadequacy of one reason. The moral bridge is the prohibition on retaining unjustified control over permissible means of ending one's restrictive power. Where ceasing that control requires reasonable administrative cooperation, a duty of cooperation follows. A more extensive duty of provision arises when the arrangement continues to foreclose independent access and providing or arranging a substitute is its reasonable means of discharging that responsibility.

The limited-mandate objection can now be answered. A licensing office's employees may indeed lack authority to run a school. They should not pretend otherwise. But a governing arrangement cannot justify prohibiting independent preparation, withholding an authorized substitute, and treating the absence of a training mandate as the end of the matter. It must either permit another safe route or allocate responsibility for providing one, unless it has adequate reasons against both. Protecting the public can justify control over preparation; when that control forecloses independent provision, it can also generate ancillary responsibilities not mentioned in a narrow description of the licensing task.

## 4. Access, arrangement and payment

The duty has different practical implications that should not be conflated.

First, the institution must remove an unjustified eligibility barrier. If unlicensed people can practice safely under supervision, lack of a licence alone does not justify excluding them from such practice. Removing the barrier must include the ordinary administrative work needed to make access effective: a usable application process, fair consideration and recognition of an appropriate assessment. Announcing theoretical eligibility while preserving procedures that make entry impossible does not discharge the duty.

Second, where the restrictive arrangement still makes independent access unavailable, the institution must reasonably arrange a route. This can involve providing facilities, recognizing an external provider or coordinating a supervised placement. The obligation concerns whether there is an institutionally available route on defensible terms. It does not guarantee that every individual will succeed or that each will receive the preferred service immediately.

Third, the distribution of the costs of preparation requires a further argument. Control over an obstruction does not establish that the restricting body's budget must pay every expense of overcoming the disqualifying condition. Teaching, supervision and living expenses during training are real costs. Some would exist even under a fair and open qualification regime. They cannot all be classified as costs of repairing the institution's interference.

Consider a harder version of Mara's case. The authority opens the simulator to uncertified operators, gives her a fair place in the queue and charges the same reasonable fee as other applicants. The fee covers supervision. Mara cannot afford it. An assistance fund can finance only one of several equally needy and promising applicants.

Reopening alone does not establish that Mara must receive the grant, that the licensing office must pay her fee, or that prior certification gives her priority. If the access terms and allocation are independently justified, the institution may have discharged the responsibility arising specifically from its former eligibility barrier even though Mara still needs assistance. General duties concerning opportunity and disadvantage may require that assistance. The present principle supplies neither an exemption from those duties nor a complete account of them.

This limit should not be exaggerated in the other direction. An institution cannot make an unnecessary access rule financially costly and then cite the cost it has created as sufficient reason to preserve that rule. Nor can it insist that a person separately purchase an administrative change the authority already owes to everyone similarly situated. Justification must address the expense under a fairly administered alternative, not simply inherit whatever price the existing arrangement puts on escape. Special compensation for lost skills, delay or other damage remains a further question.

The distinction situates reopening within the recourse literature. Venkatasubramanian and Alfano treat recourse as supporting extended agency and trust. Their discussion of excessive costs recognizes limits to individual adjustment and a place for structural reform (2020, §§1.1, 4.6, 5). Reopening identifies a directed responsibility where a restrictive arrangement controls a relevant obstacle; it does not turn all unaffordable recourse into a claim against the decision-maker's budget. [Venkatasubramanian and Alfano](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372876).

Similarly, Vredenburgh's defense of informed self-advocacy explicitly distinguishes informational requirements from material ones (2022, §3.2; accepted manuscript p. 8, n. 19). Reopening specifies one responsibility within that material domain. It leaves room for important unmet needs after its particular obligation has been discharged. [Vredenburgh](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopp.12262).

## 5. Who falls within the principle?

Mara's earlier certification is not essential to the argument. Suppose Nara is a first-time applicant with the same missing competence, prospects and need. The authority's certification requirement applies to her too, and it controls the only safe preparation. Nara also has a claim against an unjustified rule admitting only certified people. Prior inclusion may support additional claims based on reliance or loss, but it does not monopolize the basic claim to a justifiable qualification regime.

Accepting this implication is preferable to defining every suspension as a restriction while treating identical constraints on applicants as mere denials. The morally significant facts are the scope of the authority, the importance of the interest and responsibility for the obstacle. A change of administrative label does not alter those facts.

The same point supplies a boundary. A voluntary chess club may reserve its tournament for accomplished players without owing novices remedial instruction. Ordinarily it does not prohibit them from learning elsewhere, control entry to an important activity across a wider jurisdiction, or administer a public opportunity to which they already have a claim on fair terms. Owning a useful training room and setting an admission standard do not alone establish the relation of authority at issue here.

If the club were also the sole public certifier of a livelihood, however, calling it voluntary would no longer settle the question. Equally, a benefit denial is not automatically exempt just because its administrator describes it as withholding a benefit. An agency controlling a substantial public entitlement may exercise the relevant power. Whether there is a prior claim to access on justifiable terms requires a separate moral assessment. The principle does not manufacture that claim from the fact that someone wants an institution's resources.

Responsibility for the obstacle also needs precision. If a person can already obtain recognized preparation independently on justified terms, the authority need not duplicate it. If scarcity or the nature of the activity makes safe preparation unavailable regardless of the restrictive arrangement, institutional control has not itself supplied the alleged obstacle. A prohibition can then remain justified without the authority owing an impossible route to qualification.

Conversely, administrative fragmentation does not make a created obstacle disappear. The office issuing suspensions, the department operating facilities and the body authorizing their budgets may be different. Their responsibilities differ as well. A review office that receives a credible account of a blocked route must identify the competent body, refer the problem and pursue an answer within its authority. The body controlling the relevant rule must assess alternatives. The governing institution responsible for their division of powers must make reasonable coordination possible.

A clerk's physical ability to unlock a door is not enough to establish authority to allocate a public facility. But a chain of offices, each accurately reporting its limited remit, does not justify a governing arrangement that leaves nobody responsible for a foreseeable consequence of their combined power. Reopening is discharged through an appropriate allocation of responsibility, not by insisting that every official personally supply every remedy.

These limits explain why the argument extends the rehabilitation analogy selectively. Restrictive institutions vary greatly in the interests they control and the opportunities they displace. Confinement can create unusually extensive dependency and exceptionally strong enabling claims. Licensing commonly permits independent preparation and can leave ordinary training expenses with applicants. The shared structure is control over permissible routes to satisfying a terminating condition; the strength and content of the resulting duties depend on the surrounding relationship.

## 6. Assessing a route fairly

Reopening requires comparison between feasible policies over time. Immediate release and unchanged restriction are not the only options. Suspension with safe preparation may impose no greater danger than suspension without preparation while reducing the prospect of prolonged exclusion. The comparison must count the burdens of continued restriction as well as the expenditure required for a route out of it.

Present incapacity remains relevant. Its severity may make a proposed course ineffective, demand more supervision or create risks that no available safeguard can address. The modest claim is that *bare* present disqualification does not explain why a specified alternative may permissibly be rejected. A complete reason must connect the relevant facts to the alternative's prospects, risks, costs or fairness.

Thus “Mara is currently unqualified” answers too little when used to deny supervised preparation. “The proposed supervision cannot control the risks associated with her particular deficit” adds a claim that bears on the proposal. A fair queue or an urgent competing need can also justify postponement, even if Mara's participation would otherwise be safe and successful. The appropriate question concerns the institution's choice between policies, not whether its reason varies between different kinds of applicant.

Feasibility must likewise be assessed as a policy. One exceptional place might be affordable while comparable provision for everyone similarly situated would be impossible. Fair scheduling, recognition of other providers or phased expansion may provide a reasonable route without immediate access for all. On the other hand, repeated underfunding of a foreseeable need cannot automatically establish that a route is morally unavailable. Capacity planning and the decision to impose the restrictive regime belong in the same assessment. A present official may face a real shortage while the institution owes a change in how that shortage is produced.

The comparison is prospective. It does not reconstruct the person Mara would have been in a world untouched by institutions. Her skills were already formed through workplace practices and training. Ortmann's discussion of continuing, suspending and replacing a performative model illustrates why the existing arrangement need not be treated as causally neutral (2025, §6.3). Here the relevant alternatives start from the actual situation and ask which changes can reasonably be made. [Ortmann](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-025-00651-7).

Reopening can therefore enable either restored ability or new learning. It can also legitimately produce favorable evidence. If the criterion concerns safe performance after training, making the person competent serves that criterion. If it concerns possession of a skill at an earlier date, subsequent training cannot establish it. An institution must keep these questions distinct. Preparing someone to perform independently differs from coaching an appearance of competence on an unrepresentative test.

Uncertainty sometimes justifies investigation before provision. A credible account of how a rule blocks preparation, together with a plausible safe alternative, can warrant a limited trial or a feasibility assessment. The person need not first prove that she would succeed: that may be what the proposed opportunity is meant to discover. Equally, a bare allegation of obstruction does not establish an unlimited obligation to experiment. Inquiry must itself be proportionate and sensitive to others' claims.

A reasonable route offers a serious opportunity, not guaranteed success. Mara may fail after adequate preparation or decline to participate. The duty does not authorize forced improvement. Nor does a breach automatically require lifting a restriction that continues to protect others from an unacceptable risk. Immediate restraint and a duty to repair the surrounding arrangement can coexist. What ceases to be defensible is the claim that the restraint's present necessity exhausts the institution's responsibilities.

## 7. The resulting obligation

The argument supports the following principle:

*Where an institution exercises conditional restrictive authority over a substantial interest to which people have a claim on justifiable terms, it must remove unjustified institutional obstacles to satisfying or demonstrating its terminating condition. If it reasonably retains control that forecloses independent access, it must reasonably arrange a substitute route, unless considerations of safety, feasibility or fair allocation justify withholding it. Responsibility for the ordinary costs of qualification requires an additional justification.*

This is a condition on the administration of power, not a complete theory of opportunity or a guarantee of successful rehabilitation. It applies even when the original restriction was warranted, because enabling responsibilities need not be compensation for an earlier wrong. Refusing reasonable reopening may be the first wrong in the history.

Its philosophical payoff is a distinction between two forms of answerability. An institution must answer whether its verdict correctly applies a legitimate standard. It must also answer for its control over the permissible routes by which that standard could be met. The second responsibility is neither dissolved by perfect accuracy nor automatically satisfied by another hearing.

Mara need not operate a bridge before she qualifies. But she is owed more than the repeated explanation that she does not qualify. Where the institution controls a safe route to doing so, it must justify its policy toward that route. The next accurate verdict can warrant caution today. It cannot, on its own, justify closing the way to a different tomorrow.
