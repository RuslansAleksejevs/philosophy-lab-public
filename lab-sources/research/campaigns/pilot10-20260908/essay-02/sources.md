## Load-bearing primary works

### 1. Dana Sarah Howard (2015)

**Transforming Others: On the Limits of “You’ll Be Glad I Did It” Reasoning.** *Res Philosophica* 92(2): 341–370. DOI: https://doi.org/10.11612/resphil.2015.92.2.9.

Read version: author's accepted/draft manuscript, https://philpapers.org/archive/HOWTOO-5.pdf, 34 PDF pages. Publication metadata corroborated by the indexed published issue. Read manuscript pp. 1–10, 19–30, and located the remainder of §5; did not read all of the journal issue. Citations in the essay will use section locators, avoiding a false conversion from manuscript pagination to journal pagination.

Selected exact excerpt, manuscript p. 22, §4: “the problem is appealing to the predicted preferences of only one course of action.”

Relevance: a direct predecessor to the broad initial proposal. Howard rejects one-path predictive endorsement and investigates reasonable future attitudes across alternatives. Her discussion prevents claiming that future satisfaction's inadequacy is a new discovery. The new draft must concern an additional procedural issue: who can make a negative assessment count as a completed assessment.

### 2. L. A. Paul and Cass R. Sunstein (undated preliminary draft)

**“As Judged By Themselves”: Transformative Experiences and Endogenous Preferences.** Author-hosted preliminary manuscript, 9 pages: https://www.lapaul.org/papers/As%20Judged%20by%20Themselves%20Paul%20Sunstein.pdf.

Read all nine pages of this manuscript. The author's publication list labels the item a 2019 SSRN draft; the PDF tool title contains “Spring 2021” while the visible first page gives no date. The essay will cite it as an undated preliminary manuscript rather than invent a definitive publication year or venue. Relevant locators: pp. 5–6 (endogenous satisfaction and competing futures), pp. 8–9 (process, autonomy, and coercion).

Selected exact excerpt, p. 6: “we need a further criterion.”

Relevance: already diagnoses why satisfaction induced by a choice cannot uniquely recommend that choice and raises process concerns. A contribution must go beyond an endogeneity warning, identifying a distinct condition on the use of endorsement as authorization of a review procedure.

### 3. Richard Pettigrew (2026)

**Consent and the Formation of Preferences.** *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, Early View, first published 10 June 2026. DOI and full text: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phpr.70131.

Read main text §§1–5, including seven preference-formation mechanisms, the attention discussion, and the Frankfurt-style case in §4. Bibliography was checked selectively, not read as source evidence for uncited authors.

Selected exact excerpt, §4: “What matters is the way the preferences are actually formed”.

Relevance: preference formation can preserve agency even amid imposed circumstances; counterfactual coercion need not invalidate actually free consent. This is the strongest challenge to a proposed counterfactual closure condition. The draft must distinguish a token person's effective consent from the institutional authority claimed for the procedure that selects such tokens.

### 4. Eric Chwang (2009)

**A Defense of Subsequent Consent.** *Journal of Social Philosophy* 40(1): 117–131. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9833.2009.01441.x. Author-hosted published PDF: https://spot.colorado.edu/~chwang/chwang-eric-a-defense-of.pdf.

Read main argument §§1–7, especially §3.2 (not minding), §5.1 (vacillation), §5.2 (causal relations). The online PDF has 16 pages; journal page numbers printed in it are used where needed.

Selected exact excerpt, §5.2, p. 127: “rather, it depends on the causal relations between those events”.

Relevance: subsequent consent is not merely gratitude; a broad temporal dismissal would evade the strongest opponent. Chwang already recognizes that value-forming causal relations need scrutiny. The essay should identify a limited failure of authority-based procedural vindication, leaving the possibility of some valid subsequent consent open.

### 5. David Enoch (2017)

**Hypothetical Consent and the Value(s) of Autonomy.** *Ethics* 128(1): 6–36. DOI and full text: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/692939.

Read §§I–III and VI–VIII in the primary full text; §V was read in the returned contextual passage. Do not claim exhaustive reading of §§IV or IX. Relevant citation: §VIII (sovereignty and nonalienation).

Selected exact excerpt, §VIII: “the fact that I would have decided it differently under different conditions”.

Relevance: distinguishes honoring a person's actual decision-making authority from alignment with deep commitments. The draft's issue is narrower: retaining authority over when the person's later judgment may settle a disputed intervention.

### 6. Farbod Akhlaghi (2023)

**Transformative experience and the right to revelatory autonomy.** *Analysis* 83(1): 3–12. First published online 31 December 2022. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/anac084. Full text accessed through https://academic.oup.com/analysis/article/83/1/3/6966040, which redirected to the publisher's minimal article view.

Read §§1–4 and relevant footnotes. Relevant locator: §3, final paragraphs on rational persuasion and self-authorship.

Selected exact excerpt: “Rational persuasion does respect an agent’s ability to be a competent, capable reasoner.”

Relevance: respect for rational deliberation need not settle respect for a person's self-making. This is a predecessor to the general distinction; the proposed claim must specify the neglected role of closure and its particular justification.

Search results are discovery aids, not evidence that an unread argument has been assessed. No precise existing statement of the proposed closure condition was identified in the works read. This is not an exhaustive novelty search and does not establish originality.

The original broad thought was substantially anticipated by Howard, Paul and Sunstein, and the autonomy literature. Preserve question.md unchanged as the first candidate. The refined proposal concerns **closure of ratification**: if an intervener claims legitimacy from deferring to a transformed person's later judgment, equally competent rejection must be capable of settling that appeal to authority under a rule whose warrant does not itself depend solely on later approval. A procedure that treats approval as final and rejection as grounds for further preference-shaping cannot cite its eventual favorable endpoint as proof that it surrendered the decision to the person. A token consent generated within it may nevertheless validly authorize a new action or waive a particular remedy. That separation, its justification, and its limited procedural consequence are the candidate contribution.

## Intended argumentative tests

- Compare procedures with identical actual final reflection and different treatment of competent dissent. Avoid declaring the present person inauthentic merely because of a bad origin.
- Distinguish evidence of present welfare, normative permission for a next action, release of a specified claim, and vindication of the process.
- Address the objection that consent exercises a power rather than reports a truth; the critique of an endorsement metric alone is insufficient.
- Address voluntary delegation, educational persuasion, responsive repair, incapacity, and Frankfurt-style nonintervention.
- State a necessary constraint on one kind of justification, not a full theory of autonomy, retrospective consent, or optimal transformation.
