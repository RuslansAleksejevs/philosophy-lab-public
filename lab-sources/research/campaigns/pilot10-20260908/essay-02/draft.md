# Who Gets to Declare the Transformation Complete?

Suppose someone changes your life and, in doing so, changes the values with which you assess what they have done. You later approve. Your approval might be sincere, informed, and carefully considered. It might also be exactly what the person who changed you intended to produce. These possibilities are compatible. The interesting question is therefore not simply whether the approval is authentic. It is what the approval entitles its recipient to claim.

Consider a residential institute that helps adults explore new vocations. Its advisers arrange encounters, classes, and practical work, sometimes with effects on residents' deepest commitments. One adviser redirects Arden's programme toward environmental restoration. Whether this falls within the adviser's discretion is disputed. Arden was willing to explore unfamiliar work but had not expressly agreed to this particular redirection. After several months, Arden values the work, identifies with its community, and reflectively approves the adviser's initiative. There has been no deception about what happened. Arden can leave without penalty and has access to people who reject the institute's outlook. Let us stipulate that the resulting judgment is autonomous.

The institute now offers a defence: its initiative was respectful because the person who experienced it was allowed to judge it afterwards. To assess that defence, we need one more fact. What would the institute have counted as a completed judgment if Arden had rejected the initiative?

I argue that an appeal to a transformed person's authority requires a constraint on who may bring ratification to a close. An intervener cannot claim to have deferred to that person while treating competent approval as a verdict and equally competent rejection, merely because it is rejection, as an unfinished stage of transformation. Call this the *closure condition*. It governs a specific justification: the claim that a procedure is legitimate because its subject has been entrusted with judging it. It does not supply a complete test of legitimate influence, nor does its failure automatically invalidate each act of consent produced within the procedure. Indeed, its significance emerges most clearly when the person's present consent remains effective.

The familiar observation that future satisfaction cannot by itself justify an intervention is not my contribution. Howard argues that considering only the future attitudes produced by the proposed action neglects the attitudes that alternatives would produce (2015, §§4–5). Paul and Sunstein similarly show that endogenous preference change can make subsequent satisfaction insufficient to distinguish transformative options; they also raise concerns about the process producing that satisfaction (undated, pp. 5–9). These arguments establish an important starting point. My question concerns a further choice that an intervener makes: which stage of the person's changing assessment will be recognized as the stage at which that person has finally answered.

This matters because deference can fail even when the approved outcome is neither inferior nor based on defective preferences. An institute might help residents acquire valuable commitments while reserving to itself the power to declare critical assessments premature. Its error would then concern the allocation of authority, rather than the quality of the lives it produces. Identifying that error should not require us to rank all possible future selves, diagnose the residents as victims of false consciousness, or insist that their earliest values were their truest ones.

We should first separate four things that later approval can do. It can tell us how a person experiences her present life. It can express a wish that a relationship or activity continue. It can release a specified complaint or claim to a remedy. Finally, it can be invoked to vindicate the procedure through which the present situation was created. These are different objects of judgment and different possible normative effects. A person's enjoyment of her present life does not establish her willingness to waive a claim; waiving a claim need not express the belief that there was never a wrong. Equally, approving an activity from now on need not confer approval on everything that led to it.

For example, someone who was wrongfully prevented from accepting a position elsewhere may come to love the life she built where she remained. She can truthfully testify to that love, decide to stay, and decline compensation without undertaking to defend the prevention. Conversely, she might regard the prevention as justified under exceptional circumstances while wanting to leave now. Neither combination is incoherent. The object of her present decision, rather than a generic attitude of satisfaction, determines what others may infer from it.

Nor should we settle the issue by stipulating that all subsequent consent is powerless. Chwang distinguishes consent from gratitude and defends the possible moral efficacy of subsequent consent. He treats some problematic cases as failures in the causal production of consent rather than failures arising simply from its temporal position (2009, §§3.2, 5.2). That is a serious challenge to any argument that merely says the intervention came first. The closure condition accepts the challenge. It concerns an intervener's control over the selection of a ratifying judgment, and can therefore be violated before an intervention as well as after it.

Return to Arden. Imagine two procedures under which the actual encounters, deliberation, and approving statement are identical. Under the first, a review takes place when Arden judges that there has been enough opportunity for consideration, subject to an independently specified minimum of information and competence. Approval or rejection will be registered as the result. Arden may later change that judgment, but the adviser cannot require Arden to undergo more transformation merely because the answer was unfavourable.

Under the second, the institute calls a review complete whenever a resident approves its initiative. A resident who rejects it is offered another period of experiences, another interpretive framework, or another mentor, and is classified as still working through the transition. If the resident eventually leaves while rejecting the initiative, the file records a withdrawal before completed assessment. The institute preserves the critical statements accurately. Its fault is not falsifying what residents said. It is assigning different authority to their statements according to whether they vindicate it. The present thought experiment need not assume that residents are coerced into staying or that every offered experience undermines agency.

Arden approves at the first review in both procedures. No further influence occurs in either. We can therefore preserve the stipulation that Arden's consent is equally autonomous. Yet the second institute's claim to have entrusted judgment to its residents is false in a way the first institute's claim need not be. It has entrusted them with a route to approval while retaining the power to decide when other answers count as answers. An adviser who says, “You will decide whether I acted acceptably,” but would not accept an equally considered negative decision has not surrendered the issue in the promised sense.

There is an elementary evidential defect here. If the records labelled completed assessments contain approval because approval is required for completion, the presence of approval in those records is no independent evidence of an approving population. More elaborate and more sincere testimonials do not remove that selection fact. They may provide further evidence concerning particular experiences, but the institution cannot infer vindication from the fact that all completed assessments vindicate it. No numerical model is needed to see this. A rule selecting only one answer cannot use the resulting unanimity as evidence that it allowed both answers to settle the question.

The moral defect, however, is not exhausted by misleading evidence. Imagine that every relevant feature of the protocol is openly disclosed and that nobody mistakes its reports for a representative survey. The institute still cannot defend its procedural discretion by saying that residents possess the final authority over whether its redirections are acceptable. Public knowledge that only favourable judgments receive final standing makes the distribution of authority clearer; it does not change that distribution. The issue is not whether we have inferred the residents' preferences accurately. It is whether the residents' preferences are being used within a practice that gives their judgments the authority claimed for them.

The argument can be stated without relying on the institute's precise arrangements. First, an authority-based defence of an intervention presents the subject's judgment as capable of settling some question that the intervener was not entitled to settle unilaterally. Otherwise, the appeal to the subject contributes no authority: it merely reports an outcome the intervener likes. Second, settling that question requires a rule for distinguishing a delivered verdict from an inquiry still in progress. Third, if the intervener may deem any competent unfavourable verdict unfinished solely because it is unfavourable, it retains control over that distinction. The subject's judgment then cannot settle the question on the terms the defence claims. Therefore, such a procedure cannot derive the claimed authority from its favourable endpoint alone.

The second premise is the least obvious. Why must deciding a question include any authority over when one's answer counts? Because a decision that must be reopened whenever another person dislikes it is subject to that person's continuing veto. The decision-maker may still reason excellently, state conclusions freely, and even choose actions within a permitted range. What she lacks is the standing to make her judgment operative as a judgment. This is a familiar difference in ordinary delegation. Asking someone to choose a restaurant while reserving an undisclosed veto over every restaurant except one's favourite does not give that person the advertised authority, even if she immediately chooses one's favourite for excellent reasons.

Transformation makes this defect harder to notice. In an ordinary disagreement, an intervener who will not accept the answer appears simply unwilling to defer. When the subject's standpoint is expected to change, the intervener can redescribe that same refusal as patience: the person has not yet become able to appreciate the experience. Sometimes this description is correct. But if approval is itself what marks the person as having reached the appropriate standpoint, the purported reason for withholding authority assumes the eventual answer it is meant to justify. The possibility of genuine development then supplies cover for retaining control over which developed person gets heard.

The closure condition blocks this move without requiring that the person never change again. It asks whether competent rejection can end the present attempt to obtain ratification, and be acknowledged as rejection, under standards whose justification does not consist merely in the prospect of later approval. The subject remains free to reconsider. Others remain free to form their own moral judgments. The condition does not make a person's negative evaluation infallible, nor does it give her a right to prohibit all independent inquiry into the intervention. It requires the intervener to distinguish “the person has not ratified this” from “the person has not finished becoming someone who will.”

The condition also allows relevant differences between assessments. A hurried answer given without essential information need not have the same authority as a considered answer given after it. Severe confusion can justify delay. What is excluded is an asymmetry whose only warrant is the verdict's favourable or unfavourable content. Standards concerning understanding, information, and freedom from pressure must be defensible without using agreement with the intervention as their sole test. The intervener need not be neutral about every fact or value to satisfy this requirement. It must avoid making approval the credential for being authorized to assess whether approval is owed.

There is a powerful objection. Autonomous consent does not require an available alternative at every moment. Pettigrew explicitly considers a case in which preferences are actually formed through free agency, although an oppressive regime would have imposed them if necessary. He treats consent based on the actually free formation as effective (2026, §4). Our second institute did not interfere with Arden's actual reasoning beyond the encounters stipulated to be permissible. Why should a rule that never affected the actual course of events matter?

The objection correctly protects Arden's token consent. If Arden now permits the institute to arrange a further residency, the mere existence of its defective review rule does not establish that the permission is invalid. To invalidate that permission we would need another argument concerning its scope, production, or circumstances. But the institute's defence of its review procedure has a different object. It claims that the procedure placed it under the residents' authority. A claim about the distribution of authority includes how contrary judgments would be treated. This dispositional fact may be irrelevant to whether Arden freely said yes while being essential to whether the institute ever gave Arden the power to settle the issue.

The resulting distinction is not between genuine and fake consent. It is between the exercise of a person's normative power on this occasion and a further claim that the institution's policy derived its legitimacy from submitting to that power. The former can succeed while the latter fails. Just as a person's competent decision can be respected on one occasion by someone who maintains a policy of overriding such decisions, effective consent can occur inside a procedure whose claimed deference is illusory. A favourable actual path does not make the policy itself a transfer of authority.

This distinguishes the argument from a general suspicion of preferences with troubling origins. Enoch's distinction between autonomy as nonalienation and as sovereignty already separates alignment with deep commitments from having the relevant decision in one's hands (2017, §VIII). Akhlaghi likewise argues that respecting someone's capacity for rational reflection need not suffice to respect her autonomous self-making (2023, §3). The present proposal specifies a particular way those dimensions can come apart: the person retains control within deliberation, while the intervener controls when deliberation is allowed to count as completed. The additional demand concerns that second allocation of control.

A further objection presses the authority point more deeply. Suppose Arden explicitly says, “I waive my complaint, and I approve the entire procedure, including its way of seeking my approval.” If Arden is competent and autonomous, does refusing the institute its vindication deny Arden precisely the authority the argument promises to protect?

We should grant as much force to Arden's statement as its object and circumstances warrant. Arden may be able to release a personal claim. Arden may authorize participation in a similar procedure in the future. Such decisions can alter what the institute owes Arden now. But none entails that the institute's earlier procedure was already justified by its having placed the issue under Arden's authority. The institute did not do that merely because Arden subsequently approves its having retained control. A person can now authorize another to retain discretion without showing that the discretion was hers to exercise at every earlier point.

Someone who accepts a strong theory of subsequent consent may describe Arden's later statement as giving the past intervention a new moral status. The closure argument does not settle that wider dispute. Its conclusion is that the institute cannot explain its earlier procedure as an exercise of deference solely by pointing to a verdict it selected under the disputed procedure. If later consent does more than release present claims, that additional force needs an account. It cannot be supplied by the false description that the institute allowed Arden's judgment to settle matters independently of which judgment Arden reached. Respecting a person's present authority does not require accepting every historical or philosophical claim made on its behalf.

The institute might instead defend its redirection by its benefits, its authorized professional role, or the costs of alternatives. Those defences could succeed. They would also expose the real question: what entitled it to exercise the relevant discretion? The appeal to eventual consent cannot eliminate that question by making its own expected success the answer. This is especially important before any intervention occurs. A promise that one can produce later approval is not yet evidence that one has arranged a process through which the person's later authority will constrain what one does.

Does the closure condition forbid education, which often aims at changes students initially resist? No. An instructor need not treat every student's negative judgment as settling whether a claim is true, whether a skill has been mastered, or whether a qualification should be awarded. Those assessments have grounds independent of the student's approval. The condition applies when an instructor invokes the student's ratification to justify an exercise of power over the student's development. A student can be wrong about a proposition and still have competently declined to ratify the manner in which she was taught it. A teacher may defend that manner on other grounds, but cannot count the student's intellectual disagreement itself as proof that her judgment about the procedure has not yet been delivered.

Nor does the condition prevent someone from requesting persistent efforts to change her values. A person might ask a guide to help her appreciate a demanding artistic practice and expressly ask the guide not to abandon the attempt after the first disappointed response. In that case, continuing after an initial negative assessment has an independent basis: the person's authorization of that form of persistence. The authorization will have a scope and may have limits. But the asymmetry is not being justified solely by the later taste it is expected to produce. One can delegate authority over parts of one's development without being able to predict the resulting preferences in detail.

The same distinction answers an objection from repair. When a resident dislikes a programme, further experiences might respond to a legitimate complaint rather than wear resistance down. The institute could change poor accommodation, remove an intrusive adviser, or provide opportunities the resident requested. It need not freeze the situation at the first moment of dissatisfaction. The question is whether the resident may settle that she rejects what has happened while authorizing a new attempt. Acknowledged failure followed by authorized repair differs from a procedure that refuses to recognize any completed negative verdict. The difference concerns the rights and reasons carried forward between stages, not how many stages occur.

A practical implication follows for systems capable of adapting what they offer to people's evolving reactions. Imagine an advisory system that recommends activities, monitors approval, and changes later recommendations partly to produce favourable retrospective assessments of its service. Such a system could help people find valuable projects. But evaluating its legitimacy solely by users' settled approval would overlook the process that decided when their approval was settled. An evaluation should therefore ask separately whether users' refusals of ratification can be recorded as completed judgments and whether continued attempts to change those judgments have a basis other than the desired endorsement. These are questions about authority that can be investigated without first resolving which possible future preferences would make users happiest.

They also suggest a less paternalistic response when an intervention has already gone wrong. We need not insist that the transformed person reject her present attachments before we can recognize a procedural wrong. Nor should we force restoration of an earlier outlook as though the earlier person had permanent ownership of the later one's life. We can honour the person's decision to continue while refusing the producer's inference that this decision proves its method was respectful. Remedies should be assessed as new actions toward the person who exists now, with their own requirements of permission. The failure of an earlier claim to authority creates no automatic authority for a rescuer.

The closure condition remains only a necessary condition on a particular defence. A review can recognize rejection fairly while the experiences before it were unjust, the information incomplete, or the permission insufficiently specific. Conversely, a procedure that fails the condition might contain genuinely beneficial encounters and wholly effective present consents. Its value lies in identifying an error without pretending to solve every question about transformation. We often cannot know in advance which values a person will acquire, or settle by inspection whether a new attachment is an improvement. We can nevertheless ask whether a professed surrender of authority includes letting a competent unfavourable judgment count.

Later endorsement deserves attention because it belongs to the person whose life has changed. That is also why its recipient must not control which version of the answer is allowed to become final. If a transformation's defender wishes to say that the person has decided, the defender must be prepared to distinguish a decision from the successful completion of an effort to secure agreement. The person may choose the life that resulted. The procedure still owes an answer for who was permitted to judge it.
