## Strongest reconstruction

The essay's best claim concerns a procedure's claim to respect a subject's authority. Suppose an institution justifies discretionary transformation by saying that the transformed person will judge the exercise of that discretion. It cannot retain the exclusive power to treat competent negative judgments as merely preparatory while counting equally competent positive judgments as delivered verdicts, and then claim that its favourable endpoint demonstrates submission to the subject. Actual consent at that endpoint can still be effective for its particular object. The defect concerns the institution's standing claim to have surrendered a decision, not necessarily the subject's present capacities or permission.

The paired institutes make the distinction intelligible: the actual approving path is held constant, while the treatment of counterfactual dissent differs. The four-way separation among present welfare, continuation, waiver, and procedural vindication is useful. So is the refusal to demand restoration of the earlier self. Those should survive revision.

The argument succeeds against an institution that explicitly promises answer-independent adjudicative authority and then withholds it. It does not yet establish that every morally significant appeal to ratification contains such a promise. Nor does the present case adequately specify what makes institutional recognition operative. These two gaps bear directly on the claimed contribution.

## 1. The authority argument needs a narrower first premise

Draft line 31 treats the subject's capacity to settle a disputed question as the necessary form in which an appeal to the subject contributes authority. This is stronger than the later concessions support. Consent can contribute authority by exercising a normative power without making the recipient's whole policy answer to the consenting person's adjudication. In particular, a recipient can respect an actual permission while lacking a respectful general disposition. The draft itself grants this at lines 43–45.

Consider a charitable fundraiser who hopes to persuade Rowan to donate. Rowan initially refuses, later freely accepts an invitation to learn more, acquires a new commitment to the cause, and finally gives an informed, unpressured donation. The fundraiser stops seeking authorization upon a yes, but would have continued making permissible invitations after a no, unless Rowan asked for no further contact. Its authorization-seeking project has an asymmetric success condition. Nevertheless, the donation is authorized by Rowan. The institution did not have to give Rowan the power to make the fundraiser permanently abandon its hope of receiving a donation.

The point is not that your institute is necessarily as innocent. It is that an asymmetric effort to obtain permission and an asymmetric adjudication of a person's verdict are different practices. Only the second directly contradicts the advertised distribution of authority. A defender who says that autonomous ratification makes the relevant intervention permissible need not also say that the ratification procedure was a transfer of adjudicative control. Your reply to the explicit ratification of the entire procedure, at lines 49–53, eventually allows this possibility. It then denies only that subsequent consent makes the earlier history one of deference. That conclusion is plausible but narrower than a general limitation on the justificatory force of later consent.

**Revision:** State the target as a claim of *procedural respect through submission to judgment*. Distinguish it explicitly from (a) the moral efficacy of an actual ratifying act and (b) the permissibility of trying to obtain such an act. Replace the suggestion that these exhaust all authority-based defences. A short passage should grant that a subsequent-consent theory might justify the intervention while leaving the description of its procedure as deferential false. This is an accepted limit, not a rebuttal to that theory.

If you want the stronger conclusion that defective closure defeats the efficacy of ratification itself, you need an additional argument. The current manuscript quite reasonably does not provide one. Do not imply that its historical-description argument has already done that work.

## 2. “Counts as completed” must have specified practical or expressive force

In the bad institute, dissent is accurately recorded, the resident may leave without penalty, and further experiences are offered rather than imposed. What, precisely, prevents a rejection from being operative? As described, the institution could recognize every resident's claim to a remedy, stop all contact on request, and allow outside review, while retaining its objectionable terminology. The good institute's contrasting prohibition on *requiring* more transformation does not distinguish it from the bad institute, which is not said to require any.

There are at least three possible defects, and the essay should choose its object:

- A complaint, review, or remedy is suspended because the institution has not accepted the resident's assessment as complete.
- An institution keeps pursuing preference change without authority to keep that process open.
- It falsely describes a person's delivered judgment, or appropriates that judgment in claiming to have respected the person.

The third can matter even if no material consequence follows. But its moral significance needs an argument about recognition, representation, or respect; it cannot simply be treated as already equivalent to the loss of control over one's life. Conversely, the first makes closure an identifiable institutional power, without requiring coercion over Arden's actual approving path.

**Revision:** Give the review a concrete function. For example, the institution's procedure could commit it to acknowledging a complaint as unratified and referring it to an independently warranted review once the resident delivers a competent negative judgment. The bad protocol instead keeps that referral pending until assent or departure, irrespective of the completeness of the person's reasons. Approval and rejection then visibly affect who must justify what. Keep exit possible and actual assent autonomous, so the central distinction is preserved.

This is a proposed reconstruction, not an assumption already supported by the draft. If you prefer the purely expressive case, defend that case directly and reduce the language of veto and operative authority accordingly. Otherwise a critic can answer that the institute controls only its filing categories, while the person retains the relevant normative powers regardless of those categories.

## 3. Distinguish rejection, termination, reconsideration, and ratification

The closure condition currently moves between letting a negative answer count, ending the present effort to obtain ratification, and stopping further transformation. These are not interchangeable.

Rowan can say, “I do not ratify what happened; I want to keep considering it; further invitations are welcome.” The negative judgment is delivered now without terminating the inquiry. Rowan can also say, “I do not yet have a verdict, but do not contact me again.” Termination then does not require a completed assessment. And Rowan can later reverse a negative judgment after a permissible invitation from the institution, rather than only by wholly self-initiated reconsideration. None of these possibilities is automatically a failure of deference.

The draft's repair discussion already points in the right direction, but “end the present attempt” remains ambiguous. What individuates an attempt? If the institute changes the file number after each rejection, a purely administrative interpretation is too weak. If any later invitation belongs to the original attempt, the condition is too strong. It should turn on the relevant authority and the person's choices concerning continued engagement, not on the label or mere temporal continuity.

**Revision:** Specify three separate judgments: whether the past act is currently ratified; whether the institution is authorized to continue soliciting reconsideration or offering transformative experiences; and whether the person has now exercised a further power, such as a waiver. Reject the inference from future hoped-for assent to the present suspension of an already delivered refusal. A later valid change of mind need not retroactively turn that earlier refusal into a non-answer.

That last point offers a promising temporal formulation: respect can require acknowledging a person's judgment when delivered even if the same person may subsequently supersede it. It does not privilege the earlier self forever. Explain the normative consequence during the interval, rather than relying on the metaphor of finality.

## 4. The strongest existing challenge is now about originality and significance

The manuscript appropriately acknowledges that endogenous endorsement is insufficient by itself. The promising added focus is the selection of the temporal stage at which endorsement is counted. However, the basic counterfactual argument is already close to established accounts of authority and control.

Enoch's §VIII does more than separate deep commitments from sovereignty: his composer example permits persuasion while denying that overriding the composer's actual decision thereby respects sovereignty. Your discussion is a plausible application to assessment timing; it should identify its additional temporal mechanism rather than presenting a further dimension of autonomy as established. [Enoch, 2017, §VIII, especially the salt and composer passages](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/692939).

An independently located predecessor is Pettit's account of control that does not depend on another party's willingness to comply. His final section explicitly explains why actual directed influence may still fall short of interpersonal control when the other party retains discretion. This is very close to your reply to the Frankfurt-style objection, though the setting and normative target differ. [Pettit, 2012, “Control = Influence + Direction” and “Three Conditions on Suitable Control”](https://academic.oup.com/clp/article/65/1/59/356902).

Lindblom's argument about incomplete authorization and contestability is another relevant institutional comparison. It treats channels for voice and a forum for resolving contests as distinct requirements. This is especially pertinent if you give the institute's recognition policy a concrete role. [Lindblom, 2019, §4, opening paragraphs](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-ethics-quarterly/article/consent-contestability-and-unions/C73DCAAE1D9EA54109FDE555453A962C).

These sources do not prove that your exact contribution exists already. They do prevent a confident claim that preserving an autonomous actual choice while denying procedural control is itself a new discovery. A restaurant version already illustrates the general structure without transformation. You need to explain why transformation creates a distinctive problem rather than merely furnishing a vivid example.

**Revision:** Develop the mechanism by which changes of evaluative standpoint allow an institution to redescribe dissent as unfinished personal development, thereby controlling which temporal assessment acquires institutional standing. Show how the proposed norm handles this problem differently from merely checking whether each particular influence was rational and consensual. If the result remains an application of existing sovereignty and contestability principles, describe it as that application and defend its usefulness. A modest but exact contribution is stronger than an uncertain claim to a new general condition.

## 5. Further stress tests worth resolving in the revision

**The transparent fundraiser:** Explain why completion of an assent-seeking project is permissible while pretending that a review lacks a negative result is objectionable. The distinction should not rest solely on renaming the procedures.

**The welcome continuation:** Preserve the present refusal as an answer while allowing the resident to invite more experiences. No renewed project should require the person to withdraw that refusal as the price of participation.

**The independently fixed review:** A review date can be scheduled through a justified procedure rather than chosen unilaterally by the subject. Your first institute currently gives Arden timing control, but your core argument requires only that the institution not disqualify an otherwise sufficient answer because of its content. Say so to avoid defending an unnecessarily strong power over timing.

**The explicit ratification of asymmetry:** Accept that a competent person may waive a claim arising from a bad review procedure if that claim is waivable. Explain exactly what remains: a false assertion that the procedure already distributed authority fairly, a procedural wrong at an earlier time, or a current obligation. These are different residuals. Do not infer an unwaived obligation merely from the persistence of a historical fact.

**The institution that never claims deference:** Benefits, professional authority, or valid delegation might justify its action. The closure argument is silent about their sufficiency. This is a genuine restriction of scope, not an objection the essay has to eliminate.

## Final assessment and revision priorities

Clarity is already strong: the central example is understandable and the concessions protect the transformed person's present agency. The argument's main weakness is not a mistaken citation. It is the unresolved relation between actual normative power, a promise of procedural deference, and institutional recognition of a temporal verdict. Significance depends on giving that recognition a consequence. Originality remains tentative because close sovereignty and contestability arguments cover much of the general structure.

Prioritize: (1) narrow the target to the claimed procedural deference; (2) specify what a completed judgment changes in the institute's obligations or claims; (3) separate refusal from termination and later reconsideration; (4) engage Pettit and the closer Enoch passage; and (5) explain the distinctive role of transformation in selecting whose temporally located judgment receives standing. Cut repeated qualifications to make room for these arguments. No expansion toward the word ceiling is needed merely for length.

My recommendation is REVISE, not SET_ASIDE. There is a defensible insight here, especially if expressed as a constraint on appropriating a person's changing judgment for claims of procedural respect. The stronger thesis that defective closure generally defeats later ratification has not been established, and this critique preserves that disagreement.
