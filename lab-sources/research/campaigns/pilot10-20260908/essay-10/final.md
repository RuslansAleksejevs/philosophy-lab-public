# Why Innocence Does Not Close the Question

An institution may act fairly and still owe someone the means to examine whether it acted fairly. That claim is easy to accept when we suspect concealed wrongdoing. It becomes harder when the institution really did everything it was supposed to do. If its decision was correct, its explanation truthful, and everyone satisfied, what could be wrong with destroying the records needed for further examination?

The answer depends on the opportunity for examination that remains owed. Where a person retains a justified opportunity to question a decision, actual substantive compliance does not by itself permit the decision-maker to disable that opportunity. Destroying its indispensable evidence can therefore be a procedural wrong even when no false belief or subsequent material loss results. The destruction does not extinguish the person's right. It obstructs its exercise and may create a further claim against the institution.

This is an argument about the evidential conditions of procedure after successful performance. Its central distinction is between maintaining the means of inquiry, restricting an inquiry, and issuing a binding settlement. These acts require different justifications. An institution can replace evidence with an adequate equivalent without settling any dispute. Conversely, a completely accurate assessment can contribute decisive evidence without possessing authority to terminate another person's opportunity to participate. Keeping these distinctions apart explains why innocence is an insufficient justification for some deletions without making preservation an unlimited duty.

## 1. Fair allocation, premature destruction

Imagine a town distributing a finite fund for essential home repairs. Residents are entitled to fair consideration of their applications. A committee must assess need under defensible criteria, but discretion is unavoidable and applicants cannot directly follow every comparative judgment. The town's procedure gives applicants thirty days after receiving the reasons for their allocation to ask a review panel to examine a relevant issue. Pending requests receive a responsive assessment before the matter is closed. Assume that the period and access conditions are reasonable in this setting; thirty days is an illustrative arrangement, not a proposed universal standard.

The process permits an applicant to ask, for example, how a departure from the initial ranking was justified. A full hearing is not available merely on demand: the panel can reject irrelevant or repetitive requests and tailor its response to a specific question. Applicants need not establish wrongdoing before seeking clarification that could disclose a ground for challenge. The opportunity is meaningful because it permits them to respond to the committee's actual application of the criteria, something an initial application alone cannot accomplish.

A small body of records contains the applications, the criteria actually applied, and the reasons for changes in ranking. Those materials enable competent examination of favoritism and other relevant departures from the committee's obligations. The public arrangement specifies no separate duty to preserve particular records.

The committee performs the allocation impeccably. No applicant is improperly favored, no consideration improperly excluded. It then destroys the only copies of the records on the first day of the review period, although secure retention would have been easy and inexpensive. It issues a truthful summary and honestly announces that the originals are gone. Its members know that the allocation was fair and believe there is nothing wrong for review to uncover.

Suppose every applicant continues correctly and reasonably to believe that the allocation was fair. Nobody complains, nobody loses money because of the destruction, and nobody is deceived. The committee completes this single task and dissolves. We need not invoke its becoming less careful next time. Nevertheless, it has disabled an opportunity that the applicants were entitled to retain.

Now vary only the handling of evidence. In one version, the complete records are securely migrated to a new format, with their provenance, accessibility, and relevant investigative uses preserved. The obsolete copies are destroyed. In another, useful records remain available through the justified review period and any pending assessment, after which disposal is permitted. These cases should not be condemned along with the first. Physical deletion is not the underlying wrong. What matters is whether an inquiry still owed has been foreclosed.

“Foreclosed” here means made practically unavailable, not legitimately brought to an end. A negligent custodian can foreclose inquiry without claiming authority to settle anything. A committee can also falsely declare a matter closed while retaining every record. Control over evidence and authority over finality are distinct. Their interaction, rather than their identity, is what needs explanation.

## 2. An opportunity whose value survives a correct answer

One might think the town's review arrangement decides the matter by stipulation. Of course the committee should not sabotage a procedure it has undertaken to operate. But why must the procedure remain available to applicants who have already received everything substantively due to them? Perhaps its best moral interpretation contains an exception for impeccable decisions.

That exception can be stated objectively: the opportunity expires whenever all the relevant substantive obligations have actually been satisfied, regardless of what the committee believes. This is coherent. A false belief in compliance would confer no permission; a true fact could terminate the entitlement without granting the committee power to terminate it. There is no logical passage from “the right expires on compliance” to “the committee can release itself from the right.” Many normative relations have termination conditions that do not require anyone to adjudicate their satisfaction.

The objection must therefore be answered morally, not through an equivocation between compliance and self-assessment. The question is what the review opportunity protects. If its whole purpose were to increase the chance of a correct allocation in this particular case, an objectively correct allocation would remove that purpose. My disagreement is with this exhaustive account of the opportunity's value.

Applicants participate in a public arrangement that settles how claims made by them will fare. Their place in that arrangement has two aspects. Their circumstances must receive proper consideration, and they must have a reasonable opportunity to question how that consideration was given. The second aspect does not make them final judges of their entitlements. It gives them a role in the assessment of the public authority exercised over those entitlements. Their contribution can be rejected, but it must be possible for them to make it and receive an appropriate response.

This role matters because public administration is a relationship between officials and people with claims, not simply an activity that produces benefits for passive recipients. An authority can correctly identify the treatment due to someone while still excluding that person from a part of the process they are entitled to occupy. Treating a contribution as unnecessary because it would not succeed confuses its likely effect with its title to consideration.

The point can be tested by a comparison. A panel correctly anticipates every objection an applicant might raise. It can therefore prepare an excellent response in advance. That preparation may shorten the eventual exchange. It does not show that the applicant's opportunity to address the panel has already occurred. The difference is not an additional item of knowledge that the panel needs. It is whether the applicant can enter an exchange as a participant rather than have their contribution simulated by someone else. An opportunity can be supplied and declined; accurate prediction of its nonuse does not itself supply it.

There are familiar philosophical grounds for this account. Waldron connects procedural respect to the opportunity to present and confront evidence and receive a responsive assessment. Crummey directly argues that the value of fair procedures is not exhausted by their producing the correct outcome, appealing to participation and the public expression of equal citizenship. The underlying rejection of outcome sufficiency is established territory. [Waldron 2011, §5, pp. 14–15](https://philarchive.org/archive/WALTRO-67v1); [Crummey 2020, “The narrow instrumental view of fair procedures” and “Fair Procedures and Participatory Citizenship”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1468-2230.12556).

The present application concerns an initially proper decision followed by destruction of the means for an opportunity that remains open. It need not claim that every exercise of consequential discretion generates a further round of review. Some decision-makers are legitimately final; emergencies can justify procedures unavailable in ordinary administration; a proper initial hearing may sometimes provide all the participation owed. The town case instead specifies a defensible allocation of procedural roles and asks what follows for custodians of the evidence those roles require.

Nor does the argument show that a separate institution must always supply finality. The same body may be authorized both to decide and to bring a process to an end. These powers can be justified together. What does not follow is that correct performance alone settles whether the participant's procedural opportunity has been completed.

An instrumentalist can still reject this account of participation. The claim defended is that applicants have an interest in occupying an active procedural position which is not exhausted by a particular correct result. An argument that measures procedures only by that result leaves this interest unexplained; a broader account of consequences might accommodate it. Nothing here proves that all consequentialist explanations fail.

## 3. From participation to evidential custody

Recognizing an opportunity for review is not yet specifying a document-retention regime. The connection is a duty not to disable the reasonable exercise of another's procedural entitlement when the necessary means are under one's control.

The argument has four steps. First, the applicants retain an opportunity for responsive examination whose endpoint is specified independently of whether their allocation was substantively correct. Second, the records are indispensable to the meaningful exercise of that opportunity. Third, the committee controls them because of the very role whose exercise may be examined. Fourth, it can preserve an adequate route to examination without disproportionate costs or competing wrongs. Destruction before the endpoint therefore violates a duty owed to the applicants unless some further justification defeats it. Actual compliance leaves the first premise intact, for the reasons just given.

This is more specific than a general duty to improve other people's knowledge. A distant stranger might possess a useful fact without owing every applicant assistance. The committee, by contrast, controls the evidential conditions of a procedure for which its institutional role makes it responsible. Its informational advantage arises through the exercise of the authority in question. It cannot discharge that responsibility merely by leaving the review panel's formal powers untouched while destroying what the panel needs.

The duty protects an effective opportunity, not every possible evidential advantage. Its scope must be fixed by the inquiry actually owed: in the town case, examination of the criteria applied, the handling of applications, and departures from the ranking, with suitable privacy protections. An instrument is then assessed against that scope. The committee cannot call an arithmetic check “adequate” by redefining the permissible inquiry to exclude favoritism. Adequacy for an arbitrarily narrowed task is not adequacy for the applicants' entitlement.

For the same reason, a large quantity of favorable material may preserve little. A hundred testimonials to the committee's conscientiousness cannot replace the only record showing why an applicant's ranking changed. Conversely, a compact record can suffice if it retains all the distinctions an examiner is reasonably entitled to investigate. The pertinent question is whether the remaining evidence can support an assessment responsive to relevant differences in what happened.

There is no novelty in requiring useful rather than indiscriminate records. Kroll treats contextual recordkeeping and auditability as involving both evidence and governance. Cobbe, Lee, and Singh explain reviewability through information appropriate to the relevant accountability relationships, including organizational processes, and warn that recording everything can impede review. These accounts supply important antecedents for the present duty. [Kroll 2021, §§3.3, 3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.09385v1); [Cobbe, Lee, and Singh 2021, §§3.2, 4.4, 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.04201v1).

The further point concerns when custody becomes morally significant. No request must already have been made. If the evidence must be retained now for a timely request to be examinable later, waiting for the request can defeat the opportunity. The duty to keep its means available therefore precedes the duty to conduct any particular review. A threshold for commencing a detailed hearing does not automatically become a threshold for preserving its irreplaceable materials.

This distinction also allows a presumption of compliance. The committee does not owe evidence of a nonexistent violation. It owes the conditions under which a relevant question could be investigated. An inquiry can properly confirm compliance. Cen and Alur's treatment of audit evidence distinguishes the presumption chosen for a test from the evidence needed to make that test informative. That is a useful analytic comparison; the directed moral duty defended here requires the separate procedural premises above. [Cen and Alur 2024, §§5.2–5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04772v1).

## 4. Maintenance, restriction, and settlement

The temptation is now to say that every replacement of original records must be both evidentially adequate and approved by someone entitled to close the inquiry. That would be a mistake. Replacing evidence need not close anything.

Consider the complete migration described earlier. Suppose the new format preserves every investigative capability relevant to the review owed, including means of examining authenticity. Applicants and the panel retain the same access, and no deadline is shortened. Deleting the obsolete copies is ordinary evidential maintenance. It requires competent custody and the permissions appropriate to handling the materials; it does not require an additional authority to bind applicants to a final judgment. Nothing in their procedural opportunity has been curtailed.

Now consider a complete but unsolicited audit. An expert examines all the relevant materials and produces an accurate, fully inspectable report covering the legitimate scope of inquiry. The records and the means of questioning the report remain available. The report can be excellent evidence. Applicants may rely on it, and the panel may adopt its reasoning rather than repeat its work. Yet the report's correctness does not confer authority to declare that no applicant may bring a timely question. It answers the merits well without itself supplying the procedure through which those merits become settled for the participants.

This is a case of evidential success without binding finality. Its force does not depend on calling a selectively chosen audit adequate. The legitimate scope is held fixed, and the report addresses it. What is missing is not another factual question for the expert to answer, but a mandate to treat the report as the conclusion of the applicants' procedural opportunity. A panel may answer a subsequent question by directing the applicant to a conclusive passage; participation does not require a second full investigation. It requires a response through an entitled procedure.

The converse case is an appointed panel that lacks the evidence needed to perform its task. Its mandate does not make an empty assessment responsible. Appointment and evidence solve different problems. A procedure must identify someone entitled to settle the matter and enable that person to assess it adequately, but those requirements need not be attached to every intermediate act of custody.

There is a third category between maintenance and final judgment: restricting an inquiry that remains open. An institution might replace detailed materials with a summary that permits checks of eligibility but removes the means to examine ranking exceptions. That is not preservation-equivalent maintenance. If the removed questions belong to the legitimate scope, the replacement is inadequate relative to the existing entitlement. It might nonetheless be justified as a restriction—for example, because retaining particular details would seriously violate another person's privacy. But then the justification must address the restriction and the authority to impose it, rather than pretending no relevant opportunity has been lost.

This yields a practical distinction. When evidence is changed, ask first whether the opportunity already owed remains adequately exercisable. If it does, the change may be maintenance. If it does not, ask what justifies restricting that opportunity and who is entitled to implement the restriction. When a conclusion is treated as binding, ask what confers finality; its accuracy alone cannot answer that question.

The link between participation and legitimate finality is itself longstanding. In the university-repository abstract of “Procedural Justice,” Solum expressly connects a reasonable opportunity to participate with the fairness of finally binding proceedings and distinguishes participation from accuracy. The distinction here develops that established concern for a narrower purpose: separating evidence-preserving changes, inquiry-limiting changes, and the settlement of an opportunity. [Solum 2004, abstract summarizing parts V–VI](https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/881/).

An institution's possession of the records does not determine which of these acts it is performing. A software migration and an inaccessible summary may both be described as “replacing the file.” Their moral character depends on what happens to the legitimate opportunity for inquiry, not on that administrative description.

## 5. What is wrong when nothing further goes wrong?

The committee's original destruction is wrongful on this account even though no one exercises the review opportunity. That is not because an unsuccessful challenge secretly produces a benefit. It is because the entitlement includes a period during which the applicant can decide whether to use it. The opportunity is not supplied by forecasting, correctly, that they will decline.

Pettit's account of contestatory control already recognizes that a capacity can matter while unexercised and makes recorded reasons relevant to its operation. The present case removes the committee's future conduct from consideration to isolate the applicant's continuing procedural position. The duty need not be justified through a later intervention or deterrent effect on that committee. [Pettit 2006, pp. 302–304, 308–310](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf7426/files/documents/Democracy%20National%20and%20International.pdf).

A critic might say that calling the destruction wrongful without harm simply hides the harm inside “loss of an opportunity.” But the argument does not stipulate away every possible setback. It excludes false beliefs and subsequent material losses, then asks whether disabling a procedural capacity can itself matter. That is a substantive question. If the role defended in section 2 is valuable, loss of the means to occupy it is an actual change in the applicants' position. If that role has no value apart from correcting this allocation, the critic should reject the account at that earlier point.

The normative right can remain after its means are lost. Applicants may still be entitled to an account, an attempt at reconstruction, or acknowledgement that an opportunity owed to them has been made unavailable. The committee cannot acquire permission by making compliance impossible. It also need not have meant to humiliate anyone. A careless deletion can breach a duty of custody without purporting to end the relation. Intention and excuse affect blame, but the object that needed protection remains the same.

There is close prior work on erasure and accountability. Sturino argues that removing or revising published content can shield its producers from criticism and create incentives for evasion. The case examined here is narrower: no substantive failure is concealed, and the complaint arises from the impairment of a particular procedural entitlement after proper performance. This applies and refines an existing family of arguments; it does not establish priority for an ethics of record preservation. [Sturino 2025, §5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/papa.12284).

This refinement changes what counts as a remedy. Wrongful destruction is not proof that the allocation was unjust. In the example, that inference would be false. Nor does the procedural wrong automatically give every applicant the substantive result they wanted. An appropriate response concerns the lost opportunity: restore a competent inquiry where possible, accurately identify what cannot be reconstructed, and acknowledge the failure. A remedy must not manufacture substantive guilt to make the procedural wrong intelligible.

## 6. Finite opportunities and competing claims

A justified opportunity for review can end. It is not a right to eliminate every chance of error or to keep all possible challenges alive forever. Fair notice, adequate access, a reasonable period, and a responsive procedure can establish an endpoint even when further inquiry might conceivably reveal something. A valid waiver may do so earlier. The resulting finality is justified by the terms and purposes of the whole procedure, rather than by actual correctness alone.

The thirty-day case illustrates the distinction. If nobody submits a relevant request and applicants have genuinely been able to consider doing so, the opportunity has been supplied. Subject to any further obligations, the committee can then dispose of records. If it destroys indispensable materials on day one, the period has merely elapsed while its promised practical substance was absent. The calendar is not a substitute for the opportunity it measures.

General rules can govern these matters. Every applicant need not negotiate a retention period, consent to a file format, or personally appoint a reviewer. Equally, a general rule can fail to provide meaningful access if it ignores foreseeable barriers. The account requires a defensible procedure, not unanimous acceptance of every detail. Determining what makes such arrangements fully legitimate exceeds the present argument; the allocation example isolates a case in which that background is in place.

Costs and privacy can also justify limits. O’Neill's warning about monitoring that displaces professional purposes applies here: an obligation to support inquiry is not a reason to record everything, at any cost. Its scope must be proportionate to the opportunity owed and the interests it affects. [O’Neill 2002, §§3–4](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rmhttp/radio4/transcripts/20020417_reith.pdf).

If a secure summary preserves the whole legitimate scope while omitting intimate details, it may be an adequate equivalent. If no such equivalent exists, retaining the details might still create a risk serious enough to justify restricting review. The institution should then acknowledge the conflict and use a procedure entitled to resolve it. A justified restriction can leave some inquiry unavailable; not every loss is a wrong. But “we allocated fairly” neither identifies the competing claim nor shows why it should prevail.

Finally, preserving review does not require mistrust. An applicant can trust the committee and choose not to inquire. The evidential duty keeps that choice practically available for the justified period; it does not demand suspicion or repeated inspection. Institutions can be worthy of trust partly because they make reasonable scrutiny possible, while their participants remain free to rely on them without using it.

The resulting principle is modest but exact. Where a justified procedural opportunity remains open, the custodian of its indispensable evidence owes reasonable care to preserve its effective exercise. Actual substantive compliance is not an exception merely because further examination would confirm it. Equivalent maintenance is permissible without a new act of adjudication; restricting inquiry requires an adequate justification; a binding settlement requires the relevant authority and procedure. An institution's innocence can settle a substantive question. Whether another person's opportunity has been respected is a further question, and the surviving truth of the first answer does not make the second disappear.
