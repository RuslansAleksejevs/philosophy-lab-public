## Critical outcome of investigation

The broad initial suggestion is not new: meaningful accountability requires available evidence, intelligible records, and avenues for challenge. Neither targeted retention, third-party custody, latent control, process values, nor evidence capable of exposing noncompliance is a claimed discovery. Sturino is particularly close on erasure and accountability; Cen and Alur already make the formal point about detectable noncompliance and evidentiary burdens.

The remaining candidate contribution is a restricted argument about closure authority. Where the affected party retains standing to contest the exercise of consequential discretionary power, the executor cannot terminate that standing's indispensable evidential conditions solely on the strength of the executor's true judgment of successful compliance. Success satisfies the first-order duty without by itself deciding who may close an account. A criterion for permissible evidential substitution must therefore have both functional adequacy (ability to differentiate relevant failures) and legitimate authority (who can accept it and settle the scope of inquiry). This two-part distinction will do the work; no generic assertion that all opacity is domination will substitute for it.

Planned central case: a fair allocation whose decisive records are cheaply preservable; no deception, false beliefs, contract clause specifically requiring records, or subsequent material loss is needed. Compare three systems: preserved evidence with a real hearing; abundant favorable self-report with all diagnostic evidence erased; diagnostic evidence assessed by a representative who is authorized to close the issue. Seek to explain why truthful assurance alone differs from authorized closure.

Strongest objections: (1) the conclusion simply assumes a contested right to review; (2) a procedure can be prudentially valuable without an intrinsic right; (3) duty expansion threatens trust, privacy, and professional freedom; (4) endless review and regress; (5) a self-selected evidence scope can conceal what the duty was meant to expose. The manuscript must defend a limited retained-standing premise, admit its normative cost, and specify closure and substitution rather than require indefinite retention.

Novelty remains UNCERTAIN. This is a proposed philosophical refinement and derivation, not an established priority claim. Search is bounded, with archival ethics, fiduciary law, and procedural-rights literatures not comprehensively surveyed.
