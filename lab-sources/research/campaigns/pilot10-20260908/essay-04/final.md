# What Survives a Lost Appeal?

A public board charges an applicant an additional examination fee. She objects that she submitted the document establishing her exemption and asks for review. Normally, a reviewer would inspect the board's submission file. The board has negligently destroyed that file during the appeal period. The decisive receipt was held exclusively by the board; no adequate substitute remains. The applicant can establish that her objection falls within the appeal procedure, but she cannot establish the exemption without the lost material. The board replies that appellants must prove their claims. Payment remains due.

Something is wrong with that response even if the fee was probably correct. The board treats the applicant's failure to perform an evidential task as an ordinary reason to reject her challenge, although its own breach disabled that performance. Yet the error in this response does not immediately establish that the fee must be waived. A further question remains: what grounds, if any, permit collection after the ordinary appeal has become impossible?

This essay defends a principle about that transition. An institution that culpably disables an independently justified opportunity to challenge its decision loses its entitlement to rely on the resulting evidential failure as an ordinary procedural default. It owes the affected person an independent assessment of what may now be imposed and what remedy is due. That assessment must be able to alter the terms of continuation. It cannot require the person to prove that the unavailable appeal would have succeeded as a condition of considering its loss.

The conclusion is limited. It does not establish a general presumption of factual innocence, automatic reversal, or a particular award of compensation. In some cases collection with compensation can be justified. In others, the decision must be remade or the imposition withdrawn. The argument identifies a warrant that is lost and distinguishes the grounds that might replace it. This matters because a favorable presumption about a person's procedural position need not be a favorable verdict on the facts of her original claim.

## 1. A procedural default is a way of allocating uncertainty

An explanation can survive the destruction of an appeal. The board can state the fee rule, describe the correct exemption procedure, and identify the category into which the applicant was placed. It may possess compelling evidence of institutional accuracy. None of those statements necessarily restores the lost opportunity to assess whether this applicant's document was received and disregarded.

Pettit's account of democratic control supplies a background for taking this opportunity seriously. Contestatory control depends on available reasons and effective avenues of challenge, including avenues people never use. Recording official reasons can support that control. What follows develops a remedial question beyond that account: what becomes of an institution's claim to impose a decision when its breach disables an avenue of contestation? [Pettit 2005, especially p. 9.](https://iilj.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Petit-Two-Dimensional-Democracy-National-and-International-2005.pdf)

The relevant opportunity is not a right to win or to obtain certainty. A fair appeal can fail. It is an opportunity to bring relevant considerations before an adequately independent assessor, under conditions in which those considerations can change the institution's conduct. The person's standing consists partly in this capacity to constrain an imposition through reasons. A right to be told the rule is insufficient when its application is precisely what needs examination.

Ordinary procedural defaults help legitimate institutions function despite uncertainty. Requiring an appellant to identify an error and supply appropriate evidence can protect resources, other claimants, and finality. Once the appeal period ends without a successful challenge, a decision may ordinarily stand. But this is not a factual inference that every unsuccessful appellant was substantively mistaken. It is a practical rule assigning the consequences of unresolved uncertainty.

That assignment has a justification. Among its relevant conditions are the seriousness of the interests at stake, the evidence each party can obtain, and the opportunities provided to contest the decision. A default requiring the applicant to produce a document is easier to defend when the document was reliably supplied to her than when the institution alone possesses it and refuses access. The same verbal rule can allocate uncertainty fairly in one evidential setting and unfairly in another.

Two distinct facts must therefore be kept apart. It may be uncertain whether the applicant qualified for the exemption, while it is established that she has been deprived of the review to which she was entitled. Uncertainty about the first does not make the second merely speculative. Nor does it settle whether the default justified for an intact procedure remains applicable after the deprivation.

## 2. Why the institution loses the default

The central premise is a principle of fair procedural demand: where an institution's entitlement to count a person's evidential nonperformance against her depends partly on a reasonable opportunity to perform, the institution cannot invoke that nonperformance on the same terms after breaching its own obligation to sustain an indispensable part of that opportunity.

This is not a consequence of every breached duty. If a board violates a recordkeeping obligation serving an unrelated statistical purpose, its entitlement to collect a fee need not change. Nor does the premise say that officials forfeit all authority through wrongdoing. Its scope is a particular dependence between an institutional demand, the opportunity to meet it, and the adverse significance assigned to failing it.

Why accept the premise? The burden of proof expresses a division of procedural responsibility. The applicant must make her case, while the institution must supply the access or review resources on which that task fairly depends. Once the latter responsibility has been breached, simply enforcing the former no longer implements the justified division. It converts the institution's failure to sustain the agreed conditions into the applicant's failure to meet the demand. The resulting allocation needs a new justification; the original division cannot justify itself under materially changed conditions.

This argument does not require the institution to have caused the original liability. Suppose a board asks for a response to a notice, but wrongfully prevents its delivery. A rule making an unanswered notice dispositive cannot have its ordinary force merely because no response arrived. The missing response might well have been unpersuasive. The defect concerns the significance of nonresponse, not the likely truth of the board's allegation. A lost evidential route creates the same kind of problem for a default based on an unproved appeal.

A serious rival can accept the preservation duty, prohibit negligence, punish the breach, and provide independently determined compensation while maintaining the fee. This rival does not grant officials permission to destroy records. A prohibition with a monetary remedy is not thereby an option to purchase permission. It would be a mistake to reject the rival by treating every compensatory scheme as institutional self-exemption.

The principle instead asks what the rival uses to justify continued collection. If its answer is still only that the applicant failed to prove her exemption, punishment of the board does not repair the argument. A fine neither restores the evidential opportunity nor supplies a replacement justification for the ordinary allocation of uncertainty. But if an independent tribunal assesses the surviving basis of liability, the procedural loss, and the adequacy of compensation, collection can acquire a different justification. Such a rival can satisfy the principle rather than refute it.

The distinction changes the object of review. The applicant need no longer prove the original exemption before the institution must answer a remedial claim. Once she establishes the relevant breach and disability, the issue becomes whether the institution can defend the terms on which it proposes to continue. Its answer must survive assessment without assigning ordinary default force to the evidential failure it culpably caused.

For this assessment to respect the lost standing, it must be institutionally effective. The reviewer needs authority to require renewed review, change the imposition, or order an adequate remedy. An independent tribunal empowered only to condemn poor recordkeeping while forbidden to consider its consequences for this applicant's treatment is insufficient. It would leave the disputed allocation untouched in advance, regardless of what the applicant could show about the significance of the loss.

This establishes a right to reassessment, not a general right to suspension while reassessment occurs. Delay can itself impose serious burdens, so interim collection or refusal also requires justification within a fair process. A small reversible charge and an irreversible expulsion need not receive the same interim treatment. The central point is that an established disability cannot be dismissed by the very default whose applicability it challenges.

## 3. What can justify continuation?

It is tempting to require an “independent reason” for maintaining the decision. But independence of the destroyed record is insufficient. An unaffected study of institutional accuracy, a concern about revenue, and a rule protecting public safety may all be independent in that sense while doing different normative work. Three possibilities should be distinguished.

First, surviving evidence may restore an adequate assessment of the original objection. An independent receipt, reliable duplicate, or suitable testimony might permit review after all. In that case the loss of one file has not permanently disabled the relevant opportunity. The institution can vindicate its decision through a repaired procedure. The wrong of negligent destruction may still call for a response, but there is no need to treat the merits as irrecoverably unreviewable.

Second, the original question can remain unsettled while an independent remedial assessment justifies continued enforcement under that uncertainty. Here the reviewer must examine both the surviving support for liability and the fairness of leaving its practical consequences with the applicant. The institution bears the burden of showing why its proposed arrangement, including any compensation or mitigation, is an acceptable response to the established procedural loss. Producing a relevant reason is not enough: its weight and its relation to the loss must be assessed.

Take a difficult version of the fee case. A reliable external study establishes that decisions with all this application's surviving features are correct in 99.9 percent of cases. The study cannot resolve her particular objection. The fee is modest and reversible, collection serves a legitimate scheme of shared costs, no further case-specific evidence can be recovered, and an impartial tribunal can order compensation for procedural losses and expenses. On these assumptions, I would permit continued collection with an adequate independently determined remedy. High probability does not repair the appeal. It can, however, help justify the allocation adopted after its loss. Full waiver is not required merely because waiver would be affordable.

This answer concedes a real limit. The principle does not establish that probable correctness is irrelevant to remedial judgment or that every disabled challenge must change the substantive outcome. What it excludes is moving directly from “the appeal is unproved” to “the ordinary burden settles the matter.” The external evidence and the adequacy of the remedial arrangement now have work to do that the old default cannot do for them.

Compare a superficially similar case in which the institution has no surviving support for the charge apart from its own unexplained entry and the ordinary rule that appellants must disprove it. The institution's inability to justify continued collection cannot be repaired by observing that the applicant is equally unable to establish an exemption. That simply reinstates the defeated default. Depending on the broader grounds of the fee scheme, withdrawal may be required because no sufficient basis for this imposition remains. Withdrawal would not establish that the exemption was submitted.

A statistical award also needs independent assessment. Suppose a £1,000 charge had a one-percent chance of being overturned through the intact appeal. Ten pounds represents the expected monetary advantage of that chance. It does not follow that £10 compensates every relevant loss. Additional expenses, lost opportunities to participate, and other harms may matter. Conversely, recognizing those interests does not establish that only a £1,000 waiver could respect them. Independently assessed compensation can be adequate even where the original charge survives. The present argument supplies neither a universal valuation of procedural standing nor a formula for its remedy.

The third possibility concerns a different decision. Change the case to a license to operate dangerous equipment. A board loses the recording needed to review a failed examination. It cannot infer incompetence from the applicant's inability to overturn that assessment. Nevertheless, a requirement to demonstrate present competence may independently govern whether she can operate the equipment now. The board can arrange a fresh assessment at its expense while protecting her against avoidable disadvantages from the delay. It is then applying a prospective safety condition, not treating the earlier failure as vindicated.

If a fresh assessment is impossible, public safety may still justify refusal despite the unresolved procedural injury. This is an override by competing interests, not restoration of the lost appeal. The distinction matters: an override leaves something to remedy or acknowledge, whereas successful restoration can remove the continuing evidential disability. Likewise, an exceptional need to maintain revenue might justify a fee temporarily, but merely invoking revenue cannot establish its necessity or defeat less burdensome alternatives.

These possibilities explain why a presumption concerning procedure should not be collapsed into a presumption concerning facts or status. Someone can have a claim to remedial reassessment without having submitted the exemption; she can have a claim to a new examination without already being competent. What is withdrawn is the institution's ordinary privilege of treating a disabled evidential performance as the applicant's failure. What replaces it depends on the ground of the permission now being claimed.

## 4. The objection need not be known before access

The principle needs a trigger narrower than dissatisfaction but broad enough to preserve the informational function of review. One route is a specific eligible objection whose assessment depends on the lost material. The fee applicant can identify the exemption question, connect it to the missing submission record, and show why available substitutes are inadequate. She need not establish what the missing record contained.

There is also a second route. Sometimes access is owed so that a person can discover whether there is an objection. A candidate may be entitled to inspect her examination recording because scoring errors cannot reliably be identified from memory. If the board culpably deletes the sole recording before inspection, requiring her to nominate a particular credible error would demand the very knowledge access was meant to provide.

In that case eligibility for the inspection, culpable loss of its designated material, and resulting inability to obtain the relevant information can establish a procedural remedial claim. The candidate does not need a preliminary victory on the merits. The same circularity is recognized in a different context by *Osborn*: a hearing may be needed to establish a person's prospects of success, so access cannot always depend on already establishing those prospects. The judgment also connects participation with respect beyond accuracy, while its remedial discussion stops short of automatic substantive relief. [*Osborn* 2013, §§65–70, 88, 114–116.](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2011_0147_judgment_edf6183746.pdf)

This second route does not make every disappointed candidate entitled to the benefit she sought. It establishes the loss of a defined procedural opportunity and opens the assessment described above. Whether the proper response is renewed inspection, a new examination, compensation, or another remedy remains a further question. The threshold for investigating an access failure should not be confused with the threshold for conferring a substantive qualification.

Both routes require more than the disappearance of an object. The material must serve an independently justified opportunity; the institution must have breached a reasonable duty protecting that opportunity; and the breach must have caused a material disability that no adequate substitute removes. Records destroyed for an unrelated purpose do not automatically qualify. Nor does a duplicate's disappearance when the original remains equally available.

A bad-faith claimant creates a further distinction. Someone who knows she never submitted the exemption cannot acquire a moral right to have that false assertion accepted by exploiting the missing file. Her dishonesty does not make the institution's destruction permissible, and she might retain a procedural claim unrelated to that assertion. But her actual entitlements differ from those of a good-faith claimant whose case looks identical to an external reviewer. An administrable presumption may occasionally benefit a dishonest person because the reviewer cannot identify her. That is a cost of implementing a rule, not proof that the dishonest person morally deserves the windfall.

## 5. How far the argument extends

The account has close predecessors. Adams distinguishes consciousness-of-guilt reasoning from fairness of process and explicitly discusses reallocating the costs of lost evidence. Stewart proposes evidentiary access, burdens for missing evidence, and a favorable presumption where the relevant model version was not retained; he also distinguishes grounds for appeal from proof of discrimination or automatic reversal. The broad ideas of remedial allocation and nonfactual procedural consequences are therefore already available. The proposed contribution is the more specific account of a defeated default and of the different warrants under which authority can continue. [Adams 2012, pp. 138–139, 161;](https://www.templelawreview.org/lawreview/assets/uploads/2012/11/Adams_Reconsidering-Spoliation.pdf) [Stewart 2026, §5 and Appendix E.4–E.5.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.22716v1)

Legal comparisons also caution against deriving a large remedy from the intrinsic importance of procedure alone. *Carey v. Piphus* recognizes procedural deprivation independently of substantive success, while distinguishing nominal relief from compensation for consequential injury. It accepts an officials' showing that the same suspensions would have occurred. Although that damages decision does not settle irrecoverably lost review, it supplies a serious model of acknowledging procedural wrong without undoing a substantive result. [*Carey* 1978, pp. 260, 266–267.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep435/usrep435247/usrep435247.pdf)

Rule 37(e)'s 2015 notes likewise distinguish negligent loss from a factual inference that destroyed evidence was unfavorable. They also caution that an ostensibly curative measure cannot simply reproduce sanctions requiring intent. A waiver cannot escape questions of severity merely by being described as a restriction on authority. My principle determines the need and object of remedial reassessment; it does not settle the proportionality of every remedy or supply an alternative statement of the law. [Rule 37(e), 2015 Committee Notes, subdivisions (e)(1)–(2).](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_37)

The account is also incomplete about innocent loss. Compare identical disabled appeals after, respectively, reasonable precautions and an employee's negligent bypassing of them. In both cases the institution controlled the evidence; in both the applicant may require protection. An institution might owe a fresh assessment regardless of fault. Nothing here assigns accidental loss automatically to her.

Fault adds a more specific reason: the institution failed to discharge its side of the division of procedural responsibility that supported the default it now invokes. In the innocent case, that reason is absent, although other grounds can defeat the default. Thus culpability is sufficient for the particular objection defended here, not necessary for every remedial duty. It need not determine the entire allocation of costs.

Causal responsibility is distinct from fault. The argument assumes that compliance with the preservation duty would have maintained an adequate opportunity. If negligent disposal immediately preceded an unavoidable event that would have destroyed every usable copy anyway, there was a wrong, but its responsibility for the lost opportunity is more difficult to establish. The account does not resolve such cases of redundant causes by labeling the uncertainty culpable.

Finally, none of this demands exhaustive documentation. Preservation duties must reflect foreseeable review needs, privacy, resources, and available substitutes. Returning a reliable receipt, retaining a limited decision record, or arranging independent custody may suffice. O'Neill's critique of indiscriminate reporting is pertinent: accountability can obstruct substantive work when documentation becomes its own end. Informed assessment is a better aim than accumulating records without regard to what review requires. [O'Neill 2002, §§3–4.](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rmhttp/radio4/transcripts/20020417_reith.pdf)

These limits must be fair to both sides. As Ross argues concerning evidential gaps, concentrating only on wrongful findings against defendants can conceal the injustice of leaving harmed parties without redress. Yet reversing that partiality would be no improvement. The point of independent reassessment is to consider the established procedural loss together with the surviving grounds for an imposition and the interests it protects. [Ross 2021, §§2–3.](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/113361/1/Philosophical_Issues_L_Ross_Paper_Final_Version.pdf)

## 6. The continuing decision

After a lost appeal, three questions remain distinct: was the original decision correct, was an opportunity to challenge it wrongfully disabled, and what may the institution now do? The second can be established while the first remains uncertain. Establishing it changes how the third must be answered.

The institution must first seek adequate restoration. If restoration fails, it owes an effective independent assessment of the terms of continuation, without requiring proof of success in the lost appeal as the price of entry. Continued enforcement with compensation may be justified; fresh assessment or withdrawal may instead be required. A separate prospective condition or sufficiently weighty competing interest can also authorize conduct without vindicating the original decision.

The result is a limited defeat of authority, but not merely a correction to official language. It removes a procedural privilege, changes which party must answer for the remaining allocation, and gives the affected person standing to obtain a decision capable of altering it. “You have not proved your appeal” cannot retain its ordinary force when the authority has culpably destroyed the opportunity on which that demand depended.
