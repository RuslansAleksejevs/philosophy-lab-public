# Independent critique: The Burden of a Lost Appeal

AI critic: `/root/critic_04`. Dated 2026-09-08 UTC. Position on the central proposal: TENTATIVE. Argument status: UNDER_CRITICISM. This criticism concerns only essay 04. It is neither a simulated competition verdict nor a certification of originality, truth, or eligibility.

## Overall assessment

The essay has a defensible central distinction and a worthwhile target: an institution's culpable destruction of a fair appeal can affect what it may now require without making the original adverse decision less likely to be true. The writing is clear, the preservation duty has sensible limits, and the author appropriately acknowledges close predecessors. The fee/licensing contrast helps prevent an obvious confusion between repairing a wrong and manufacturing competence.

The manuscript presently establishes its weakest conclusion more convincingly than its strongest one. It explains why the institution owes a fresh justification and cannot present an impossible appeal as a completed merits review. It does not yet adequately establish when this defeats enforcement, what counts as an independent justification for enforcement, or why compensation determined by an independent reviewer is insufficient. Those are central philosophical issues, not requests for administrative detail. A substantial revision should make one answer to them the contribution.

## Reconstruction in its strongest form

The argument is best understood as a proposed defeater of a particular practical warrant:

1. An ordinary rule placing the burden on an appellant is justified partly by the fair opportunity the procedure gives her to meet that burden.
2. A preservation obligation can protect an indispensable condition of that opportunity.
3. When the institution breaches that obligation and thereby disables assessment of an eligible objection, the claimant's subsequent evidential failure no longer occurs under the conditions that justified the ordinary allocation of uncertainty.
4. The institution therefore cannot establish its present entitlement to continue the imposition simply by invoking that failure.
5. It must restore the opportunity, provide an adequate remedy, or identify sufficient grounds for continuation which survive the defeat of the ordinary default.

This is a plausible argument about the normative significance of an evidential failure. It does not entail a changed probability of the underlying allegation. It also does not, without further premises, entail a general presumption of reversal, a particular quantum of compensation, or a right to receive the disputed license. The strongest reading should preserve these limits.

Two transitions need defense: why culpable failure of an enabling obligation defeats the default rather than merely generating a separate claim; and how the withdrawal of this warrant translates into actual relief when another ordinary justification for the decision remains available.

## 1. A breached duty is not a reserved permission to breach

Section 2 asks us to imagine a board announcing that it reserves freedom to destroy records negligently while keeping the burden of proof. That arrangement is plainly objectionable, but it is stronger than the serious rival to the essay. The rival can prohibit negligent destruction, fund independent enforcement of the preservation duty, compensate its victims, and nevertheless hold that a substantive fee obligation normally survives. It grants no permission to destroy anything.

Likewise, the examination service need not declare that a score survived review. It can record that the score remains unreviewed because of its own wrong while maintaining that an initially credible assessment remains sufficient for some purposes. Its unwillingness to pretend otherwise does not decide what the candidate is owed. The passage moves too quickly from a sound objection to misleading institutional language to a conclusion about the force of the underlying default.

The purchase-away objection in §5 also needs this stronger opponent. Imagine the compensatory remedy is determined by an independent tribunal under rules the board cannot change, carries meaningful remedial and disciplinary costs, and expressly treats the breach as a wrong. The board has no unilateral option to price its conduct. Why must the claimant nevertheless receive relief from the fee itself? A prohibition with a monetary remedy does not become a permission merely because the original transaction survives it.

The needed bridge might be a principle that an authority may count nonperformance of a procedural demand against a person only if it has fulfilled its own reasonable obligations enabling that performance. But this is a substantive principle of fair allocation, not something contained in the bare concept of a duty. Defend it directly. Explain why assigning the specific risk back to the person would make her procedural standing dependent on the very party it is supposed to constrain, and why a separately adjudicated remedy does or does not remove that dependence. Treat this as the contested premise rather than a consequence of calling the loss culpable.

## 2. Define the independent justification; it currently threatens to absorb the result

The manuscript begins by allowing strong independent evidence that the original charge was correct. It later makes waiver appropriate when no independent justification remains beyond the default. These claims are compatible, but the difference between their two uses of independence is not specified.

Consider a fixed variant of the fee case. An external, reliable study establishes that decisions with all the surviving features of this application are correct in 99.9 percent of cases. The study is unaffected by the board's negligence. The missing record nevertheless could have resolved the particular credible objection; no substitute hearing can do that. Collection has modest administrative benefits and waiver would cause no serious third-party harm. Does the study restore the board's entitlement to collect?

If yes, the introductory accuracy stipulation does not show that practical relief is owed despite likely correctness. The result may reduce to an obligation to give the correct explanation for continuing the same imposition. If no, explain why strong evidence of the substantive liability cannot do the work, even though protection against dangerous operation can independently justify continued refusal of a license. The answer cannot merely be that one is independent: both considerations can be independent of the destroyed file. The relevant difference might concern what kind of permission is being justified and whether exercising it makes the person bear the specific procedural deficit. That needs an argument.

Distinguish at least three possibilities. Surviving evidence may make the original objection adequately assessable, so the trigger fails. A separate present condition, such as demonstrated safe competence, may independently govern future action while leaving the original adverse assessment unvindicated. Finally, sufficiently weighty public interests may justify maintaining an imposition despite an unresolved procedural wrong. These are different ways the institution can prevail. The last is an override and should not be confused with restoration of the lost warrant.

The manuscript should decide what happens in the fixed fee variant, identify which consideration decides it, and keep that answer through the conclusion. Otherwise almost any decision can be maintained by restating familiar goals such as accuracy, administrative economy, equal treatment, or revenue as independent reasons.

## 3. A justificatory burden and a presumption of relief are different claims

The thesis in §2 withdraws one warrant. The conclusion adds an order to give proportionate relief unless independent reasons justify maintaining the imposition. This can be read as imposing a new practical default. Yet no standard is given for the institution's new burden: production of a relevant reason, a convincing explanation, proof of the original result, or justification that the remaining allocation is the least unfair available?

This matters even with completely established procedural loss. Suppose the irrecoverable appeal concerned a £1,000 fee, and independent information puts the chance of a successful intact appeal at one percent. An impartial tribunal proposes £10 for the lost chance, reimbursement of procedural expenses, and a separately justified payment for the loss of procedural standing. The fee otherwise remains enforceable. Is this an adequate recognition of the lost opportunity, or does the claimant presumptively deserve full waiver? Neither answer follows from the fact that the lost opportunity is valuable independently of eventual success.

The numbers are an original counterexample, not a suggested legal formula. They isolate two objects of compensation: a probabilistic material advantage and an impaired relation of authority. If the latter cannot be adequately purchased, explain what makes it require relief from this imposition. If it can, the essay must admit that an independently assessed compensatory response may leave collection fully justified. That can still be a worthwhile result, but it is less than the rhetoric of losing entitlement may suggest.

Clarify whether the proposed principle gives the claimant a right to a new decision about continuation, a temporary suspension during that assessment, or a presumptive reduction of the burden afterward. A reason-giving duty alone can leave outcomes unchanged; a suspension or remission rule requires a further account of priority and proportionality.

## 4. The credible-objection threshold has a serious underinclusion case

Section 4 rightly distinguishes evidence of a disabled route from evidence of a successful underlying claim. Its surviving-indication example is still unusually favorable. Sometimes the missing record is needed to identify the defect itself.

Imagine a candidate who can establish that her examination occurred and that its sole recording was culpably deleted. An independently justified appeal procedure lets candidates inspect recordings because scoring errors often cannot be identified from memory. She has no surviving indication of a particular error precisely because she never obtained that inspection. Requiring her to nominate a specific credible scoring mistake would make the missing record block the remedial claim as well as the original challenge. The appeal right's informational function would disappear.

The draft already gestures toward adjustment for unavailable material and distinguishes inquiry from final relief. Develop that into an explicit alternative route: evidence of an affected decision, a duty to retain the designated review material, its culpable loss, and the informational purpose of access may justify investigation or a procedural remedy even without a preidentified merits defect. Then distinguish the threshold for that remedy from the threshold for waiving a substantial fee. Eligibility for an independently grounded inspection right should not silently become probable entitlement to the substantive result.

Conversely, clarify treatment of a claimant who knows her factual assertion is false but exploits the missing record. The institution's breach is real; the claimant's own right to relief may nevertheless depend on good faith even where an external reviewer cannot reliably detect bad faith. Distinguish the moral entitlement from administrable presumptions designed to identify it.

## 5. Fault, control, and causation need one more boundary test

The essay is careful to present culpability as sufficient rather than necessary, so innocent loss is not a direct refutation. Still, its public-authority rationale emphasizes control and dependence, properties that survive reasonable accidental loss. Why should fault matter specifically to withdrawal of the default, rather than to the size and distribution of additional remedial costs?

A short paired comparison would help. In both versions, the institution alone held the record and the same eligible appeal is now impossible. In one, storage met reasonable precautions; in the other, an employee negligently bypassed them. Name the additional reason introduced by the breach, while leaving open whatever strict procedural assurance the institution might already owe. Do not imply that prospective fairness automatically allocates accidental loss to the claimant: that allocation itself needs justification.

Also separate breach from causal responsibility for disability. If negligent disposal occurred just before an unavoidable event that would independently have destroyed every usable copy, there is a preservation wrong, but the relevant counterfactual loss of opportunity is harder to attribute to it. The thesis may reasonably bracket overdetermination; it should not make a broad claim about culpably created uncertainty without stating the causal assumption. No elaborate theory of causation is needed for this essay.

The cited passages generally support the claims made of them. I found no load-bearing fabricated source or clear misquotation in the draft. The most useful changes concern argumentative engagement and precision rather than replacement of the bibliography.

- O'Neill's §4 supports the qualification concerning informed assessment and excessive standardized reporting. Ross's §§2–3 support the warning about one-sided attention to epistemic risk. Neither settles the proposed remedial allocation. [O'Neill, transcript](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rmhttp/radio4/transcripts/20020417_reith.pdf); [Ross, manuscript](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/113361/1/Philosophical_Issues_L_Ross_Paper_Final_Version.pdf).
- Adams pp. 138–139 are accurately represented. Read the closer discussion at p. 161, “Fairness of Process and Allocation of Losses,” before characterizing the remaining novelty: the later passage already treats sanctions as reallocating the costs of evidential destruction. [Adams, journal article](https://www.templelawreview.org/lawreview/assets/uploads/2012/11/Adams_Reconsidering-Spoliation.pdf).
- Rule 37(e) is accurately described, but its 2015 note on (e)(1) adds a pointed complication: a nominally curative measure cannot simply reproduce the effect of a severe sanction without the required intent. Waiving a claim may resemble precisely such an effect. The philosophical proposal can diverge, but distinguishing truth from authority alone does not answer the proportionality objection. [Rule and Committee Notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_37).
- Stewart's §5 and Appendix E.5 are correctly cited. However, §5 and E.4 already distinguish triggering appeal and justification from automatically proving discrimination or reversing a decision. Do not suggest that the nearby theory treats every favorable presumption as a factual verdict. The narrower unresolved comparison is the effect of permanently unavailable evidence on continuing authority. [Stewart, version consulted](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.22716v1).

Two further primary comparisons sharpen the problem. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 260, 266–267 (1978), recognizes a procedural violation independently of substantive success while distinguishing nominal relief from compensation for consequential injury. It also accepts an officials' showing that the same suspensions would have occurred. This supports the strongest remedial rival, though its damages question does not settle the essay's case of irrecoverably lost review. [Official opinion](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep435/usrep435247/usrep435247.pdf).

*Osborn v The Parole Board*, [2013] UKSC 61, §§65–70, grounds fair participation partly in respect rather than improved accuracy. But §§114–116 do not derive automatic substantive relief from that recognition. This is a close predecessor to the essay's relational motivation and a reminder that the motivation leaves remedial work undone. It concerns hearings, not evidential destruction. [Official judgment](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2011_0147_judgment_edf6183746.pdf).

## Originality and significance

The broad ideas are familiar: procedural wrong without substantive error, cost allocation after destroyed evidence, effective contestability, and remedies other than an adverse factual inference. The author acknowledges much of this. Adding a new name to their intersection would not yet establish an original contribution.

There is a more promising contribution in specifying exactly which default an authority loses and separating three reasons it might nonetheless continue to impose a burden. This could explain an apparent disagreement between strong procedural rights and cautious substantive remedies. At present the distinction is an illuminating proposal, but its substantive implications are underdetermined. A clear account that survives the independent-compensation and high-accuracy counterexamples would be more significant than another general defense of meaningful appeals.

A targeted search also located the author-deposited abstract for Solum and Marzen's *Truth and Uncertainty: Legal Control of the Destruction of Evidence*. Full-text access failed, so I cannot assess its anticipation of the precise proposal or vouch for the displayed bibliographic dates. It is a serious further lead, not a work this critique claims to have read. [SSRN record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2118021). A short search cannot establish novelty. The primary decisions above supply comparisons, not a universal legal or moral theory.

## Recommended revision priorities

1. Choose the practical result to defend: exclusion of the old default plus independent reassessment of continuation, or a stronger presumption of relief. If the stronger result is retained, state and defend the additional premise that supplies it.
2. Replace the reserved-permission analogy with the strongest rival: prohibited negligence, independently adjudicated compensation, and continued collection. Explain precisely why that response succeeds or fails.
3. Resolve the fixed high-accuracy fee case. Define independence in terms of the permission being justified, distinguish repair from override, and specify who bears unresolved practical uncertainty.
4. Make the information-discovery function of appeal part of the trigger. Explain why credible access claims sometimes suffice without independently identifiable merits defects, while separating these from costly substantive relief.
5. Engage the closest predecessor passages and reduce novelty claims accordingly. The essay need not grow substantially: repeated explanations that relief is not a factual finding can be shortened to make room for the missing argument.

A defensible narrower conclusion would be that culpable disabling of review defeats the ordinary inference from an unproved appeal to a settled entitlement, thereby requiring independent reassessment of the terms of continuation. Some cases will end in waiver, others in restored review or justified continuation with a separate remedy. If that is the selected position, the revision should be candid that the principle alone does not determine which substantive remedy is due.
