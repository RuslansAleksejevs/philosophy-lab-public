# Question — essay 04

When an institution exercises power over a person and then culpably destroys the evidence needed to challenge that exercise, what follows morally from the resulting uncertainty?

Tentative thesis: duties of justification and contestability require more than explaining a decision when challenged. In suitable cases they allocate the risk of a later, institution-created inability to determine whether a challenge is sound. An authority that culpably disables the specified route of challenge should lose a rebuttable entitlement to maintain its decision, without anyone needing to infer that the decision was substantively wrong. This is a claim about remedial standing under uncertainty, not permission to believe a false factual proposition and not a general duty to record every thought.

The essay will distinguish three questions: whether the original outcome was correct; whether the original exercise of power was permissible; and who may insist on its continuation when a fair challenge has been made impossible. The potential contribution is a defended connection between prospective contestability and retrospective allocation of uncertainty, with limiting conditions that avoid an unlimited archival duty or automatic vindication of frivolous objections.

Initial example to develop: a public credentialing body denies a qualification, retains the outcome, and destroys the material required by its own appeal procedure. Later, an applicant presents a specific credible challenge. Accurate population-level evidence suggests that almost all original decisions were correct, but the individual case cannot be checked. Compare an otherwise identical case in which the records were lost despite reasonable precautions.
