# The Burden of a Lost Appeal

A public board orders an applicant to pay an additional examination fee. The applicant objects: she submitted the document establishing her exemption, but the board appears to have overlooked it. Normally, a reviewer would inspect the submission file. The board has negligently destroyed that file, although it was required to retain it through the appeal period. The applicant cannot replace it because the relevant receipt was generated and held exclusively by the board. She has enough evidence to make the objection credible, but not enough to establish the exemption without the lost record. The board replies that its assessments are overwhelmingly accurate and that appellants must prove their claims. Payment remains due.

Something has gone wrong beyond the destruction of a useful document. The board is relying on the applicant's failure to do what its own breach has disabled her from doing. Yet identifying that wrong does not require believing that she submitted the exemption. We can stipulate that independent evidence makes it highly probable that the fee was correctly imposed. Her claim against the board's present response may nevertheless be sound.

This essay defends a limited principle about authority under uncertainty. When an institution culpably destroys a person's effective opportunity to pursue an eligible challenge, it loses its ordinary entitlement to maintain the challenged decision merely because that person cannot discharge the evidential burden the destruction has made impossible. It must restore an adequate route of challenge, provide proportionate relief from the decision, or establish an independent justification for maintaining the burden. This is a principle about the authority to continue imposing a decision, rather than an instruction to find the original decision false.

The proposal develops familiar ideas about contestation and lost evidence. Its contribution is to identify the object of the resulting presumption: the institution's entitlement to rely on an ordinary procedural default. Clarifying that object explains both why relief can be owed without an adverse factual inference and why such relief need not confer the substantive status the claimant originally sought.

## 1. What the missing record removes

An explanation can survive the destruction of a challenge. The board can explain that applicants without an exemption owe the additional fee. It can describe the correct standards, identify the category into which the applicant was placed, and report an excellent record of accuracy. None of these statements enables a reviewer to determine whether this applicant's document was received and disregarded.

Pettit's account of democratic control provides a useful starting point. In its contestatory dimension, control requires opportunities to challenge official action, supported by publicly available reasons and independent avenues of review. The availability of those avenues matters even when citizens do not use them. He expressly identifies recording official reasons as one possible support for contestation. My concern is a subsequent question: what happens to an institution's claim to obedience when it defeats an avenue on which the legitimacy of its response to an objection depends? [Pettit 2005, pp. 8–10.](https://iilj.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Petit-Two-Dimensional-Democracy-National-and-International-2005.pdf)

An opportunity to challenge has several components. Someone must be entitled to raise a relevant objection; an adequately independent reviewer must be able to assess it; and an assessment in the objector's favor must be capable of changing the institution's conduct. A complaint box with no responsive decision is insufficient. So is an impartial reviewer who is required to reject every objection whose proof the institution has destroyed.

The relevant opportunity is not an entitlement to win. The applicant can receive everything she is owed and lose the appeal. Nor is it an entitlement to certainty. Witnesses forget, records are ambiguous, and legitimate procedures use standards of proof. What she is owed is access, on fair terms, to a procedure in which the merits of an eligible objection can make a practical difference. Destroying that access is a distinct injury even if an intact procedure would probably have rejected the objection.

Consider two propositions: the applicant lacked an exemption; and the applicant no longer has the opportunity to obtain the promised assessment of her exemption claim. Evidence may make the first highly probable while establishing the second conclusively. Uncertainty about the original fee does not extend automatically to uncertainty about the lost appeal. We should resist allowing one uncertainty to conceal a different, established wrong.

The distinction also identifies what requires preservation. It is whatever reasonably sustains the specified route of challenge, not a complete record of institutional life. An adequate duplicate would suffice. So might a reliable independent receipt or an authorized procedure for establishing submission by testimony. If the decision can still be assessed fairly, destruction of one file does not activate the principle. Its concern is the disabling of challenge, not the disappearance of an object as such.

## 2. From a preservation duty to a remedial burden

The existence of a preservation duty does not, by itself, settle its remedy. A critic can accept that the board acted wrongly while arguing that it should pay a fine, improve its records, and enforce the fee. To reach the further conclusion, we need to explain why the breach changes the board's relation to this applicant.

Begin with the ordinary appellant's burden. Institutions need not reopen settled decisions whenever someone merely dislikes them. A requirement to identify a relevant defect and provide appropriate support can protect public resources, other claimants, and legitimate finality. Such a requirement distributes the risk of residual uncertainty. If the applicant cannot establish an exemption, the ordinary rule leaves the cost of that uncertainty with her.

But the justification of that distribution cannot be assessed apart from the opportunities each side has to supply the required evidence. Suppose the board announced in advance: applicants must prove timely submission; we alone will hold the decisive receipt; and we reserve the freedom to destroy it negligently while retaining the same proof requirement. This is not simply a demanding evidential standard. It gives the board unilateral practical control over whether a person's procedural entitlement can constrain it. A nominal right to appeal then coexists with a permission for its duty bearer to disable its exercise without changing the authoritative result.

The objection is not that every rule producing unequal chances is impermissible. An applicant may properly bear the burden of information she can obtain more readily than the board. Nor must a right guarantee successful exercise under every disaster. The objection is that a default justified as part of a fair procedure is being invoked after the institution's breach has removed a material condition of that procedure. The institution cannot justify this invocation simply by repeating the rule that applied before its breach.

A private analogy isolates the point. Suppose an examination service undertakes to let a candidate challenge an allegedly incorrect score through a recheck, retains the only answer sheet, and carelessly destroys it. A refund of the small recheck fee does not automatically entitle the service to declare that the score has survived review. It has not survived review. The default that an uncorrected score stands can no longer acquire its ordinary force from the candidate's inability to demonstrate an error using the service's own missing material. Whether the service owes a new examination, a refund, or some other remedy depends on what it undertook and what is feasible. Its first answer cannot be that the candidate failed the very evidential task it disabled.

Public authority strengthens this argument. The person ordinarily cannot escape its decision by selecting a competing institution. She is also entitled to treatment as someone whose relevant objections can constrain the terms imposed on her. Where the institution controls indispensable evidential resources, that entitlement carries a corresponding restriction on what the institution may do with the uncertainty resulting from its breach. Otherwise the right is protected only while its duty bearer happens to comply.

The resulting principle has four conditions. There must be an independently justified opportunity to challenge, not merely a review mechanism the institution happens to advertise. The objection must fall within that opportunity and be raised on fair terms. The institution must have breached a reasonable duty whose function included maintaining the relevant evidential route. And that breach must have materially disabled assessment of the objection, with no adequate substitute available.

When these conditions hold, the institution bears a remedial burden: it must answer for its proposed continuation of the decision without treating the disabled performance as an ordinary failure by the claimant. Restoring the opportunity is normally the first response. If restoration is impossible, the board must give a justification for the allocation of the remaining practical costs that recognizes its own breach and the claimant's lost power of challenge. The original default alone cannot provide it.

This conclusion is deliberately narrower than automatic reversal. It withdraws one warrant for continuing the decision. It does not deny that another warrant might exist. Nor does every procedural fault remove that warrant: a missing irrelevant attachment, a harmless clerical error, or the destruction of material available in an equally useful form elsewhere fails the materiality condition.

## 3. A presumption about authority, not truth

The clearest temptation is to convert the remedial burden into an inference about the lost file. Perhaps we should assume that it contained the exemption. This may be appropriate under some evidential rules, but it is not the principle defended here.

The difference already has important predecessors. Adams distinguishes two bases of spoliation doctrine: an inference from destruction to consciousness of guilt, and protection of fair process. Thus neither remedies for lost evidence nor their separation from guilt inference are new ideas. The present argument specifies a further distinction within the latter family: a restriction on maintaining one's own authoritative decision differs from a finding about what the missing evidence would have shown. [Adams 2012, pp. 138–139.](https://www.templelawreview.org/lawreview/assets/uploads/2012/11/Adams_Reconsidering-Spoliation.pdf)

The 2015 notes to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) make an especially useful comparison. They explain that negligent destruction does not logically imply that the lost information was unfavorable. The rule distinguishes curative measures from specified severe measures requiring an intent to deprive. These provisions do not establish my proposed moral principle, and I am not offering an interpretation that overrides their limits. They illustrate the need to keep the evidential meaning of destruction separate from the appropriate response to resulting prejudice. [Rule 37(e), 2015 Committee Notes.](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_37)

Imagine that the board's negligent deletion selected files randomly. Knowing that a file was deleted adds no evidence that its decision was wrong. Nevertheless, the board has removed the applicant's route to a fair hearing. A reviewer can therefore consistently say: “The fee was probably correctly assessed; the board may not collect it solely on the ground that this appeal remains unproved.” No contradiction is involved. The first statement concerns the probability of a substantive condition. The second concerns a present permission to act.

This distinction is more exact than a blanket instruction to presume in favor of the affected person. Stewart's recent proposal for evidentiary rights in algorithmic decision-making includes access to the version that made a decision, shifting burdens when evidence is missing, and a favorable presumption if the original version was not retained. Those proposals directly anticipate much of the institutional territory at issue here. The question that remains is what a favorable presumption should favor. It could concern a factual allegation, eligibility for a benefit, or relief from an institution's continued reliance on an unreviewable decision. These objects can come apart. [Stewart 2026, §5 and Appendix E.5.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.22716v1)

Change the example to a license to operate dangerous equipment. A board negligently loses a failed applicant's examination recording. The applicant has a credible objection about the assessment. It would be a mistake to conclude from the lost recording that she is competent and must receive a license. The licensing requirement protects other people as well as the board's administrative interests. A new examination at the board's expense, expedited access, and protection against avoidable disadvantages caused by the delay may be the proper response. The board cannot treat the original failure as conclusively established through an appeal the applicant was unable to pursue. But it can require independently adequate evidence of present competence.

Now return to the additional fee. Suppose no substitute assessment is possible, and there is no independent justification for collection beyond the ordinary rule that an unproved appeal leaves the fee standing. Waiver can be appropriate without a finding that the exemption was submitted. The board can accurately record the outcome as relief for loss of a fair appeal. It should not record the applicant as factually vindicated. This is no more incoherent than refunding a service that failed to provide an agreed check without declaring that the check would have favored the customer.

The license and the fee differ because the disputed substantive statuses have different grounds. Nobody becomes competent through another person's recordkeeping breach. But a board's entitlement to collect a particular fee can depend on whether it has met the conditions governing that imposition. A moral theory of remedies should track these grounds instead of mechanically transferring uncertainty into an artificial factual verdict.

## 4. What must the applicant show?

An obvious difficulty concerns the principle's trigger. If the lost evidence was needed to prove the objection, how can the claimant prove that its loss mattered? A demanding requirement would recreate the problem; an undemanding one would invite opportunistic claims.

The solution is to distinguish proof that an objection is sound from proof that a route for assessing it has been disabled. To show the latter, the claimant need not establish what the record contained. She must identify an objection that would matter under the governing standards and connect it to material the institution should have kept. The kind of record, its normal function, the timing of its destruction, and the absence of substitutes can establish that connection without establishing the underlying claim.

Our applicant points to a particular exemption procedure, some surviving indication of her participation in it, and a submission log designed to settle disputes of exactly this kind. A person who merely asserts that an unspecified missing file might reveal any conceivable irregularity has not made an equivalent showing. Likewise, if an independently preserved entry conclusively shows that the alleged submission occurred after the deadline, the lost file may no longer prevent adequate assessment of the timely-submission objection.

This threshold is not a demand for a preliminary miniature victory. Requiring evidence of likely success would protect only people fortunate enough to possess substitutes for the destroyed route. An opportunity to challenge is sometimes valuable precisely because the individual cannot know whether her suspicion is sound until the material is inspected. The threshold should therefore concern the relevance and credibility of the proposed challenge, adjusted for what the institution's breach has made unavailable. It should not covertly demand proof of the unavailable fact.

Who judges that threshold matters. Allowing the original board unreviewable discretion to decide that its own missing evidence was immaterial would reproduce the control the remedy addresses. An independent reviewer must assess both the evidential connection and the adequacy of any substitute. This requirement does not entail endless appeals: the reviewer's remit and the period for invoking it can be settled by fair institutional rules. It requires an assessment that can count against the board, rather than the board's repetition of its initial conclusion.

One should also separate access to investigation from ultimate relief. It may be reasonable to open an inquiry on a relatively modest showing, while withholding a costly substantive remedy until the inquiry establishes the preservation breach and resulting disability. There is no reason to make every procedural stage carry the same burden of proof. The applicant's inability to establish her original exemption does not excuse a failure to establish the distinct conditions of the remedial claim.

## 5. Accuracy, costs, and innocent loss

The strongest objection is that this proposal sacrifices accurate decisions for procedural symbolism. If the board is almost certainly right, why waive a justified charge or spend scarce resources on reassessment? Would it not be better to enforce the fee and penalize negligent recordkeeping separately?

Part of the answer is that penalties and institutional reform are directed at different objects. They can deter future breaches and acknowledge an established wrong. They do not themselves restore this person's ability to change the terms imposed on her through an eligible challenge. If restoration is practicable, replacing it with a fine paid elsewhere leaves the particular relation of authority intact on the very terms the institution has wrongfully rendered unchallengeable.

That relation need not disappear merely because money changes hands. Compensation can sometimes form an adequate remedy, especially when the original decision is irreversible. But its adequacy must be justified in relation to the lost opportunity and resulting burdens. An institution cannot establish that adequacy just by assigning a standard price to its own failure. The right to challenge would otherwise become a right the duty bearer can unilaterally purchase away while enforcing the disputed decision.

Nor is the alternative epistemically neutral. As Ross argues in a different setting, rules that withhold a finding under evidential uncertainty can systematically deny redress to harmed parties. Concern about wrongful findings against one side does not settle what should happen to the other. My case is narrower than his defense of some uses of statistical evidence: the preservation breach and loss of challenge can be established even while the original decision remains doubtful. [Ross 2021, §§2–3.](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/113361/1/Philosophical_Issues_L_Ross_Paper_Final_Version.pdf)

Still, the right is not an absolute priority over every competing interest. A new examination could be prohibitively expensive; waiving a fee could imperil an essential service; a license could endanger third parties. The remedial burden requires attention to these facts. It does not require an institution to make every procedural injury disappear regardless of cost.

The key is to distinguish an independent reason for maintaining an imposition from repetition of the disabled default. In the dangerous-equipment case, protection of others supplies a reason why the applicant must establish present competence. The board should satisfy that requirement through a fresh and fair assessment while absorbing the avoidable costs of its breach. Where even that is impossible, continued refusal may be justified, but the institution still owes an account of the unresolved procedural injury and appropriate mitigation. It should not claim that the original adverse assessment has thereby been vindicated.

A different worry is bureaucratic excess. Officials cannot preserve every utterance against every possible future complaint. O'Neill's criticism of indiscriminate accountability requirements is pertinent: exhaustive reporting can obstruct the work institutions are meant to perform. Her alternative emphasizes knowledgeable independent assessment rather than the multiplication of standardized indicators. The proposed principle is compatible with that objection because its preservation duties are defined by reasonable routes of review, not by an aspiration to total documentation. [O'Neill 2002, §§3–4.](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rmhttp/radio4/transcripts/20020417_reith.pdf)

Reasonableness must be specified prospectively enough to guide action. The importance and reversibility of decisions, the foreseeable objections, the availability of substitutes, privacy, and preservation costs all matter. An institution can sometimes satisfy the duty by returning a reliable receipt to the person, retaining a limited decision record, or arranging independent custody. Keeping more personal data is not always better. A genuinely adequate alternative preserves the relevant opportunity while reducing burdens or intrusion.

These limits also prevent hindsight from converting every unexpected dispute into a preservation violation. An obscure objection raised long after a fair appeal period may not concern material the institution ever had a duty to retain. Conversely, a board cannot justify a deliberately inadequate period merely by announcing it. The fairness of the opportunity is an independent condition, not whatever the institution writes into its policy.

Finally, consider innocent loss: the same record disappears despite all reasonable precautions. The person may still need help, and some institutional commitments may guarantee another assessment regardless of fault. The principle defended here does not imply otherwise. It identifies a sufficient reason for withdrawing reliance on an ordinary default, not a complete theory of every failed appeal. In the innocent case, the allocation of unavoidable uncertainty depends on the fairly justified terms of the procedure and the interests it protects. In the culpable case, the institution cannot treat the uncertainty as merely the ordinary risk those terms assigned to the applicant. Its own breach has changed the basis for that assignment.

## 6. Continuing power after a failed challenge

The morally important document in these examples is not simply a container of truth. It helps determine whether a person can constrain an institution through reasons. Losing it can therefore change the moral basis on which the institution continues to act, even when it does not change anyone's rational estimate of the original decision's accuracy.

The appropriate response has a definite order. Establish whether a reasonable opportunity to challenge has been materially disabled by the institution's breach. Seek an adequate substitute or a fresh assessment. If neither is available, justify the remaining practical burden without treating the claimant's disabled evidential performance as an ordinary failure. Give proportionate relief unless independent reasons justify maintaining the imposition, and address the unresolved injury where those reasons prevail.

This account preserves a place for finality, third-party interests, and limited resources. It also refuses to manufacture factual certainty out of institutional fault. Its demand is that an authority distinguish “we were probably right” from “we may continue on these terms.” Once it has culpably destroyed the route by which those terms could be challenged, the first claim does not by itself establish the second.
