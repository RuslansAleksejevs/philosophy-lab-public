# When the Record Ends: Evidential Closure and the Burden of Continuing Authority

A decision can be justified when it is made, its supporting records can later be permissibly destroyed, and its continuing enforcement can nevertheless become unjust. These claims are consistent. What changes need not be the probability that the decision was correct. It can be the distribution of responsibility for establishing whether an institution may continue to impose it.

I defend a principle governing this distribution. When an institution controls the feasible means of challenging a serious continuing burden, choosing to eliminate those means normally obliges it to reconsider the burden without requiring the affected person to supply the evidence thereby lost. The institution may retain its rational belief in the original verdict. It may even renew the burden. But it must justify that renewal on terms that do not treat the person's manufactured inability to challenge as sufficient reason for continuation. Preservation is one way of meeting this obligation; withdrawing the burden or establishing an independently justified rule of finality are others.

This is a claim about practical authority and its evidential conditions, rather than a claim that information must always be preserved. Its interest lies especially in innocent closure: cases in which neither an erroneous verdict nor wrongful destruction has been established.

## 1. A verdict outlives its evidence

Consider a cooperative that controls access to a unique workshop. After a fair investigation, its board finds that Lea deliberately damaged shared equipment. The evidence is strong, the investigators are competent, and Lea has been heard. The cooperative imposes a substantial additional charge whenever she uses the workshop. The charge has no fixed end date; the board will remove it if the original finding is shown to have been mistaken.

The investigation relied on recordings that also disclose sensitive information about other members. Suppose that, after a reasonable period, deleting these recordings is permissible. This might be because retaining them creates a serious risk to those members, while secure preservation is unavailable. Their confidentiality matters independently of whether the board got Lea's case right. Deletion is announced honestly, not performed to cover up an error. The board retains its written finding and an accurate account of its procedures.

Years later, a new method of interpreting the equipment's behaviour makes a previously unrecognized alternative explanation plausible. It does not establish Lea's innocence. Applying it to the recordings could have done so. There is no other feasible way to recover the relevant sequence of events. The board replies that the charge must continue because Lea has produced no new evidence disproving its finding.

This answer is defective even if the original verdict remains more likely correct than incorrect. The new interpretive possibility matters, but the particular defect does not depend on its making innocence probable. The board requires a kind of showing while its own authorized policy has removed the means of making it. A requirement that was demanding before closure has become insuperable for an institutionally produced reason. Treating these as the same situation conceals a change in who bears the cost of possible error.

The case does not establish that Lea should immediately receive unrestricted access, that she was innocent, or that the records should have been kept despite the risks. It establishes a narrower demand: the cooperative owes a response to the continuing charge that takes responsibility for the loss of review. “Bring us the recording” is no longer an adequate account of what Lea must do.

The background concerns are familiar. Pettit's account of contestatory democracy includes institutional arrangements that enable people to challenge decisions, including arrangements that pre-empt the need for an actual appeal (2005, section 2, pp. 9–12). Landström shows how archival destruction can obstruct redress and transmit epistemic injustice across time (2021, “The ‘migrated archives’” and “Denial of past wrongdoing as epistemic injustice”). These insights rule out treating the connection between records and accountability as a new discovery. The question here concerns a more specific tension: what remains owed when ending access to the evidence was itself permissible?

## 2. Three permissions

We should distinguish permission to decide, permission to close an evidential resource, and permission to continue treating a verdict as decisive unless its subject rebuts it.

Permission to decide responds to a practical need. Institutions cannot suspend every decision until error is impossible. A sufficiently careful investigation can justify imposing a cost despite residual uncertainty. That uncertainty need not make the institution negligent or the decision illegitimate.

Permission to close concerns a different action. Records consume resources, expose people, and sometimes have to be destroyed in the very process of learning from them. A unique sample may be consumed by an indispensable test. A temporary record may be erased to protect someone who cooperated with an investigation. Even a broad commitment to corrigibility cannot entail that every evidential item must survive every justified use.

The third permission determines how the institution proceeds after the initial adjudication. It may allow the institution to decline reconsideration unless a challenger supplies evidence meeting a threshold. Such arrangements allocate the risk that a mistaken decision will persist. They can have good reasons: reconsideration is costly, other people rely on settlements, and endlessly reopened disputes can become instruments of harassment. But those reasons are not identical to the reasons supporting the verdict or the deletion.

One invalid inference between these permissions is epistemic. The absence of successful challenges can sometimes increase confidence in a verdict, but only if challenges had an adequate opportunity to succeed. In a simplified model, suppose a functioning review process produces no corrective finding in nine out of ten cases where the original verdict is right, but in only two out of ten cases where it is wrong. A period without correction then favours the original verdict. Suppose instead that deletion disables the only effective review process. There will now be no corrective finding whether the verdict is right or wrong. The silence supplies no additional discrimination between those possibilities.

These are stipulated numbers, not an empirical model of institutions. Their purpose is to isolate a familiar evidential point. A silence produced by disabling a test cannot be assigned the significance of a silence after passing it. Harris's discussion of evidence control makes a related point about the informational force of apparent consensus: intervention in which contributions remain available can alter what the surviving appearance supports (2024, section 5).

This point does not establish a duty to preserve. Nor does it show that the written finding loses all evidential value. A trustworthy witness does not cease to provide evidence merely because the witnessed event cannot be replayed. The board's retained report can likewise support belief after deletion. What cannot legitimately be added is the supposed vindication provided by a challenge that no longer has a route to success.

The more difficult inference is practical: from “we were entitled to decide this” and “we were entitled to destroy these records” to “we remain entitled to demand that you overturn the decision using them.” Rejecting that inference requires an argument about responsibility, not simply a correction to probability theory.

## 3. Responsibility for the terms of challenge

The relevant responsibility follows from how an institution uses its control over evidence in a relationship already structured by its authority.

An institution imposing a serious continuing burden must be able to offer an adequate justification for continuing it. This requirement does not imply that justification must be freshly produced every morning. A past investigation, reliable testimony, or a defensible settlement procedure can transmit justificatory force over time. Nevertheless, the institution must have an answer to relevant changes in the conditions under which it asks people to accept its decision. That answer cannot consist solely in pointing to the very condition whose acceptability is at issue.

Suppose an institution says that a restriction remains justified because a competent finding stands unrebutted. Two things are doing work. The finding is evidence about what happened. The requirement of rebuttal is a rule governing which party must move the dispute forward and suffer if it cannot. These can come apart. A finding may still be good evidence when it is no longer fair to place the entire task of reopening on the affected person.

Before closure, the rule might have the following defence: Lea can obtain a review by bringing an alternative interpretation of the accessible record; the cooperative need not repeatedly reinvestigate a matter she has given it no reason to reopen. After closure, that defence is unavailable for challenges that depend on the destroyed material. Lea can identify an alternative, but she cannot develop it into the evidential showing the cooperative requires. The board's action has changed the practical meaning of its own rule.

Why should the cooperative, rather than Lea, bear responsibility for this change? First, it is the cooperative that continues to exercise authority over her. Second, the disputed requirement is part of the cooperative's justification for doing so. Third, the cooperative controlled the choice that disabled compliance. Taken together, these facts place on it a burden of explaining why the resulting disadvantage may be assigned to Lea. Its special responsibility is not derived from having done something blameworthy. It arises from using a permissible exercise of one power to determine the conditions under which another power can continue.

A mundane analogy helps distinguish responsibility from punishment. A housing association may permissibly close an unsafe staircase. If it continues requiring a resident to attend essential meetings upstairs, however, the permission to close does not settle whether it may treat her absence as refusal to participate. It owes an alternative route or a reconsideration of the attendance requirement. Providing one does not punish the association for closing the stairs. It prevents one justified policy from silently changing the justification of another.

The evidential case is less straightforward because preservation offers a possibility of vindication, not a guarantee that a justified challenge exists. Yet this difference does not remove the responsibility. The staircase might have been usable by a resident only on some days. Eliminating it still changes what can fairly be demanded of her. Similarly, a feasible review route matters even if most people using it would lose. Its point is to distinguish the cases, not to assure each challenger a favourable outcome.

The proposed principle is therefore conditional:

**When an institution permissibly closes an evidential resource under its control, and this substantially disables the feasible means of contesting a serious burden it continues to impose, it must reassess any requirement that the affected person supply the missing evidence as a condition of relief. Maintaining that requirement needs a justification addressing the loss of contestability, rather than merely repeating the original verdict or the justification for closure.**

Four features delimit the principle. There must be a continuing institutional imposition, rather than merely someone's desire to discover a historical truth. The institution must have relevant control over the resource or its availability. The lost material must matter to a feasible class of challenges, rather than merely to an imaginable discovery under unlimited technology. And the challenger must lack an adequate substitute. The principle concerns substantial impairment, not every deletion or every increase in inconvenience.

A person need not prove that the destroyed item would have vindicated them. Requiring that proof would recreate the problem. They must instead identify the burden, the lost route, and its relevance to a recognizable way the verdict could be mistaken. In Lea's case, that way involves distinguishing deliberate damage from the newly intelligible equipment behaviour. This is more than announcing that anything is possible; it is less than establishing the missing evidence's content.

Where the authority plans closure before anyone can identify a particular error, the appropriate level of assessment is the policy. It should ask which normal forms of challenge will become unavailable and how continuing burdens will be handled after that point. An evidential deadline should not produce a permanent practical disadvantage simply because no one could yet articulate the discovery that would later make review valuable.

## 4. Closure can change a burden without ending authority

What does reassessment require in practice? There are three main possibilities, none of which is automatically sufficient in every case.

The institution can preserve an adequate route of challenge. This might involve limited retention, protected access, or an independent reviewer able to examine the relevant material. The object of protection is the route's effectiveness. A surviving summary that records only the board's conclusion may preserve evidence for its view while excluding the details needed to assess alternatives. Conversely, preserving every detail is unnecessary if a less intrusive record permits the relevant disputes to be examined competently.

If no route survives, the institution can reconsider the continuing burden without insisting that the affected person first establish the unavailable defeater. The original finding remains admissible evidence. What changes is the requirement to overcome it before receiving an assessment. The institution must evaluate whether the remaining support, the costs of error, the duration of the burden, and the interests of others justify its continuation under the present conditions of irrecoverable uncertainty.

In Lea's case, that assessment might support a temporary charge followed by review of present conduct, a limited charge shared across the cooperative, or cessation of the additional charge. These are possibilities, not conclusions supplied by the principle. A severe risk to other workshop members could still justify a restriction. If so, it would rest on an explicit assessment of that risk, including the uncertainty the cooperative has chosen to leave unresolved. The principle requires responsibility for the decision, not a fictional finding that Lea was innocent.

Finally, the institution can justify a settlement rule under which the burden survives the record. Consider a carefully bounded levy established through an accessible adjudication, where reopening after a known date would undermine many members' legitimate financial plans. Such a procedure could justify finality even though later evidence might have changed the result. But a defence must address the rule as a whole: the opportunities initially supplied, the seriousness and duration of its effects, the reasons for the deadline, and the interests protected by refusing further inquiry. The reliability of the original decision is part of this assessment, not a substitute for it.

This possibility marks a disagreement with a recent proposal that treats destruction of the evidential basis as categorically dissolving delegated epistemic authority (Sophia 2026, sections 2–3). Auditability is valuable, but its continuing presence cannot be necessary for every justified reliance on an expert's completed work. A warranted destructive test is a counterexample. More fundamentally, losing a check on a decision and losing every ground for authorizing its practical effects are different losses. My principle requires a normative assessment of those effects; it does not announce an analytic extinction of authority.

There is also a procedural analogue rather than a claim of absolute novelty here. Rule 37(e) of the United States Federal Rules of Civil Procedure distinguishes measures to cure prejudice from stronger measures requiring an intent to deprive another party of evidence. Its applicability presupposes, among other conditions, a failure to take reasonable preservation steps. The present argument concerns a different question: even when closure was permissible, continuing the previous distribution of evidential burdens can require a new defence.

That difference matters. The institution need not be sanctioned, disbelieved, or declared illegitimate. It must decide what follows from the conjunction of permissible closure and continuing power. Fairness after an innocent loss is not exhausted by determining that nobody deserves punishment.

## 5. The costs of keeping correction possible

The most serious objection is that the principle makes closure only nominally permissible. If an institution must either preserve expensive records or expose every old verdict to renewed challenge, it may be forced into indefinite retention. A permission that can never be exercised without intolerable consequences is of little use.

The answer is not that preservation is cheap. Sometimes it is not; sometimes it is harmful. Rather, the original case contains two independently adjustable features: the lifetime of the records and the lifetime of the presumption attached to the decision. An institution can limit either. The assumption that must be defended is that its effects may last indefinitely while its revisability is temporary. Where resources warrant a short evidential lifetime, this gives reason to consider correspondingly limited effects, rather than always buying a larger archive.

This pairing does not demand exact numerical equality between retention and enforcement periods. Some burdens can reasonably survive their records because their original adjudication and finite settlement have adequate justification. Others require continued reassessment despite extensive records. The argument rejects an automatic inheritance: one decision about deletion must not silently settle a second decision about the terms on which a person remains burdened.

A related objection concerns expertise. Perhaps preserving a route of challenge forces experts to produce reasons comprehensible to whoever disputes them, replacing good professional judgement with whatever is easiest to document. Nguyen argues that demands for public transparency can distort both expert reasoning and the particular understandings of communities; moving assessment to relevant experts reduces but does not eliminate the problem (2022, section 6). This warning constrains what counts as an adequate route. A person can have a claim to competent consideration without a claim that every reason be translated into terms they can independently verify.

Nor does preservation require publication. Keeping sensitive material available to an appropriate review procedure differs from making it permanently searchable. Pressly grounds an important defence of forgetting in the possibility of forming relationships that are not preconditioned by access to one's past (2024, sections I and III). That interest cannot be dismissed as hostility to truth. Public memory can constrain agency even when the remembered facts are accurate.

Indeed, the present principle can support deletion. If a record must be removed to protect its subjects, an institution should ask which restrictive classifications derived from it should also cease to operate. Deleting the underlying story while retaining a permanent negative label may combine the harms of remembrance with the harms of forgetting: the person's past continues to govern their treatment, while the means of correcting its official interpretation disappear. That combination needs a stronger defence than either component considered alone.

There remain hard conflicts. A record may involve several people with divergent interests. One needs it to contest a restriction; another needs it destroyed for safety. Giving the challenger absolute control over preservation would allow an open dispute to hold the second person captive. The principle does not grant that control. It places responsibility on the institution to reconsider its burden if closure is required. Sometimes no adjustment will compensate for the loss of truth, recognition, or vindication. A practicable account of authority should acknowledge that remainder rather than promise that a procedural substitution repairs everything.

## 6. Finality, abuse, and the location of the duty

A second objection appeals to equal treatment. Everyone affected by a procedure might prefer, in advance, a cheap system that deletes records after a short period while treating its decisions as final. Why should a later disappointed person receive a special opportunity unavailable under those agreed terms?

If the agreement is informed and fair, and the procedure's stakes are suitably limited, this may be a successful defence of the package. It exemplifies the independent justification the principle permits. But the symmetry of a written deletion rule is insufficient. People can be equally subject to a rule while bearing very different consequences of its failures. Those who lose a decision may bear the continuing costs of error, whereas everyone benefits from savings. A defence of finality must explain this allocation rather than point only to uniform dates or uniform wording.

The distinction between finite settlement and indefinitely renewing exclusion is especially relevant. Others may reasonably depend on a dispute being settled. That reliance can justify leaving a past transfer in place even after its basis becomes unreviewable. It does not automatically justify every new use of the old verdict. A settlement of who pays for damaged equipment and a permanent classification of who deserves inferior standing are different exercises of power. An institution cannot obtain the latter's legitimacy merely by giving it the former's name.

A third objection concerns strategic challengers. A guilty person might wait for the record to expire and then demand reconsideration. If invoking missing evidence always secured relief, the system would reward delay and manufacture baseless disputes.

But the principle grants no automatic relief, no presumption of innocence about the historical event, and no right to unlimited hearings. The institution can use evidence that the claimant deliberately frustrated an earlier review, the availability of genuine substitutes, and a justified period of finality. It can reject challenges that do not identify a relevant lost route. It may also conclude that the surviving evidence adequately justifies continuation. What it cannot do is answer a qualifying challenge solely by insisting on the material it has itself made unavailable.

Finally, why emphasize chosen closure rather than accidental loss? A fire could create the same obstacle. If future standing matters, should it not matter equally after the fire?

It can. Continuing institutional burdens may require reassessment after losses for which nobody is responsible. My claim identifies a sufficient ground for a special duty, not the only ground for any duty. Chosen closure has an additional feature: an actor exercising authority controls whether the evidential requirement remains realistically satisfiable. That connects responsibility for setting the requirement with responsibility for withdrawing its means. After a fire, the appropriate response may depend more heavily on a general allocation of unavoidable misfortune. After deliberate but permissible closure, the institution must additionally answer for the package of choices it maintains.

The duty thus falls initially on whoever controls the continuing imposition. An archivist may advise or facilitate preservation, but cannot normally abolish a workshop surcharge or change a benefit rule. Delegating custody should not allow the body imposing the burden to declare the resulting loss somebody else's problem. Its task is to govern the use it makes of what remains.

A rational verdict can survive the end of its record. What cannot pass unnoticed through that ending is the demand that the person subject to it bear the whole burden of correction. An institution can choose closure, preserve authority, and still owe a change in the terms on which its decisions continue to govern a life.
