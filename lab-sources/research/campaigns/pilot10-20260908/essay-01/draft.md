# The Objection That Outlived Its Evidence

A public institution receives a serious objection to a policy. Its officers agree that the objection requires an answer. Before they provide one, an unforeseeable accident destroys the relevant records. Staff retire; the institution continues. Years later, its new officers have respectable evidence for the policy and no evidence establishing that the old objection was correct. May they require the objector to make the case again from the beginning?

There is something wrong with answering yes simply because the institution now knows less. But saying that forgetting cannot improve justification will not settle the matter. The objection might have been mistaken, current officers might reasonably believe the policy sound, and nobody might be blameworthy for the loss. The outstanding problem concerns what the institution owes someone who has already challenged its authority.

I defend a principle of non-regression in contestation: where an institution continues a decision under the authority to which a qualifying objection was addressed, losing information in its custody does not by itself undo the objector's completed burden of initiating a response. The institution must answer, establish that the matter was properly closed, or arrange a proportionate substitute for the response it can no longer provide. This is a claim about the persistence of a procedural position, not the persistence of a belief's evidential force. Its most important consequence concerns what institutions must preserve: the capacity to continue a challenge from the point it reached, rather than every fact that once entered their files.

## 1. Two things an objection does

Consider a fictional municipal trust that allocates rehearsal rooms to local musicians. It ranks applications using a measure advertised as identifying applicants who lack affordable alternatives. A group of musicians submits a documented objection: the measure counts access to a school facility as an affordable alternative even when the facility cannot accommodate their instruments. The trust verifies that the submission identifies a relevant problem and undertakes to investigate. The musicians have supplied enough to deserve an answer, although their interpretation might ultimately prove incorrect.

An accidental fire destroys the submission and the underlying correspondence. No one could reasonably have prevented this loss. A short register survives: it identifies the objection, its admissibility and its unresolved status. A new board inherits the allocation scheme. Its recent survey supports the scheme's overall performance but does not address the disputed treatment of school facilities. The musicians ask for the promised determination. The board replies that it has no evidence sufficient to establish a defect and that they must assemble their case anew.

The survey may support a favorable assessment of the scheme. It does not explain why the musicians must repeat work that the trust had accepted as sufficient. An objection has two potentially independent effects. It can supply evidence against a proposition. It can also exercise someone's standing to require a response. Losing access to the evidence may change what one should believe; it does not follow that the exercise of standing never occurred.

The difference is familiar outside inquiry. Losing the record of a request does not show that the request was never made. Yet the distinction becomes easy to miss when what was requested was a justification. Here both the missing material and the outstanding performance involve reasons. An institution can slide from “we can no longer assess those reasons” to “no one has supplied reasons requiring us to respond.” That inference silently converts an informational loss into a change in another party's position.

I shall call an objection *qualifying* when it is specific enough to identify a decision and an alleged defect, relevant enough that its correctness would materially affect the decision's justification, and supported enough to warrant a response under defensible standards for the practice. Qualification does not entail truth. A misunderstood criterion may warrant an explanatory correction; a sound criticism may require policy change. Nor does qualification require officials' discretionary approval. A submission can satisfy the appropriate standard even if an obstructive office refuses to record it. The trust's acknowledgment makes the opening case especially clear, but it does not create a general power to decide which objections count by ignoring them.

The argument applies to institutions whose exercise of authority includes an obligation to entertain challenges from those subject to it. It does not assume that every disappointed preference generates such an obligation. Its purpose is to identify what follows over time once a challenge has the requisite standing.

## 2. Why this is not simply a theory of memory

Michael Huemer distinguishes acceptable acquisition from acceptable retention of a belief. On his account, remembering does not convert a badly acquired belief into a justified one merely because its origin has been forgotten (1999, §§2–4). The general thesis that memory can only preserve epistemic status is disputed: Jennifer Lackey explicitly argues that memory can generate it (2005). Nothing here requires deciding that dispute. [Huemer](https://spot.colorado.edu/~huemer/papers/memory.htm); [Lackey](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2005.tb00418.x).

The trust might justifiably believe its policy excellent and nevertheless owe the musicians an answer. Conversely, it could respond properly to them and still have poor grounds for believing the policy excellent. A candid admission that the objection succeeds, followed by a relevant policy change, may discharge the challenge while abandoning the original belief. A technically impressive survey that never addresses the objection may strengthen the belief while leaving the challenge unanswered. The two dimensions can therefore vary separately.

The account also differs from tracing blame to an earlier culpable choice. Tracing locates responsibility for an outcome in an earlier moment of control; Vargas identifies difficulties involving its epistemic conditions (2005, abstract). The fire case removes the earlier culpable choice. Its point is that an obligation to respond can remain even when there is no culpability to trace. [Vargas](https://repository.usfca.edu/phil/3/).

These distinctions do not make the broader subject new. Bovens characterizes accountability through an actor's relation to a forum that can demand explanation, question and judge (2007, abstract). Goggins argues that institutional forgetting can exclude social memories from policymaking (2023, abstract). Forst develops the political importance of people's standing as participants in justification, rather than merely recipients of favorable outcomes (2025, pp. 125–129). The present proposal isolates a temporal requirement within such a relationship: an institution's reduced informational capacity is not, without a further justification, a reason to reverse the progress another party has already made in invoking it. [Bovens](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0386.2007.00378.x); [Goggins](https://doi.org/10.1177/17506980231184563); [Forst](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108868143.010).

A right to initiate a challenge and a right to have that challenge remain pending are distinct. An institution could honor the first at every moment while frustrating the second indefinitely. Each new board might invite submissions, assess them conscientiously, then require resubmission when organizational memory fails. Every encounter could look locally open while the procedure as a whole denied anyone a completed hearing. The relevant unit of assessment is consequently the continuing contest, not a succession of isolated opportunities to speak.

## 3. The argument against restarting

Three considerations support non-regression.

First, the initiating burden must have a point of completion. If providing the required grounds never establishes a position that survives anything short of a final answer, a purported right to an answer amounts only to a conditional invitation: keep reproducing the grounds whenever the addressee needs them. Sometimes a practice can permissibly impose such a condition. A request tied to rapidly changing circumstances might require renewed evidence each year. But that condition needs justification. It cannot be inferred simply from the institution's inability to remember what it received.

In the trust's case, the musicians met a stated evidential burden, the relevant policy continues, and the condition challenged has not changed. The demand for fresh work is not caused by a fresh question about the merits. It is caused by a failure of the recipient's capacity to complete the original procedure. Treating those causes identically assigns the cost of institutional continuity to whoever happened to challenge it.

Second, the institution receives objections as part of exercising its authority. Its ability to allocate rooms across years depends on a durable office: decisions, rankings and permissions survive the departure of particular officers. The opposing side of that relationship must have some comparable persistence. If the trust may carry forward its allocation while requiring the musicians to recreate their challenge after each internal discontinuity, institutional authority is durable but the standing to contest it is episodic.

This is not an argument that every power must have a perfectly symmetrical liability. Institutions can reasonably have records, resources and time limits unlike those of individuals. The asymmetry at issue is more specific. One party continues to invoke a settled procedural history—the allocation was made, the policy remains in force—while its own loss of information is treated as sufficient to unsettle the other party's history—the qualifying objection was made, a response became due. The event offered as the explanation bears on the institution's capacity to perform, not on the objector's standing. Without an additional reason, it does not support the proposed change.

Third, an institution should not enjoy unilateral control over the practical expiration of challenges to its continuing authority. If forgetting alone resets the initiating burden, a board can achieve what an overtly exclusionary rule would achieve by changing its retention practices. The result need not be intentional. A procedure that makes the survival of a challenge depend entirely on the addressee's internal information management is structurally vulnerable even when every incumbent behaves decently. The lack of bad intent removes one ground for blame; it does not make the arrangement an adequate way to secure contestation.

This third consideration does not imply that every accidental loss amounts to domination. It identifies why information custody matters to the allocation of the resulting burden. The trust can organize the office through which allocation and review take place. The musicians cannot. A system that treats receipt as completion of their initiating task must therefore assign the trust a continuing task of managing the response. Otherwise the apparent division of labor collapses whenever fulfilling it becomes inconvenient.

Together these considerations support a limited conclusion. Where a qualifying objection remains unresolved, the challenged decision continues, and the loss occurs within the institution's responsibility for handling the challenge, the loss alone cannot return the parties to their positions before submission. They do not prove that the objection succeeds, that the policy must stop immediately, or that all costs belong to the institution. They establish who owes the next response and who must justify departure from that allocation.

## 4. What an innocent successor inherits

The new board did not cause the fire or make the promise. Why should it bear the remaining burden?

Because it occupies the office that continues to allocate the rooms. The obligation is attached to the institutional role through which the contested authority is exercised; it is not a personal penalty inherited from former officers. A board cannot explain why its old allocation remains binding by appealing to the trust's continuity while explaining why the challenge has vanished solely by appealing to its personnel's discontinuity. The relevant continuity concerns the decision and the authority exercised over the claimants, not a metaphysical theory of a corporate mind.

There are important limits. Purchasing an unrelated piece of equipment from the trust does not make the buyer responsible for its unfinished hearings. Nor does the mere resemblance of a new organization to an old one establish succession. The strongest case is a successor that takes over the practical power to maintain, revise and defend the same decision toward the same affected parties. Where power is divided or the old institution dissolves, responsibility may need a further allocation. The argument does not solve every case of historical succession. It shows why replacing officeholders within an ongoing authority cannot itself extinguish the challenge.

Nor must the successor answer from nowhere. Ought implies can constrains the performance demanded. If the original evidential basis is irretrievable, an answer purporting to determine exactly what it established may be impossible. The obligation then concerns the nearest adequate feasible response: disclose the loss, recognize the point the challenge had reached, support reconstruction where useful, and consider an accommodation or policy review where reconstruction cannot settle the issue. Which response is adequate depends on the importance of the burden, the likelihood that renewed inquiry would help and the effects on others.

There is a difference between requiring the musicians to assist and requiring them to requalify. They might uniquely remember which instruments were excluded. Asking for that information need not wrong them. But the trust should treat their assistance as part of completing an existing investigation, with reasonable support where reconstruction imposes substantial costs. It should not make its loss a ground for declaring that no investigation is due until they meet the original threshold again.

A completely forgotten challenge brings out a further distinction. Suppose even the register is destroyed and no current officer knows that the musicians submitted anything. A present officer may have no reason to initiate this particular inquiry. That concerns what the officer can reasonably recognize and perform. It does not show that the musicians' position disappeared. When a credible receipt or testimony later establishes that a qualifying submission remained pending, the trust should resume from that point. It need not infer that the substantive complaint was correct.

Thus two offices with identical current evidence about the policy's merits can owe different things. One may never have received a qualifying challenge; the other may have an unfinished one. Their evidence concerning their respective procedural histories will normally differ once the challenge is invoked. The argument does not conceal that difference, or use it to manufacture an epistemological paradox. Its claim is that information about a prior procedural act establishes a present obligation even when it contributes little evidence about the truth of the original allegation.

## 5. Closure, new evidence and unreasonable demands

The hardest counterexample is an objection that was fully answered before the fire. Does non-regression now force the trust to answer it forever?

No. The surviving position depends on what occurred. If the trust supplied an adequate answer through a defensible procedure, made it available to the musicians, and allowed whatever opportunity for challenge that procedure properly required, the initial obligation was discharged. Forgetting the answer does not make that performance unhappen, any more than forgetting a submission makes the submission unhappen. Persistence applies to closure as well as initiation.

There is nevertheless an evidential problem about whether closure occurred. A reliable record that an identified objection received an answer may justify treating the matter as concluded even when the full answer is no longer retained. The record's adequacy depends on the practice: for a simple misunderstanding, a dated correction and notice may suffice; for a serious challenge to an enduring rule, an unexplained “closed” label may not. An institution cannot turn every unresolved matter into a settled one merely by recording its preferred outcome.

The principle therefore creates no presumption that missing evidence would have favored the challenger. It creates a presumption against treating a known pending challenge as never initiated. If the surviving evidence leaves it genuinely uncertain whether a qualifying submission occurred or was answered, that uncertainty needs a fair procedure of its own. Testimony, receipts and ordinary evidence of reliable handling may all matter. Non-regression governs the significance of established procedural history; it does not supply fictitious certainty about that history.

A second objection concerns fresh evidence. Suppose a new inquiry conclusively shows that the trust's measure handles school access appropriately. Must the board reconstruct the lost investigation nevertheless?

Again, no. An obligation to answer does not require reproducing the original route to an answer. A new inquiry can satisfy it if the trust explains how its findings meet the actual objection. The reason “we have since answered your challenge” differs from “your challenge ceased to count when we lost it.” Only the latter treats forgetting as discharge. Indeed, a fresh answer may be better than reconstructing obsolete files. What matters is that the response connects to the issue raised and can be recognized as a response by those entitled to receive it.

A third objection is that any person can manufacture endless burdens by submitting complaints. Qualification addresses part of this problem. A bare suspicion or an objection already answered without new grounds need not initiate the relevant duty. Further protections can include consolidation of repeated submissions, reasonable deadlines, representative responses and a threshold responsive to the severity of the decision's effects. These devices require independent justification; none is ruled out by non-regression.

The threshold itself cannot require the objector to establish the ultimate claim. A right to challenge would otherwise protect only people who can already demonstrate that the institution must change course. The trust's musicians may be unable to determine the full effect of its classification without the trust's data. Their initiating burden should be enough to identify a material issue that merits examination, not enough to perform that examination unassisted. A defensible procedure can separate the cost of bringing an issue into review from the later cost of determining it.

There is also a genuine resource objection. If reconstructing one pending challenge would prevent the trust from maintaining usable rooms for hundreds of other musicians, it may permissibly decline that reconstruction. Calling an obligation continuing does not make it absolute. But the trust then owes a reason concerning the comparative burden and, where appropriate, an alternative response. It cannot describe the objectors as having failed to initiate a challenge. That distinction matters even when no additional investigation occurs: it identifies whose demand is being limited and makes the limitation itself answerable.

Finally, perhaps a right to challenge always allows the institution to reset procedures under generally reasonable retention rules. A fair arrangement might require claimants to keep copies for a specified review period, after which a challenge expires. Such an arrangement can alter the case. But a justified limitation enacted as part of the terms of contestation does the normative work, not the subsequent destruction of records. Moreover, a period that predictably expires before the institution can answer is not vindicated merely by being announced. General rules can disguise the same failure to make initiating a challenge an effective step toward receiving a response.

## 6. Preserving a position without preserving everything

Non-regression yields a different ideal of institutional memory from accumulation. The trust needs to preserve enough to carry a challenge forward: what decision it concerns, what issue qualified for consideration, what remains to be answered, and what has already discharged parts of the obligation. When the substantive materials are necessary to that task, it has a reason to retain them. When a compact and reliable account is adequate, retaining all the materials need not be required.

This makes preservation selective in two directions. An institution may need to retain information of little value for improving its own predictions because that information establishes someone else's procedural position. Conversely, it may have no entitlement to retain personally revealing information merely because it might be useful for future predictions. These are separate normative questions.

Basu's account of forgetting sharpens the second point. She distinguishes erasure, archiving and siloing, and argues that forgetting can protect people's ability to shape their identities and maintain appropriate contextual boundaries (2022, §§1–2.7). An institutional response duty is therefore no warrant for indefinite exposure of a complainant's private life. [Basu](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2022.36).

Suppose the musicians' original submission included confidential family circumstances explaining their lack of alternatives. The trust might preserve a sufficiently specific, independently reviewable conclusion about eligibility while deleting unnecessary identifying details. If the detail remains indispensable, restricted custody or an agreed independent custodian might preserve access for the relevant review without making it generally available. These arrangements are possibilities, not automatic solutions: a summary can omit exactly what later makes a criticism intelligible, and restrictions can render an ostensible archive unusable.

The normative test is functional but not merely predictive. Can the surviving arrangement sustain the claimant's position and the institution's ability to respond under fair terms? A vast archive that nobody can retrieve may fail that test. A short record accepted through an adequate procedure may satisfy it. The obligation is to maintain usable continuity of contestation, not to maximize the amount remembered.

Where privacy and effective response genuinely conflict, there may be no costless answer. The institution should seek a justified settlement of that conflict with those whose interests are at stake. In some cases it should abandon reliance on the disputed classification, rather than retain intimate information indefinitely to defend it. The authority to continue a policy does not bring an unconditional entitlement to whatever informational resources would make defending it easiest.

The central fire case ends similarly. The board may remain convinced that its allocation scheme is generally sound. It may have good reason to keep the rooms operating while reviewing the challenge. It can tell the musicians exactly what was lost, help identify the disputed applications, and obtain a fresh assessment of the relevant criterion. If that is disproportionate, it can explain the constraint and propose an accommodation. What it cannot infer from an empty file is an empty procedural history.

An institution's knowledge can diminish while its obligations remain. Recognizing that possibility avoids both assigning guilt to innocent successors and letting institutional ignorance dissolve the claims of people already heard. A durable authority needs durable ways for challenges to progress toward completion. What must outlive the evidence is not a verdict against the institution, but the objector's standing to receive the next answer.
