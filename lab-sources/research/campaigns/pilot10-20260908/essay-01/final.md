# The Objection That Outlived Its Evidence

A public institution receives a serious objection to a policy. Before the objection is answered, an unforeseeable accident destroys the relevant records. Staff retire; the institution continues. Its new officers have respectable evidence for the policy and no evidence establishing that the old objection was correct. May they require the objector to make the case again from the beginning?

Something is wrong with answering yes simply because the institution now knows less. Yet neither a ban on forgetting nor an accusation of irresponsible ignorance identifies the problem. The objection might have been mistaken; current officers might reasonably believe the policy sound; nobody might deserve blame for the loss. What remains is a question about the position of someone who has already qualified for a response.

I defend a principle of non-regression in contestation. When a qualifying challenge has been reliably established as pending, an institution's loss of the material it was responsible for handling does not itself cancel the claimant's completed initiating task. The institution retains responsibility for managing the challenge toward an answer or a justified termination. That responsibility protects concrete procedural achievements, while permitting reasonable cooperation requirements and genuine resource limits. It does not automatically require compensation, a substitute benefit or a favorable verdict.

The crucial distinction is between responsibility for managing an unfinished proceeding and responsibility for performing every task needed to recover its evidence. The first normally remains institutional. The second can be distributed fairly. Preserving that distinction shows both why a cheap request to resend a document can be permissible and why demanding a completely new application can be objectionable, even when both requests follow innocent information loss.

## 1. A submission changes more than the evidence

Consider a fictional municipal trust that allocates rehearsal rooms to local musicians. Its ranking system purports to identify applicants who lack affordable alternatives. A group submits a documented objection: the system counts access to school facilities as an alternative even when those facilities cannot accommodate their instruments. They submit through the designated review office. Their evidence satisfies the defensible threshold for opening an investigation, and its receipt and admission are reliably recorded. No official makes an additional personal promise.

Before any determination, an accidental fire destroys the submission and correspondence. Nobody could reasonably have prevented it. A short register survives, identifying the issue and its pending status. A new board inherits the scheme. A recent survey supports its overall performance but does not examine the disputed treatment of school facilities. The musicians ask for the determination. The board replies that they must apply for review again, paying a new filing charge, satisfying the admission threshold anew and joining the back of the queue.

The survey may support a favorable assessment of the scheme. It does not explain the new charge or the loss of a procedural position. An objection can do two things: provide evidence against a proposition and exercise standing to require a response. Losing the evidence may alter what the institution should believe. It does not follow that the exercise of standing has been revoked.

Call an objection *qualifying* when it identifies a decision and an alleged defect, concerns something that would materially affect the decision's justification if correct, and has enough support to warrant a response under defensible standards for the practice. Qualification is weaker than success. A misunderstanding can deserve a clarifying answer; identifying a possible defect can justify inquiry without proving it. Requiring a claimant to establish the ultimate allegation before admitting it would often demand the very investigation that review is meant to provide.

Qualification also differs from acknowledgment. A submission may objectively qualify even when officials refuse to record it. Conversely, a careless clerk may acknowledge a submission that fails the relevant standard. The opening case stipulates reliable establishment of qualification, not an infallible power of stamps. What follows is a claim about an established pending proceeding. Uncertainty about whether a proceeding ever reached that point requires separate treatment.

The argument presupposes a practice in which affected people have a justified claim to challenge an exercise of authority. It does not establish that every institution owes everyone a hearing. It addresses the strongest case of continuity: the responsible office continues a materially relevant decision toward the affected claimants. An obligation to account for a past proceeding may survive even after a policy ends; that broader proposition is not needed here.

## 2. From a continuing status to a continuing task

The bare observation that a procedural event occurred establishes too little. A board could acknowledge that the musicians once qualified, place that acknowledgment in a beautiful archive and do nothing further. Historical accuracy alone does not secure a useful right to challenge. Nor does the persistence of an obligation to provide one thing automatically establish an obligation to provide any convenient substitute when performance becomes impossible.

The relevant starting point is a division of labor. Through its review office, the trust determines where a challenge must be delivered and assumes the work of bringing admitted challenges to a disposition. The musicians must identify and initially support their issue. The trust must then organize its investigation and communicate what becomes of it. Submission can establish an obligation to manage a process without guaranteeing a particular factual finding or granting the institution an unlimited claim on the complainants' future work.

Why should that division survive accidental loss? Because a defensible route into review must do more than repeatedly let people start. If every interruption inside the receiving office restores the entire initiating task to the claimant, successfully following the designated route never secures a position from which a response can be pursued. The office has offered only a succession of chances to begin. For a continuing institution, effective access requires some persistence of what was achieved through access.

This is not an appeal to sunk costs. The musicians could have labored just as hard on an unsent submission without acquiring the same claim. Delivery of a qualifying challenge matters because it completes the task assigned to them within a justified procedure. Nor does mere physical control over paper create every subsequent duty. Custody matters within an existing allocation of procedural responsibilities. An archive that happens to possess a duplicate need not thereby become responsible for deciding the complaint.

The objection that powers and burdens need not be symmetrical is correct but incomplete. The trust's enduring power does not entail every imaginable duty to every former applicant. The argument concerns a particular relationship already activated by a qualifying challenge. The trust continues to invoke the allocation while remaining the office responsible for its review. Personnel change explains why someone else must perform the office's task; it does not explain why the task returns to the claimant.

This rationale applies without bad intent. A procedure that places the survival of an admitted challenge entirely at the mercy of the addressee's internal arrangements leaves effective access unsecured. Malicious destruction would add a further wrong. Its absence does not make an automatic reset a fair division of responsibility. Innocent accidents can change what performance is possible without determining who must manage the consequences.

These premises support a minimum practical requirement. After an established pending challenge suffers custodial loss, the institution must preserve completed entry requirements, organize the next feasible step and provide an answerable disposition if it proposes to terminate or materially restrict review. It must not use missing contents as sufficient reason to erase the claimant's original filing date, impose a duplicate initiation charge or require fresh admission. Such changes can sometimes be justified for other reasons, but their justification must address the status already acquired.

The date does not confer an indefeasible queue position. Emergencies or fair prioritization among claims can warrant changes. The point is that a challenge should not automatically become new merely because the office has lost its materials. Likewise, a claimant admitted under an erroneous decision may sometimes be removed; correcting an admission requires grounds about the error, not simply the inability to reproduce what supported it.

This is more demanding than recording history and less demanding than guaranteeing redress. It identifies a default allocation of the next procedural task. The institution owes an account of what is now required, why it is required and how the proceeding can move toward completion. A right to enter review carries some claim against being returned to its entrance by failures within the route one was required to take.

## 3. Cooperation without starting over

Suppose the musicians retain a legible copy that takes thirty seconds to send. The trust would need weeks to reconstruct the same evidence. It can reasonably ask for the copy and ordinarily make progress conditional on receiving it. Management of the proceeding remains with the trust; efficient provision of a particular item can belong to the musicians. They keep their admission and filing date. They do not owe a new fee merely because resending happens to reproduce the same bytes as the first submission.

If they refuse without a good reason, the trust may suspend or even terminate the investigation under defensible cooperation rules, with notice and an opportunity to explain. That would be a response to their present refusal, not a finding that they never initiated review. The difference is practical: the trust must identify the specific requested assistance and defend its reasonableness. It cannot require an entire new qualification process where a copy would suffice.

Now suppose their copy is also gone. Recreating the submission would require another month of interviews, measurements and unpaid work. The trust cannot establish that this work is theirs merely by pointing to the gap in its files. Once delivery is the recognized route into review, making the claimant automatically insure all subsequent procedural continuity undermines the effectiveness of that route. The office must assess alternatives: use surviving information, conduct its own inquiry, fund a limited part of reconstruction or explain why a narrower review is appropriate.

That assessment does not predetermine who pays. Public resources and other applicants matter. Sometimes the complainants have special knowledge whose acquisition cannot reasonably be duplicated. Sometimes a common disaster leaves neither side able to proceed. A fair arrangement may require shared work or end an investigation. What persists is institutional responsibility for making and defending that arrangement from the procedural point already reached.

Additional assistance has a further basis. Where a low-cost institutional contribution is necessary to make an admitted challenge practically reviewable, and would not impose disproportionate burdens on other legitimate claimants, refusing it would make the promised form of access ineffective. The reason for assistance is then effective access under fair terms, applied to an existing proceeding. Its extent depends on those terms and competing claims. It does not follow from a general rule that innocent loss generates compensation.

Contrast two boards. One asks the musicians to resend an available copy while keeping their admission and identifying a date for its determination. The other says that, because the old file is empty, a new application must be made within an already-expired deadline. The first distributes a small recovery task. The second turns an internal loss into forfeiture. Non-regression distinguishes them without treating either the burden of evidence production or the burden of institutional management as absolute.

## 4. The institution cannot simply declare itself finished

A stronger difficulty concerns impossible performance. If the evidence is irretrievable or reconstruction would consume resources needed to keep hundreds of rehearsal spaces usable, the original investigation may reasonably end. Does calling that ending a disposition rather than a reset accomplish anything?

It does if the proposed termination is itself accountable. A defensible cost or feasibility explanation should identify what cannot be recovered, what alternatives were considered, why the available alternatives are insufficient or disproportionate and what effect this has on the particular challenge. The fact that a loss occurred is one premise. A demonstrated constraint on answering is another. Neither entails that the original allegation is false, and a constraint on answering may sometimes matter to whether reliance on the contested decision should continue.

The same board may be well placed to provide this information but poorly placed to have final authority over its adequacy. Otherwise it could respond to every complaint with a formulaic cost statement and call itself discharged. The problem of unilateral expiration would reappear as a power of unilateral certification.

Disputed material restrictions should therefore be assessable under publicly defensible criteria through a proportionate route not wholly controlled by the addressee. For a small trust, this might be a reviewer from another municipal office, a mutually accepted assessor or a public supervisory process. It need not be a new tribunal for every missing page. The required separation concerns control over a disputed disposition, not a compulsory organizational form. A minor request for an available copy may need only an ordinary opportunity to explain; ending a serious admitted challenge requires more.

This requirement grows from the same justification as the initial review right. A challenge to authority is ineffective if its addressee can terminate it on grounds the claimant has no practical way to contest. When cost claims become the grounds of termination, effective contestation must reach those claims. No endless appeal follows. A justified procedure can designate a final forum with fair participation and limited review powers. Finality rests on that authority's defensible role, rather than on the challenged office's inability to complete its work.

The distinction is close to Pettit's defense of institutions through which people can contest public decisions, with reduced burdens and hearing bodies distinct from those responsible for the decisions challenged (2004, §§2.2–2.3). The temporal extension proposed here is that this capacity must protect progress within a challenge, including scrutiny of a termination prompted by lost evidence. [Pettit](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1616).

A reviewer might endorse termination without ordering further investigation or assistance. The musicians have then received no substitute rehearsal room and no favorable judgment. Nevertheless, their earlier admission has made a difference: the institution had to address a pending matter and justify ending it through the appropriate process. A mere reset would have required them to establish a right to reach that process again.

There is room for honest disagreement over whether a proposed restriction is proportionate. The principle offers no quantitative formula for comparing every claim on a public budget. Its more limited contribution is to prevent the distribution of uncertainty and cost from being settled automatically by the institution's informational failure. The burden is to justify limiting an admitted challenge, rather than to demand that its initiators prove afresh that it merits attention.

## 5. What survives, and what does not

An objection fully answered before the fire is a decisive limiting case. If the trust supplied an adequate answer through a defensible procedure and made it available to the musicians, its obligation was discharged. Loss does not make that performance unhappen. Initiation and closure must receive parallel treatment: forgetting neither cancels an established submission nor revives an established pending duty that was already discharged.

Whether closure occurred is an evidential question. A reliable record may establish that an identified issue received an adequate response even when the full answer is gone. Its sufficiency depends on the seriousness and complexity of the matter. An unexplained “closed” label is not an automatic substitute for evidence of a completed procedure. Conversely, a complainant's inability to recall an answer need not outweigh a trustworthy record of its delivery.

The same distinction governs admission. Suppose new evidence shows that the clerk routinely stamped submissions admissible without reading them. That evidence can warrant review of whether the musicians qualified. The reason for reopening is the defective admission practice, not the later fire. A fair correction procedure must consider the reliability of what survives and any distinct protection created by reasonable reliance on the official acceptance. The present argument does not decide all questions about the binding force of erroneous admissions.

Where qualification was never reliably established and its evidence is missing, non-regression supplies no fictitious certainty. A register can be evidence of standing without constituting it conclusively. The main principle is conditional: it specifies what follows from an established procedural status, while allowing independent grounds to contest that status. It blocks treating the missing support for an admission as, by itself, a reason to withdraw an otherwise reliable admission.

Suppose instead that even the register is destroyed, and nobody in office knows that the submission occurred. No current officer need undertake an investigation they cannot reasonably identify. The existence of an outstanding claim, an officer's evidence about it and personal blame are different matters. When credible evidence later establishes the pending submission, the trust should recognize its prior initiation. It need not infer that the lost evidence proved the musicians right.

An innocent successor consequently inherits a role-bound management task, not another person's guilt. The strongest succession case is a new board able to maintain, revise and defend the same allocation toward the same affected musicians. An unrelated buyer of the trust's equipment does not thereby acquire every unfinished review. Divided authority, dissolution and broader historical redress raise additional allocation questions. Nothing here depends on a complete metaphysics of institutional identity.

New evidence can also make reconstruction unnecessary. A fresh investigation may answer the disputed school-access question more effectively than recovering old correspondence. The board must connect its findings to the actual issue. “We have since answered the challenge” is a reason for closure; “the challenge ceased to count when its file disappeared” is not.

Similarly, the policy might retain its name while its relevant mechanism changes. If school access no longer figures in allocation, the trust can explain the change and its consequences for the pending review. Accounting for what happened and deciding whether to alter a current policy are distinguishable tasks. Some remaining historical claim may require an answer, but preserving initiation does not force the reconstruction of an obsolete question in its original form.

Finally, defensible review systems may set time limits and cooperation duties in advance. A claimant might reasonably be required to preserve a copy during a short review period. Such rules can allocate some continuity risks to claimants. Their fairness depends on expected burden, notice and a real opportunity to obtain a response. A period that routinely expires before an institution acts cannot be vindicated merely by announcing it. An independently justified limitation can terminate a proceeding; the eventual destruction of records is not what justifies the limitation.

## 6. The kind of memory answerability requires

The institutional problem does not reduce to the epistemology of remembering. Huemer's dualistic theory distinguishes justified acquisition from justified retention and criticizes simple preservationism as well as the idea that mere retention can launder a bad origin (1999, §§2–4). The present account takes no side on his theory. An institution can have reasonable grounds for its policy while owing a particular claimant a response; it can also respond adequately by admitting that the policy was unjustified. [Huemer](https://spot.colorado.edu/~huemer/papers/memory.htm).

Nor is the broader connection between files and complaint new. Ahmed describes the labor of complaints, a disappeared inquiry file and the failure of merely filing a complaint to address its subject (2018, paragraphs on complaint files). Her discussion makes a preserved record with no further action an especially important challenge to any account of procedural continuity. The narrow claim defended here concerns the division of tasks after innocent loss, rather than a general discovery that recordkeeping affects institutional power. [Ahmed](https://feministkilljoys.com/2018/02/11/opening-the-file/).

Forst's account supplies another background commitment: justice concerns people's standing in practices of justification, not simply the benefits they receive (2025, pp. 125–129). Non-regression specifies one temporal implication for an admitted challenge. A procedure can be open at every new encounter yet fail to honor what earlier encounters established. Its adequacy must therefore be judged across the continuing proceeding. [Forst](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108868143.010).

The resulting requirement of memory is selective. The trust should preserve enough to identify the issue, the procedural stages completed, what remains due and any legitimate closure. Some original evidence will be indispensable. In other cases, a compact reliable record plus access to a new investigation may suffice. The standard is the capacity to continue or properly conclude the proceeding from the position it reached.

This differs from maximizing information. A fact of little predictive value may be indispensable because it establishes timely initiation. Conversely, a revealing personal detail useful for predicting future demand may be unnecessary to preserve the claimant's position. Usable procedural memory can require retaining the first while permitting or requiring deletion of the second.

Basu distinguishes erasure, archiving and siloing, and argues that forgetting can protect identity and appropriate contextual boundaries; the relevant duties are defeasible (2022, §§1–2.7). The need to answer a complaint therefore provides no general entitlement to retain or expose the claimant's private life indefinitely. [Basu](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2022.36).

If the musicians' submission contains intimate family details, the trust may be able to retain a sufficiently specific eligibility determination while deleting unnecessary details. Restricted access or independent custody may sometimes reconcile effective review with privacy. None is automatically adequate: a summary may omit what a later objection needs, and a nominally available archive may be practically inaccessible. The test is whether the arrangement preserves fair participation and response capacity with justified informational burdens.

An institution could meet a records checklist while failing this test, by keeping every submission and never taking the next step. It could also satisfy the test after losing nearly everything, by crediting the established admission, organizing fair recovery and providing a reviewable determination of what can be done. What matters is the relation between memory and procedural action.

The fire does not establish that the musicians deserve different rooms. It does establish a difficulty that the trust, as manager of an unfinished review, must address. It may ask for an easy copy, investigate afresh or justify a narrower disposition. Those possibilities preserve a division of labor in which bringing a qualifying challenge was an achievement with continuing practical consequences. A durable authority needs ways for challenges to progress toward completion. What must outlive lost evidence is not a verdict against the institution, but an established claim on what it does next.
