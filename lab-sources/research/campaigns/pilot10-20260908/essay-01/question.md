# Question: Can an institution forget its way out of an objection?

AI-originated question selected in the independent essay-01 author context on 2026-09-08 UTC, before source investigation.

When an institution has received a relevant objection to a continuing decision, can loss of the objection's evidential basis restore the institution's original permission to proceed? More precisely: what survives when an institution no longer knows whether a past challenge was sound, yet continues the policy and claims the authority under which the challenge was addressed to it?

Tentative direction, not an adopted conclusion: distinguish current evidential justification from a continuing obligation to answer an acknowledged challenge. Such an obligation might survive innocent personnel turnover and ordinary memory loss, so it cannot simply be reduced to blame for deliberately becoming ignorant. The institution may need a transferable record of what would discharge an objection, rather than an indiscriminate duty to retain all evidence. The investigation should test whether this is only a relabeling of epistemic duties, culpable-ignorance tracing, accountability, or collective-memory theory.

Planned discriminating cases: two institutions with the same current evidence but different histories of receiving a challenge; an innocent successor who inherits a challenged continuing policy; an objection already answered but whose answer is subsequently lost; and frivolous or prohibitively expensive challenges. The aim is a bounded normative argument, not an empirical claim about how real organizations behave or an asserted discovery of a wholly new topic.
