# When a Remedy Becomes Evidence

A municipal fund reimburses residents for damage caused by its contractors. It requires photographs, supplies the only inspection service, and undertakes to preserve the resulting records. Mara files a timely claim. Before it is decided, the fund deletes the entire photographic archive to save money, despite knowing its preservation obligation. The deletion is indiscriminate: nobody looks at the photographs or selects inconvenient claims. Mara has a receipt proving that an inspection occurred, but nobody can now establish what it showed.

The fund eventually recognizes that it owes Mara a remedy for destroying her opportunity to substantiate her claim. It awards a payment. Months later, another office receives a list of “successful claims” and treats Mara’s entry as evidence that the contractor damaged her home. Alternatively, suppose the fund refuses reimbursement but pays separate compensation for the lost opportunity. Its list of “unsuccessful claims” later becomes evidence that Mara’s accusation was unfounded. Either way, the institution converts a decision about what to do under damaged evidential conditions into an apparently authoritative answer to the question the damage prevented it from answering.

The problem survives a fair payment and an honest initial hearing. I will argue that a remedy for wrongful evidence loss carries a continuing obligation concerning its later use. The institution must take reasonable steps to keep a practical settlement from acquiring factual authority that its grounds do not support. This obligation protects claimants from an official history of supposed defeat, but it also protects others from unsupported accusations disguised as remedial success. Its object is neither perpetual indecision nor favorable belief. It is a settlement whose authority remains appropriately limited as the decision travels.

## 1. Three questions hidden in one outcome

Three propositions must be distinguished in Mara’s case:

1. The contractor damaged Mara’s home.
2. The fund wrongfully destroyed evidence relevant to Mara’s claim.
3. Mara is now entitled to the payment awarded by the fund.

The first may remain unsettled while the second and third are established. The third need not be a polite fiction. If the fund has a justified power to award compensation for an evidential wrong, exercising that power can create a real entitlement. Mara may truthfully insist that the money is hers even if she cannot truthfully insist that the fund established the contractor’s responsibility.

These propositions can diverge in the other direction as well. The fund might have conclusive evidence that the contractor caused no damage, while still owing Mara compensation for delay or expense caused by an earlier destruction of records. An evidential wrong does not guarantee that the underlying claim was true. Conversely, a claimant can have a true underlying claim without having suffered an evidential wrong. The differences concern grounds of entitlement, not degrees of sympathy.

This distinction becomes important because institutions often need categorical outcomes. Payment cannot remain simultaneously authorized and unauthorized while the fact-finder suspends judgment. Yet a categorical practical answer does not require a categorical answer to every historical question on which the dispute initially depended. A newly created remedial entitlement supplies a different ground on which practical deliberation can end.

The distinction between action and belief is familiar. The further issue is what the institution may let its decision communicate to people who did not observe the adjudication. A record can correctly communicate the third proposition to a cashier while misleadingly suggesting the first to someone evaluating the contractor. Its adequacy depends on its use, and that use may change after its author has stopped attending to it.

## 2. What the existing remedies leave to be explained

The thought that evidence loss itself calls for redress is established. Porat and Stein defend liability for wrongfully worsened uncertainty, including compensation and changes to the burden of persuasion. Their argument expressly recognizes a claimant’s interest in information needed to pursue rights. Friedman’s response favors a plurality of remedies adapted to different circumstances. These positions already defeat any claim that evidential harm matters only as an obstacle to proving some other wrong. (Porat and Stein 1997, abstract; Friedman 1997, introductory passage.)

Nance supplies a closer antecedent. He separates the management of evidence from the determination of merits and favors explicit judicial responses to tampering over misleading or prejudicial inferential routes. He criticizes concealing a remedy for an evidential problem inside an apparently ordinary merits determination; he also discusses effects on later malpractice claims. My proposal extends this concern from the initial allocation of adjudicative tasks to an institution’s continuing responsibility for the public use of its result. (Nance 2010, pp. 1103–1104, 1110–1114.)

These antecedents narrow the contribution. It is not a discovery that a wrongdoer can owe compensation despite uncertainty, that sanctions differ from factual findings, or that records should be accurate. The question is whether an initially adequate remedy remains adequate when its output predictably moves into a context where its grounds are no longer visible. I argue that, in the relevant cases, its issuer has an affirmative duty to prevent this change in practical use from becoming an unauthorized change in factual authority.

Nor is the proposed duty a substitute for deciding the appropriate material remedy. How much Mara should receive depends on matters this essay does not settle: the value of her lost opportunity, the fund’s undertaking, available substitutes, and competing claims on its resources. Suppose a payment has been fairly determined. That supposition isolates a further obligation which even a correct award can leave unfulfilled.

## 3. Why the custodian owes more than a payment

Start with the original preservation obligation. Why does the fund owe Mara continued access to the photographs? Partly because it has organized a system in which she must establish her claim through evidence it controls. The fund cannot reasonably demand that residents use its exclusive inspection service and then treat preservation as an entirely internal convenience. The means of meeting its evidential demand are part of what it owes those subject to the demand.

The destruction creates two different disadvantages. Mara loses an opportunity to obtain a favorable decision. She also loses the means of showing why later assessments of her claim should be accepted, rejected, or qualified. A payment may address the first disadvantage. It does not automatically address the second. The latter matters whenever other people rely on the fund’s records as a substitute for conducting their own inquiries.

Consider the unsuccessful-claim version. Mara receives fair compensation for the lost evidential opportunity. The fund tells her, accurately, that it has not established the contractor’s responsibility. It records the reimbursement decision as “denied” in a payment system where this code means only that no reimbursement was authorized. There is no original lie. Later, the same fund gives those codes to another office which uses unsuccessful claims to identify allegedly unreliable complainants. A locally adequate code now invites a judgment that the fund had no grounds to make.

The fund should prevent this foreseeable use. Three considerations together explain why. First, Mara had a directed claim against the fund to preserve an evidential route, not merely an interest in society’s generally knowing more. Second, its breach has made her less able to correct subsequent assessments of the dispute. Third, it controls an official substitute for the missing evidence: the decision record on which others are invited to rely. By circulating the substitute without its material limitation, it permits its own authority to magnify the disadvantage its breach created.

The resulting obligation is reparative. It is owed even if the original deletion was not undertaken to harm Mara, even if the payment removes any financial advantage from deletion, and even if nobody consciously mistakes denial for falsity. Institutional information can do its work through routines which no participant examines closely. The fund cannot discharge its responsibility merely by avoiding an explicit false sentence at the moment of adjudication.

This does not mean that compensation can never cover future informational injury. An adequate, informed settlement might provide alternative protections and release some continuing duties. But payment for the lost chance of reimbursement does not, by itself, purchase permission to manufacture a public impression that the underlying claim was refuted. A person who accepts money because a damaged procedure cannot establish her claim need not thereby accept a history in which that procedure established her mistake.

The same reasoning reaches an innocent successor. A new administrator inherits the fund’s records, resources, and obligations; she need not inherit personal blame for deleting photographs. Her duty is to administer an outstanding institutional repair correctly. Replacing every employee does not restore Mara’s capacity to answer an official representation of her claim. The relevant continuity is continuity of the institution’s undertaking and its control over the representation, not continuity of anyone’s culpable mental state.

## 4. A constraint on the travel of decisions

The principle can now be stated:

**When an institution’s remedy depends materially on an unresolved evidential deficit that it wrongfully created, it owes those affected reasonable measures to preserve the distinction between what the remedy establishes and what the deficit leaves unresolved, wherever it authorizes or can reasonably control consequential reuse of that remedy.**

“Materially” excludes missing evidence that makes no difference to the decision’s grounds. If independent photographs conclusively resolve Mara’s claim, the earlier loss need not qualify a subsequent finding about the contractor. The fund may still owe compensation for other consequences, but it is no longer repairing an unresolved deficit in deciding the underlying matter.

“Reasonable measures” excludes a demand to control every inference anyone might make. The fund should describe the grounds of its award, attach an appropriate limitation to consequential transfers, and correct predictable institutional misclassifications within its control. It need not police every private conversation or publish Mara’s entire file. If a receiving office needs only to know whether a payment entitlement exists, it may receive precisely that information. The historical uncertainty becomes relevant when the office proposes to infer something for which the entitlement is being used as a proxy.

The distinction must survive transfer in substance, not necessarily in a particular format. An intelligible statement that the award repairs missing evidence may suffice. In an administrative system, separate records of the established breach, the practical outcome, and the unresolved allegation may be needed. The philosophical requirement concerns permissible inferences. It does not prescribe a universal form or demand that every record contain every qualification.

The principle also has a positive side. A remedial award can be excellent evidence that the preservation obligation was breached. It can establish a genuine present entitlement. The fund should communicate those conclusions confidently when its grounds warrant them. Preserving the unresolved question is compatible with affirming everything that has actually been resolved.

One useful test concerns a change of audience. Imagine that a competent recipient learns exactly why the original decision was made. Would that information materially weaken the particular historical inference the recipient is being encouraged to draw? If so, the institution cannot adequately transmit the decision for that purpose while withholding the relevant basis, absent a sufficiently strong competing reason. This test does not require the recipient to duplicate the original inquiry. It asks whether the issuer is concealing a known limitation on the very authority the recipient is being invited to trust.

## 5. The strongest challenge: authority requires finality

An institution must sometimes settle a dispute precisely because the parties cannot agree about the facts. If an adverse party can reply forever that a settlement proves nothing, the authority to settle looks empty. Moreover, formal recognition can constitute a fact. Once an authorized body grants someone a status, there may be no further question whether she really possesses that status. Why weaken a remedial decision with a permanent reservation?

The objection is powerful because it identifies two kinds of finality that can be secured. The fund can conclusively determine that Mara is entitled to its payment. It can also determine that the original reimbursement proceeding will not be reopened without some specified ground. Neither determination requires the conclusion that the photographs would have vindicated Mara. Practical finality concerns what the parties may demand and what the institution will now do. Historical finality concerns whether a question about what happened has been adequately answered. The first can be justified while the second remains unavailable.

The status point reinforces this distinction. Where a legitimate act creates an entitlement, its recipient need not apologize for possessing a merely “procedural” substitute. The entitlement is fully real. But the power to create an entitlement is not thereby the power to create its usual historical basis. A fund can make a payment owed; it cannot make a contractor have damaged a house. Someone who challenges Mara’s current entitlement by pointing to the unresolved past is making the converse mistake of someone who treats the entitlement as proof that the past has been settled.

There is nevertheless a public value in outsourcing inquiry to institutions. Ross argues that criminal convictions should support rational belief in guilt and provide a basis for ending ordinary public inquiry; in later work he analyzes convictions as defeasible institutional testimony. That is an account of the authority a finding should earn through appropriate inquiry. It is not a license to transfer the same authority to every institutional resolution. (Ross 2023, §IV; Ross 2026, “Conviction as Institutional Testimony.”)

The present argument does not require criminal guilty verdicts to become non-factual instruments of compensation. Its central cases involve a different power: providing a remedy for an established evidential breach. A procedure which must attribute historical guilt to perform its proper function cannot solve a proof deficit merely by changing its label. It may have to withhold that verdict while pursuing another authorized remedy. The scope of permissible repair depends partly on what the resulting institutional act communicates and permits.

Finality is therefore not defeated by an intelligible separation of grounds. The parties know what is owed; the administrator knows what to execute; a later decision-maker knows which historical issue remains open. What is denied is an extra authority to settle that issue merely because the first two tasks have been completed.

## 6. Objections about evidence, disclosure, and other people

Perhaps the decision really is evidence of the underlying fact. Even remedial payments generally require a credible claim; authorities do not pay everyone who asks. A recipient who learns that Mara received a remedy might rationally raise her confidence that the contractor damaged the house. Prohibiting such an inference would itself be epistemically irresponsible.

The principle prohibits no warranted inference. It requires the inference to reflect the kind of decision that was actually made. Consider a deliberately artificial illustration. Among ordinary merit-based awards, ninety percent concern true underlying claims. Among awards made because a credible claim became unresolvable through wrongful record loss, forty percent concern true underlying claims. Suppose ninety percent of all awards belong to the first category. An observer told only that an award occurred might rationally assign an eighty-five percent probability to the underlying claim. An observer who knows the award belongs to the second category should use forty percent instead. These numbers illustrate information loss; they describe no actual institution.

The second observer can still regard the award as evidence relative to a sufficiently low initial probability. Nothing requires treating it as evidentially inert. The wrong occurs when the institution knows the decision’s category, foreseeably invites the ordinary inference, and withholds a material distinction it could reasonably preserve. Its authority then conceals information that defeats the inference it encourages. In the limiting case where the recipient already knows all the grounds and the rule that produced the decision, republishing the decision supplies no additional confirmation of the underlying allegation.

A different objection points to the destruction itself. Someone who burns a file may reveal knowledge that it contains damaging material. Agreed. Evidence of selective destruction can rationally support an allegation, and the fund may communicate that support. Mara’s example deliberately involves indiscriminate deletion for cost savings, so it does not automatically carry that significance. Even where destruction is probative, its actual evidential force and its remedial significance remain distinguishable. The mere fact that an adverse outcome is justified as a remedy does not show that the inference from destruction is strong enough to justify the same outcome as a factual conclusion.

Disclosure presents a harder problem. Labeling Mara’s award as remedial may stigmatize her as a less deserving recipient. The label may even expose private details to people who have no legitimate interest in them. Would preventing these harms justify describing her simply as a successful claimant?

Sometimes it would justify limiting the information shared. If a landlord needs to know only that Mara can pay rent, the fund should confirm the entitlement without inviting an inference about the contractor. The duty concerns a misleading use of authority; it is not a general demand for more disclosure. When another office is entitled to evaluate the contractor’s record, however, protecting Mara’s reputation cannot normally justify supplying it with a misleading factual proxy. A carefully framed statement can affirm her complete entitlement without representing a contested historical conclusion as established. If no such representation is possible, withholding the misleading proxy may be preferable to sending it with either a stigmatizing or an inadequate explanation.

What if the unresolved matter itself implicates third parties? Suppose a board lost examination scripts and proposes to repair the loss by certifying everyone’s professional competence. Its wrongdoing may warrant funded reassessment, compensation, or another accommodation. It cannot by itself justify exposing clients to a professional whose competence has never been established. Here the ordinary certification communicates a substantive assurance on which others are entitled to rely. Calling the certificate a remedy does not remove that assurance or transfer the risks fairly.

This case places an important limit on the earlier argument about constituted status. A board may have the institutional power to issue a credential while lacking moral permission to issue it under the circumstances. The fact that an authorized act would create a genuine status does not settle whether creating it is justifiable. A payment whose costs the responsible institution can fairly bear differs from an assurance that redistributes danger to strangers. Repair must respect the rights of people who did not cause the evidential deficit.

Finally, why should the obligation depend on wrongdoing? An accidental loss can also make an unqualified decision misleading. A responsible institution should generally prevent foreseeable misuse of its testimony. That broader obligation is compatible with a more specific reparative one. In the wrongful-loss case, a particular person can demand that the institution carry the reasonable burden of preserving the decision’s limitations, because its breach has impaired her ability to do so. A stranger might owe the same corrective act from a general duty of honesty. The custodian additionally owes it to the person whose evidential position it damaged. This distinction matters for who must act, who should bear the costs, and whose complaint persists after personnel have changed.

## 7. What remains after closure

The deepest danger of wrongful evidence loss is not always a permanent blank. Institutions are good at filling blanks with outcomes. They pay or refuse, certify or withhold, close files and generate reports. Those outputs can acquire an authority that the missing evidence would have supported only if it had survived and been examined. An institution may thereby turn its inability to establish a claim into a record that appears to settle it.

A remedy should interrupt that process. It may create a secure entitlement, close a proceeding, and authoritatively establish that an evidential duty was breached. Its issuer must also preserve the boundary between those achievements and the historical question that remains unanswered. This is a continuing component of repair because decisions continue to affect people after their immediate practical work is done.

Mara need not obtain a favorable historical verdict in order to receive a real remedy. Nor should accepting that remedy permit the fund to furnish an unfavorable historical verdict by implication. What she can demand is narrower and more durable: that the institution’s resolution of what it owes her does not become a counterfeit resolution of what happened.
