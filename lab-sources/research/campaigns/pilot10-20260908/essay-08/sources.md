## Proposed contribution after investigation

Institutions can properly create a present entitlement while remaining unable to establish the historical fact that normally grounds such an entitlement. When the institution wrongfully caused that inability, repair includes a continuing duty not to circulate its practical settlement as independent factual vindication or refutation. This is a duty governing the later uses of a decision, not a rule requiring irrational favorable beliefs. Its two sides protect both the claimant (whose unresolved claim must not become an official falsehood) and others (who must not inherit unsupported historical accusations).

The worked test will be a municipal repair fund that lost all inspection photographs. A monetary settlement, a finding that a claim was historically well-founded, and a finding that the custodian breached a preservation duty are three distinct propositions. A binary grant/deny record can conceal their divergence. The essay will defend preservation of the distinction when the record travels, while allowing secure new entitlements and practical finality.

## Literature and limitations

Porat and Stein already defend liability for evidential damage, including compensation and burden changes. Nance already distinguishes managing lost evidence from deciding merits, criticizing remedies that disguise the former as the latter; his paper even discusses consequences for subsequent malpractice claims. Ross already explains the defeasible epistemic authority of verdicts and the difference between acquittal and innocence. None of these familiar points will be presented as new. The contribution sought is a forward-looking duty of institutions to preserve the basis and limited factual authority of remedial decisions as they travel to new contexts, including contexts in which the claimant no longer has access or leverage.

This is a bounded literature search, not proof of originality. It does not establish that no one has defended the same duty. Searches covered evidential damage, evidence spoliation, evidence laundering, legal acceptance/belief, public deference to verdicts, and culpable ignorance. The most direct primary antecedent found is Nance, not the initial culpable-ignorance literature. A merely renamed anti-deception principle would be insufficient; the essay must show a duty that persists when a decision’s original wording was locally adequate and nobody makes an explicit false assertion.
