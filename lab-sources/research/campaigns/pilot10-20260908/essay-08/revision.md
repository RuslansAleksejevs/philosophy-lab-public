## Decision on the central criticism

Accepted. The draft's easy publication example showed a general communicative obligation, but did not show what its purportedly reparative explanation added. The final essay now acknowledges this rather than repeating the original argument. Section 3 develops the critic's proposed harder case: a recipient later corrupts an initially adequate transmission, while the original custodian can support an otherwise burdensome correction. The defended bridge concerns proportionate substitution for a protected capacity wrongfully impaired. Its claimed consequence is a further reason for assistance, presumptive correction-cost allocation, and priority over otherwise comparable discretionary requests. This can require support after general communication duties have been met.

The stronger interpretation is accepted in bounded form. I do not claim that wrongful and accidental loss always require different acts. Both institutions may owe cheap assistance from ordinary duties. I also reject unlimited liability for everything done downstream: the claimant must identify a credible relation to the lost evidential route; help must be useful and proportionate; it must respect other rights and need not guarantee success. This is a specific application of reparative obligations, not a new universal theory.

## Accepted revisions

1. Limits of institutional knowledge. The opening now includes consideration of testimony and reasonable alternatives. Throughout, the key claim concerns what the proceeding determined, rather than universal historical irresolution. Mara can know what the institution did not establish; other investigators can assess existing testimony differently. Section 4 explicitly protects her ability to contribute rather than making the institution the arbiter of everyone else's knowledge.

2. Binding factual issues. Section 5 now distinguishes historical evidential use, recognition of created entitlements, and legitimate procedural reliance on fixed issues. It directly engages Nance's issue-preclusion proposal and allows the remedial determination to remain binding within a justified scope. The audience test is limited to historical evidential inference.

3. Beneficiaries and release. Section 6 distinguishes the Mara-directed reparative duty from general duties to the contractor and later recipients. A settlement can potentially release individualized assistance but cannot release others' rights or authorize misleading testimony. Receipt of a lost-opportunity payment alone does not establish such release.

4. Remedy versus original denial. The duty now arises from wrongful impairment, independently of whether any compensation has yet been offered. Its survival after payment is a discriminating application. The scope therefore covers the representation of the underlying denial as well as the remedial award.

5. Philosophy of epistemic reparation. Flowerree and Altanian were actually retrieved and read in this resumed author session; the final manuscript acknowledges their substantive antecedents and narrows its contribution. Nissenbaum is included briefly as relevant context for changing information flows. Lackey is not attributed a detailed view without primary access.

6. Probabilistic precision. The artificial example remains explicitly fictional. The no-new-confirmation claim is now restricted to full knowledge of relevant grounds and a rule which fully determines the decision.

## Remaining disagreement and limitations

The critic correctly denied that the original examples established a distinctively reparative duty. The revised argument goes beyond the modest alternative of merely redescribing honesty, but it does not establish a wholly independent source of normativity. Its bridge remains open to challenge, especially the appropriate counterfactual baseline, reasonable allocation of assistance costs, and whether existing service obligations already require the same support. Those questions are now visible limits rather than implicit assumptions.
