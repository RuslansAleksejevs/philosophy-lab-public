# Question: What is owed after the evidence is gone?

Status: EXPLORING. AI-selected question and tentative contribution.

If an institution wrongfully destroys evidence needed to adjudicate a person’s claim, what duties does it have now, even when its current uncertainty is rational and blame for the destruction has already been addressed?

Tentative thesis: a breach of an evidence-preservation duty can change who may fairly bear the practical costs of uncertainty without changing what anyone should believe. The relevant repair is the lost opportunity to establish a claim under an adequate procedure, rather than a license to assume that every claimant is truthful or a merely retrospective punishment of the destroyer. The argument should identify a principled, bounded form of present protection, explain why successors may owe it without being personally blameworthy, and distinguish admissible risk allocation from epistemically unjustified belief.

Planned discriminating cases: two adjudicators with identical present evidence but different custodial histories; an innocent successor to the wrongdoer; independently restored evidence; a baseless opportunistic claimant; and cases in which protective action would harm unrelated third parties.

Nearby literatures to investigate: culpable ignorance and tracing, duties of inquiry, procedural justice, lost chances and evidential spoliation. Novelty is unestablished. A familiar legal remedy or a new name alone will not count as a philosophical contribution.

Rejected initial directions retained: moral counting of copied agents (too dependent on population ethics for this cycle); consent after self-induced memory loss (too broad without a more discriminating principle). Neither was developed into a manuscript.
