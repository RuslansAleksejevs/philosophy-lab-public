# Proposal: What the Verdict Does Not Tell Us

## Exact thesis

Sekine's specific inference from the reported intermediate conviction rate to the existence of successful individual reason-excluders is underdetermined. Even granting the three reported percentages as exact population rates, and granting comparable populations, they are compatible both with some judges wholly excluding the consideration and with every judge continuing to use it. Furthermore, equality of verdicts with a no-exposure control would not by itself establish **reason-guided** exclusion: a stable verdict can conceal a change in its motivating basis. Thus an empirical defence of the psychological possibility of second-order reason-responsiveness needs evidence sensitive to the relevant basing mechanism, not merely a mixture of verdict outcomes. This is a correction to one empirical supporting move, not a refutation of second-order reasons.

## Target and inherited premises

Kenta Sekine, “In Defence of Second-Order Reasons,” Canadian Journal of Law & Jurisprudence 39(1) (2026), 200–223, §3, publisher lines 598–604, discusses Wistrich, Guthrie and Rachlinski's 2005 study. His summary gives conviction rates of 49.1% without the additional evidence, 7.7% among exposed judges admitting it, and 20% among exposed judges excluding it. He takes this to establish that some judges can exclude considerations, though most cannot. His broader defence distinguishes logical possibility from contingent difficulty and explicitly allows indirect control. Those distinctions are retained.

The three figures are premises of the argument under examination, not estimates recalculated here. No individual dataset is available in this investigation. The exposed subgroups were divided by their own admissibility decisions, so treating their baseline dispositions as comparable is already a generous idealisation. The counterexample works even after granting that idealisation; it does not depend on demonstrating selection bias.

## Main argument / constructive counterexample

Let x be a judge's baseline deliberative score on a scale whose zero marks the conviction threshold. In an illustrative population, x is uniform on [-0.509, 0.491]. Conviction occurs exactly when the final score is positive. No-exposure conviction therefore has probability .491. When the additional consideration is used at full weight, it subtracts .414, so conviction has probability .077.

**Model A: successful exclusion for some.** A proportion q of instructed judges remove the consideration from their deliberative basis altogether; the others give it its full .414 weight. Suppose type is independent of x. Then the conviction rate is q(.491)+(1-q)(.077). Setting q=(.200-.077)/(.491-.077)=.297101... reproduces .200. Here some agents exclude completely, and all others do not.

**Model B: successful exclusion for none.** Every instructed judge gives the same consideration the reduced but strictly positive weight .291. The verdict rule becomes x-.291>0, giving probability .200. Every judge still bases the verdict partly on the excluded consideration. None wholly excludes it. All the reported aggregate probabilities match Model A.

These are stipulated countermodels, not fitted psychological theories. The score represents practical reliance on a consideration, not merely an unconscious bodily cause. Explicitly stating this avoids assuming that every causal effect is a motivating reason. Both models can have judges responding to the rule; Model B's response reduces the weight without eliminating reliance. Thus the inference to at least one successful excluder requires some premise ruling out partial reliance by everyone. That premise is not supplied by the three percentages.

The new result is not the arithmetic alone. It pinpoints a mismatch between the philosophical explanandum—refraining from acting on a consideration because of an exclusionary reason—and an outcome variable. An instructed judge can still convict while allowing the inadmissible consideration to weaken the basis for conviction; unchanged binary output is compatible with changed basing. Conversely, removing a motivating consideration need not switch the verdict. Even knowing a judge's two verdicts would not settle whether the reason was excluded. This second mismatch remains after the ordinary aggregate/individual problem is repaired.

## Defensible increment and closest comparisons

- **Sekine (2026):** shares the question of psychological possibility and the interpretation of this exact experiment. Increment: paired exact countermodels defeat a particular positive inference about individual successful exclusion while preserving the rest of his defence. The proposal is not another defence of second-order reasons or a reclassification of moral credit.
- **Maggie Wittlin, “Binding Hercules” (2023), Vanderbilt Law Review 76, 1735–1768, pp.1759–60, n.143:** already treats the relevant difference cautiously, notes its lack of statistical significance, and explicitly distinguishes discounting from disregarding. Those are prior insights, not ours. Increment: even a perfectly known, statistically secure intermediate population rate cannot identify any complete excluder. The two-model argument and the additional basing/unchanged-verdict problem answer the later philosophical capability inference, whereas Wittlin considers institutional evidence rules. We claim no novelty for discounting, caution about small samples, or institutional safeguards.
- **Rocchè (2024), abstract accessed:** empirical contamination can weaken the practical appeal of exclusion without dooming it. We do not claim that he argues universal impossibility, and do not accept Sekine's characterization as verified without his full text. Our correction concerns what the cited figures establish in Sekine's response.
- **Cuizhu Wang (2024), abstract and introduction excerpts accessed:** combines cognitive and social constraints on exclusion. The present result is about identifying complete reason exclusion from outcomes, not the existence of cognitive/social constraints. Full-text access failed, limiting the novelty check.
- **Keeling (2024), §§3.1–3.3:** a causal/doxastic account supplies a clear reason why mere verdict convergence is not equivalent to reason-guided basing. This background is inherited. The present critique neither originates her conditions nor depends on accepting them in full: any account that distinguishes acting for a reason from merely matching an outcome faces the measurement gap.
- **Holland (1986), “Statistics and Causal Inference,” bibliographic/abstract coverage only:** the general gap between group effects and individual causal effects belongs to established causal inference. No novel statistical theorem is claimed. The concrete correction adds a further philosophical distinction between an outcome effect and the agent's basis for acting.

## Objections the essay must answer

1. *Sekine needs only logical possibility.* Agree: the conceptual defence survives. But a passage purporting to establish actual psychological success contributes an empirical premise, and that particular premise is unsupported by the reported rates alone. The criticism must stay local.
2. *Partial suppression is enough to establish some control.* Agree, if causal attribution to the directive is justified. Control of degree and complete exclusion of a specified reason are different achievements. Evidence for the former need not settle the latter. Do not redefine every successful reduction as a failure of all second-order responsiveness.
3. *Models can be invented for any data.* The models use the simplest contrast at issue: a mixture of complete successes/failures versus uniform partial discounting. Wittlin's prior discussion makes partial discounting a motivated alternative, not an arbitrary rescue. The result is non-identification, not equal likelihood or a finding that nobody excludes.
4. *Acting for a reason does not equal score weight.* The models stipulate that the score term is recognized deliberative reliance. No reduction of basing to numerical weight is asserted. Any accepted account permitting several considerations jointly to motivate a verdict can instantiate the same structural contrast.
5. *A behavioural study can never identify reasons.* Too strong. Crossed interventions varying admissibility and probative force, repeated cases near thresholds, process evidence and checks on attention could discriminate proposed mechanisms. Such evidence remains defeasible. No new experiment, validated protocol, or universally sufficient operational test is claimed here.
