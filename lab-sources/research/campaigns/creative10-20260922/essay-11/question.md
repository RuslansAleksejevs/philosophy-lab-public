## Candidates considered

1. **Promissory release under moral uncertainty.** Could asking for release be a uniquely safe response when unsure whether keeping a promise would be wrong? Rejected at the idea stage. The initial search found substantial existing work on permissible promising under uncertainty, moral hedging and consent. No specific inference beyond combining familiar requirements was identified. This was not a claim that the literature conclusively contains every possible version.
2. **Promises as evidence about appropriate motives.** Challenge the treatment of promissory second-order reasons by separating what makes a reason appropriate from evidence that it is appropriate. Read Keeling (2024), especially §§3.1–3.3. Rejected as presently formulated: Keeling already allows contextual appropriateness, evidential access via reminders, and causal/doxastic variants. A general ground/evidence distinction is familiar, and a new example would not yet secure a worthwhile increment. Sekine (2026) also supplies a substantial alternative account of second-order reasons and credit. Do not recycle those accounts as a new contribution.
3. **What would a successful reason-excluder look like in the data?** Selected. In §3 of Sekine (2026), an intermediate aggregate conviction rate is used to infer that some judges successfully exclude reasons, although others cannot. A precise countermodel shows that the same rates can occur when every judge continues to give the inadmissible consideration positive deliberative weight. This is a local correction to the evidential support for a philosophical capability claim, not a new general discovery about aggregate data.

## Selected question

Do the three conviction rates invoked in a recent defence of second-order reasons establish that at least some agents can refrain from acting for a reason for a reason? What additional assumption about successful exclusion is needed for that inference?

The intended result is deliberately modest: isolate one invalid bridge from group verdicts to individual control over basing, give explicit observationally equivalent models with and without successful exclusion, and show why outcome stability is still insufficient for reason-guided exclusion. This leaves Sekine's conceptual defence and the logical possibility of second-order reasons intact.
