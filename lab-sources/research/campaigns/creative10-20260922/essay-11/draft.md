# What the Verdict Does Not Tell Us

A judge hears a consideration, rules it inadmissible, and reaches the verdict she would have reached without hearing it. Has she successfully excluded that consideration? Perhaps. But perhaps she counted it against the defendant and found the remaining evidence decisive anyway. A verdict can stay fixed while the reasons entering its determination change. Conversely, a rule can reduce a consideration's influence without removing it. These possibilities matter when judicial experiments are used to establish our ability to control which reasons we act on.

I examine one such inference in Kenta Sekine's recent defence of second-order reasons. The correction is narrow. A reported pattern of conviction rates is compatible both with some judges completely excluding a consideration and with every judge continuing to count it in deliberation. This remains true even if we grant that the rates are exact population probabilities, rather than uncertain sample estimates. The pattern therefore cannot, without further assumptions, establish the successful individual exclusion invoked in the argument. It also cannot establish that exclusion is impossible. The philosophical issue survives, but the experiment does less to settle it than the inference suggests.

Second-order reasons concern the reasons for which we act. An instruction to decide a hiring question solely on professional qualifications, for example, addresses more than the choice between applicants: it restricts the considerations on which that choice should depend. Following such an instruction is different from selecting the same applicant for an excluded reason. It is also different from being accidentally unmoved by that reason. Successful responsiveness requires a connection between the restriction and the agent's way of deciding.

In §3 of “In Defence of Second-Order Reasons,” Sekine discusses a study by Wistrich, Guthrie and Rachlinski. His account gives three conviction rates: 49.1% among judges who did not receive additional sexual-history information, 7.7% among exposed judges who admitted it, and 20% among exposed judges who ruled it inadmissible. He takes the pattern to establish that, although many judges cannot exclude the consideration, some can. He distinguishes contingent difficulty from impossibility and allows indirect control over motivation. My criticism leaves those qualifications intact. It concerns the positive inference to some successful excluders, not his broader conceptual defence. [Sekine 2026, §3](https://doi.org/10.1017/cjlj.2026.10062)

There is already a careful discussion of these figures. Maggie Wittlin notes the statistical weakness of the difference between the two exposed subgroups and distinguishes discounting evidence from discarding it. She also explains why residual influence can remain objectionable when the concern is an improper basis for deciding. These are substantive insights, not merely a warning about sample size. I take them as starting points. The further question is whether even perfectly established rates would identify any complete excluder. [Wittlin 2023, pp. 1759–60, n.143](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-wordpress-0/wp-content/uploads/sites/278/2023/11/27125213/Binding-Hercules-A-Proposal-for-Bench-Trials.pdf)

The answer can be shown constructively. I grant the three numerical premises as Sekine reports them. I also grant comparable baseline distributions, although the exposed subgroups were distinguished by the judges' own admissibility rulings. This is a generous simplification: one cannot normally assume that people making different rulings would otherwise decide identically. Nothing in the argument below depends on exploiting that additional difficulty. Nor does the argument depend on a reanalysis of the original experiment, whose full text and individual data were unavailable in this investigation.

Imagine a population of judges. Each has a baseline deliberative score, x, representing how strongly the admissible case supports conviction relative to the judge's threshold. A positive final score produces conviction; a negative score produces acquittal. Let baseline scores be uniformly distributed from −0.509 to 0.491. The interval has length one, and its positive part has length 0.491. Without the extra consideration, 49.1% therefore convict.

When counted at full strength, suppose the extra consideration subtracts 0.414 from each judge's score. A judge then convicts only if x exceeds 0.414. The relevant portion of the interval has length 0.077, producing the second rate of 7.7%.

These scores are not proposed measurements of actual judges. They describe a possible pattern of deliberation. In particular, the subtraction represents the agent counting the consideration against conviction. It does not represent a stomach ache, an unnoticed distraction, or any other influence that might cause behaviour without functioning as a reason. The model stipulates deliberative reliance because that is what the philosophical inference is meant to concern. Nor need real deliberation be literally numerical. The scores provide a convenient representation of differences in how close a judge is to a decision threshold.

Now consider two different responses to the exclusionary rule.

In the first model, some judges completely exclude the consideration because they accept the rule as a reason not to rely on it. They recognize its apparent force, but remove it from the basis of their decision. The rest continue to count it at full strength. Let the proportion of complete excluders be q, independently of their baseline scores. The overall conviction rate is then:

q × 0.491 + (1 − q) × 0.077.

Set q to 0.123/0.414, or approximately 0.2971. The rate becomes exactly 0.200. Thus a population containing about 29.7% complete excluders reproduces the reported intermediate rate. By stipulation, the successful judges exclude because of the rule; their zero reliance is not merely accidental.

In the second model, every judge responds to the rule by reducing the weight assigned to the consideration, but nobody eliminates that weight. Each now subtracts 0.291 rather than 0.414. Each still treats the information as counting against conviction. A judge convicts when x exceeds 0.291. The relevant portion of the interval has length 0.200. Again, exactly 20% convict.

The contrast can be displayed compactly:

| Condition | Model A: complete exclusion by some | Model B: partial reliance by everyone |
|---|---:|---:|
| No exposure | 49.1% convict | 49.1% convict |
| Full reliance on the information | 7.7% convict | 7.7% convict |
| Response to the exclusionary rule | 20% convict | 20% convict |
| Complete exclusion because of the rule | About 29.7% | 0% |

The same three outcome probabilities fit opposite answers to the question whether anyone completely removes the consideration from the deliberative basis. Knowing those probabilities exactly would not choose between the models. More precise estimation of those same quantities would therefore leave this particular problem unresolved.

One could obtain the first model by assuming in advance that judges either exclude completely or continue to rely at full strength. Under that restriction, the intermediate rate supports a mixture of successes and failures. But the restriction is doing essential work. It rules out the second model rather than deriving its impossibility from the verdicts. Partial discounting is especially relevant here because it is a familiar response to unwelcome information, and Wittlin already identifies it as a live possibility in discussing this experiment. The alternative is not an elaborate mechanism invented solely to evade the result.

There is an important limitation to what “complete exclusion” means in this argument. A consideration against conviction need not be a positive motivating reason for a verdict of conviction. A judge might consider it, weigh it against stronger considerations, and convict despite it. It would be misleading to say that she convicted *for* the reason that pointed toward acquittal. Model B instead says that everyone retains the excluded consideration in the deliberative basis from which the verdict is reached. The consideration remains part of what the judge takes the case for and against conviction to be.

This distinction makes the correction more exact. Sekine's passage moves from verdict frequencies to an ability to exclude considerations in deliberation, and then connects that ability with refraining from acting for a reason for a reason. The models directly challenge the first move. If exclusion is understood more narrowly as the absence of an excluded positive motive for the final action, the verdict frequencies fare no better. A judge's conviction might lack the prohibited pro-acquittal motive simply because that motive lost to stronger considerations. Its absence as a positive explanation of conviction would not show that the judge refrained from acting on it *because of the exclusionary rule*. Losing a contest of reasons is not the same achievement as being excluded from that contest.

We should therefore distinguish accepting the rule, reducing reliance in response to it, and wholly excluding the target consideration. Model B allows the first two. It does not portray the agents as wholly unresponsive to reasons about reasons. Indeed, accepting that model as psychologically possible may support an interesting weaker claim about control. What it prevents is treating evidence of reduced influence as already establishing the stronger achievement of complete exclusion. A defence that needs only some modification of deliberation should say so and defend that weaker target directly.

The problem also extends beyond the familiar warning against moving from groups to individuals. Suppose we somehow learned that a particular judge's verdict would be the same with and without exposure. In the second model, a judge with a baseline score of 0.4 convicts both before and after subtracting 0.291. She nevertheless counts the extra consideration. A judge with a baseline score of −0.1 acquits in both conditions while treating the consideration as additional support for acquittal. Outcome stability identifies neither the absence of reliance nor the explanation of its absence.

Now compare either person with a judge who would count the information if it were admissible, but excludes it in response to the rule. That judge might also give the same verdict in every observed condition because the remaining case is decisive. Here there is successful exclusion without a verdict change. Stable decisions can accordingly conceal either successful restriction or continued reliance. What matters is not simply which verdict occurs, but how the consideration enters the agent's response and why its role changes or stays fixed.

This is compatible with different philosophical accounts of acting for reasons. Keeling's account of second-order basing explicitly combines a causal role with an appraisal of the lower-order reason's appropriateness. One need not accept all its details to see the measurement issue: an outcome does not by itself reveal that relationship. The present argument supplies no replacement theory of basing. [Keeling 2024, §§3.1–3.3](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.13069)

What follows for the debate? First, the two models establish compatibility, not equal plausibility. Independent psychological evidence might make the mixture model more credible than universal partial discounting. With such evidence, the observed frequencies might probabilistically support the existence of complete excluders. My argument does not show that the experiment has no evidential value. It shows which claim the frequencies fail to establish on their own and exposes an assumption needed for a stronger inference.

Second, no finding about these particular judges' actual capacities follows from the second model. It is a counterexample to an inference, not a diagnosis. Even if nobody in the experiment achieved complete exclusion, it would remain possible that they could do so with more time, better conditions, or a different form of control. A fortiori, the result would not establish a logical impossibility. Sekine's resistance to that escalation is entirely compatible with the correction offered here.

Third, the distinction does not render empirical research into reason-responsiveness hopeless. It helps specify which observations would discriminate between proposed explanations. One could vary the apparent strength of a consideration while holding its inadmissibility fixed, compare cases where the remaining evidence is decisive with cases close to a decision threshold, and investigate how agents respond when the restriction is removed. A simple partial-weight model and a simple complete-exclusion model would then make different predictions. Such tests would need independent assumptions about stable preferences, understanding, and attention. Reports of reasoning would be additional evidence, not transparent access to a person's motives.

There is already research that looks beyond compliance alone. Hoeft, Kurschilgen and Mill study how authoritative requests affect information acquisition in a modified dictator game; their discussion considers alternative explanations for the observed behaviour. That work should prevent any claim that process-sensitive investigation is a new recommendation here. It also illustrates a relevant distinction: declining to acquire information and excluding a consideration already acquired are different routes by which a norm can shape deliberation. Success on one task does not automatically measure success on the other. [Hoeft, Kurschilgen and Mill 2025, §§1, 3, 6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irle.2024.106235)

The useful conclusion is consequently restrained. The general method of comparing models with the same observable implications is inherited, as are the distinctions between discounting and discarding, and between a verdict and its basis. Putting them together exposes a specific unsupported step: an intermediate rate of conviction need not contain any complete excluder. A population in which everybody still counts the forbidden consideration can produce exactly that rate. Establishing the capacity at issue requires a further argument about the mechanism linking a rule to the agent's reasons. Verdicts are evidence about what agents do. Their connection to what agents do *for a reason* must itself be defended.
