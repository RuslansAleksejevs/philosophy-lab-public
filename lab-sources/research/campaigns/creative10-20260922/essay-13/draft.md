# A Work Can Have a Genealogy without Having One Personal Time

A sculpture's mold is sent into the past. There it produces an authorized cast of the same work. The bronze has not traveled from the future, but the form and its authority have. If this counts as the work traveling, what is the work's personal time? The question becomes harder when the mold has several descendants. A source may be earlier than each descendant in the order of production without either descendant being earlier than the other in that order.

This exposes a limit on deriving personal time from the causal relations that preserve something. Those relations may determine how its realizations descend from one another without determining a complete chronology of those realizations. The limit matters for Cei Maslen's extension of causal personal time to multiply instantiated artworks. My claim is conditional and narrow: if that extension is meant to give one determinate scalar chronology for the work, identity-preserving causal provenance does not by itself supply it. A partial order or several histories may be adequate instead. Accepting either is a clarification of what the extension delivers, not a defeat for time travel.

## From persistence to order

Personal time helps describe a traveler who departs in 2050 and arrives in 1950. Arrival follows departure in the traveler's career even though its public date is earlier. The resulting distinction need not introduce a second physical dimension of time. It can distinguish two orderings of events already located in one history.

Maslen's main concern is how to extend the framework to travel by minds without their bodies. In section 4 she considers entities with different persistence conditions, including a sculpture with multiple instances. Her example sends a mold into ancient Greece to make an authorized instance of *The Thinker*. She then explores causation as a general basis for personal order, while conceding that it does not supply a duration metric. Without that metric, causal order alone cannot identify the familiar kind of forward travel in which little personal time accompanies much external time. She also quotes Wasserman's more specific appeal to causation that makes for identity. These qualifications matter: her discussion should not be treated as a claim that every causal influence measures personal time. [Maslen 2024, §4](https://doi.org/10.3390/philosophies9040098).

There are nevertheless two different gaps between causation and a complete chronology. We must select the relevant causal links. We must then establish what temporal structure those links determine. Solving the first problem does not automatically solve the second.

A familiar example illustrates the first. An older traveler teaches her younger self something that contributes to the younger person's subsequent development. Development runs toward the older stage; teaching runs back toward the younger stage. Both endpoints belong to one person, yet the combined causal links form a cycle. A scalar coordinate cannot increase strictly all the way around it.

The example is not a discovery. Lewis discusses both the causal basis of continuity and information passed between a traveler's older and younger stages. Van Cleve explicitly distinguishes causal loops involving a person's stages from a circular life. Their discussions already prevent the easy mistake that everything caused by a person must be personally later than its cause. [Lewis 1976, pp. 148–149](https://andrewmbailey.com/dkl/Paradoxes_of_Time_Travel.pdf); [Van Cleve, pp. 1–3](https://dornsife.usc.edu/james-van-cleve/wp-content/uploads/sites/272/2023/10/No_Loop_in_Looper_for_JVC_website.pdf).

Nor is restricting attention to identity-making causation a new repair. Hall's presentation of Wasserman's account already specifies the relevant immanent causal relation. It is that selected relation, rather than every influence between the object's stages, which is supposed to determine personal order. I grant this restriction. The question is whether granting it gives the desired structure in the artwork case. [Hall 2014, pp. 147–149](https://logos-and-episteme.acadiasi.ro/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/IN-DEFENSE-OF-THE-COMPOSSIBILITY-OF-PRESENTISM-AND-TIME-TRAVEL.pdf).

## Two descendants of one work

Consider a fictional sculpture, *The Listener*. An artist authorizes multiple casts. A master mold and its authorization provide the source for two independently usable daughter molds. One daughter remains in 2050 and produces cast A in 2051. The other travels to 1850 and produces cast B there. The production of A does not depend on the production of B, or vice versa. Neither cast is subsequently used to make the other. The history is fixed; no one changes an already settled past.

The pertinent distinctions are these. A and B are numerically different bronze objects. By the example's stipulation, they instantiate the same authorized work. Finally, their authorized production has a directed provenance. None of these statements implies either of the others merely by definition. In particular, saying that the work is one does not identify its casts with each other.

Represent a few events in this history: S is the preparation of the common source; A and B are the two successful realization events. The intervening daughter-mold events can be restored, but suppressing them makes the structure clear:

S → A

S → B

These arrows stand for the selected succession of authorized production, not arbitrary causal influence. The nodes are realization events, not automatically temporal parts of an abstract artwork. That representation earns its relevance from the proposed case of travel: if producing an authorized instance in the past is how the work arrives there, realization events are among the events whose places in its history the account needs to explain.

I do not assume that authorized production is literally persistence of one material cast. It plainly is not. The argument instead grants the permissive interpretation under examination: such production can carry the identity of the work. If this interpretation is rejected, the mold story no longer establishes time travel by the work in the intended way. If it is accepted, both branches qualify by the same criterion. The example adds no disputed fission of a human being.

The selected relation places S before A and before B. It does not place A before B or B before A. The shared source gives common ancestry, not directed succession between descendants. The fact that B has the earlier external date supplies a public chronology, but the point of personal time was precisely that its order could differ from that chronology.

One can certainly number the events S = 0, B = 1, A = 2. One can equally number them S = 0, A = 1, B = 2. Both assignments respect every selected causal arrow. The disagreement is not about whether the interval between two settled positions takes one year or ten. It is about which descendant comes first. Thus the absence of a duration metric is not the only respect in which the causal data leave a scalar chronology unsettled.

This does not show that the account cannot detect backward travel. S precedes B along the selected provenance route, while B's external date precedes S's. That comparable pair already supplies a reversal. Both numberings preserve it. The unsupported extra step would be to take the successful diagnosis of that route as evidence that the work's entire personal chronology has thereby been determined.

## What the arrows establish

An elementary finite-graph observation helps keep the claims separate. Suppose a finite set of events has selected directed links, and we seek coordinates that strictly increase along those links.

If there is a directed cycle, no such coordinates exist: following its inequalities eventually requires a number to be less than itself. If there is no directed cycle, coordinates do exist. Repeatedly choose an event with no predecessor among the remaining events, give it the next integer, and remove it. An acyclic finite graph always permits such a choice; otherwise following predecessors in the finite remainder would eventually produce a cycle.

This constructs an injective total ranking. It does not show that the graph determines that ranking. Where two events are incomparable even after all chains are taken into account, either can be ranked first. Adding a link from the first to the second cannot create a cycle unless a chain already runs back; incomparability excludes that chain. The reverse added link is equally consistent. Consequently both total rankings have extensions respecting the original links.

Three different achievements are therefore in play. An increasing assignment respects the links. An injective total ranking additionally decides an order for every distinct pair. A faithful representation of the partial order would also avoid inventing comparisons absent from that order. A single line can provide the second without providing the third. Allowing equal numbers does not generally solve the issue either: causal incomparability need not behave like equality. For example, X may precede Y while Z is incomparable with both. Assigning equality to every incomparable pair would make X equal to Z and Z equal to Y, contradicting the required difference between X and Y.

These are familiar mathematical facts, not a proposed mathematical discovery. Their role is diagnostic. Choosing constitutive links addresses which dependencies matter. Acyclicity addresses whether they can all be respected by an increasing assignment. Comparability addresses whether they settle every pair's rank. Those are distinct requirements. The fact that a relation preserves the identity of a work establishes none of the latter two simply as a matter of logic.

Only finite selections of events are needed for this objection. There is no claim here about how every possible continuous or infinite history can be represented by real numbers. Nor do a few sampled events prove that a real history lacks omitted connecting links. The fictional case stipulates independent production routes precisely to exclude the missing path that would otherwise resolve its incomparability.

## Is this merely the wrong way to divide the work?

The strongest response changes the objects being ordered. Perhaps a stage of *The Listener* consists of all its instances at one external time. The earlier aggregate contains B; a later aggregate contains A, and perhaps other instances. Such aggregates can be ordered by their external dates. The complete artwork need never divide into two competing stages.

This is a legitimate proposal for the work's history, and the genealogy argument does not prove it false. It does, however, change what supplies the ordering. External dates now collect the instances and order the collections. The reverse-directed provenance from S to B is a relation across those aggregates, running against their public order. If every relevant provenance link must increase in the aggregate's personal time, that external ordering will not qualify. If the link is instead excluded from aggregate persistence, the proponent must explain how its exclusion fits the claim that it is what carries the work into the past.

The proponent can give a two-level account: an externally ordered history of the whole work, together with a personal order for the particular transmission route. That is coherent and may be the best answer. But it confirms the need to say which history is being given personal time. The aggregate's chronology and the backward route are doing different jobs. Calling both the work's time does not make one scalar order perform both jobs automatically.

Could different aggregates, formed without external simultaneity, do better? Possibly. The result does not exclude every construction. Yet deciding which branch events belong together would then be additional work for the theory. The arrows S → A and S → B do not themselves specify that grouping. One might align events by equal distance from their common source, but equal numbers of selected links depend on how finely events were divided; subdividing a production process should not change when the work is personally simultaneous with itself. Independent physical clocks could improve such an alignment, at the cost of adding structure beyond causal provenance.

A second response assigns personal time to each cast. Each bronze object can have an ordinary career; the daughter mold that traveled can have its own unusual career. Again, there is no contradiction. What is relinquished is the automatic passage from these individual histories to one personal chronology of the multiply instantiated work. The identity of an artwork and the identity of one bronze object answer different questions.

## A partial history may be enough

A more economical response accepts the provenance relation as a partial personal order. On this account it is true that B is downstream from S, while the question whether A is personally earlier than B has no answer until a particular route or convention is chosen. The account retains enough information to locate the backward transmission without manufacturing a global rank for the two descendants.

If this is all Maslen's general ordering proposal intends, the branching example supplies a clarification rather than a counterexample. A partial order is an order. No argument should infer from the absence of a total chronology that causation delivers nothing temporal. The correction is instead to distinguish the scope of the result: causal provenance may supply determinate comparisons sufficient for some travel verdicts while leaving other comparisons open.

Alternatively, personal times may belong to selected lineages of realization. Along S-to-A and S-to-B, the relevant events have successive places. Asking for the work's personal time then needs a further specification of lineage. This is not necessarily an undesirable ambiguity. It may faithfully reflect a work whose identity permits several realizations.

Neither answer solves the familiar lack of a duration metric, and neither needs to. The choices concern order before measurement. A causal account can be precise about its output even when that output is weaker than the complete chronology initially suggested by an ordinary life.

The wider problem of branching personal persistence is already established. Gustafsson, for example, distinguishes relevant temporal relations among person-stages and invokes personal time in separating fission from the presence of younger and older stages of a traveler. My objection is not that philosophers have overlooked branching. It concerns a different inferential direction: given an accepted multiplicity of realizations, what order follows from their provenance? [Gustafsson, “Non-Branching Personal Persistence,” especially note 8 and the discussion of simultaneous stages](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/131934/8/Gustafsson2018_Article_Non_branchingPersonalPersisten.pdf).

This direction matters because a non-branching condition on persons cannot simply be imported into the artwork example. Multiple instantiation was part of the reason for broadening the account. To exclude the branching history one must either explain why authorized derivation fails to carry the work's identity or restrict the bearer of personal time to something less than the entire work. Both are substantive choices about the extension.

The resulting limitation is modest. Maslen's main project of interpreting transmigration stories need not fail. A single mind's selected career may have exactly the order the account needs. Nor does the mold case establish any practical possibility of time travel. It shows something about the proposed explanation: identity-preserving provenance and a complete personal chronology are different achievements. For a multiply realized work, the first can be available without the second. A general account should therefore specify whether it offers a partial history of the work, separate histories of its lineages, or a further construction that unites them. Its success with a particular backward route cannot make that choice on its behalf.
