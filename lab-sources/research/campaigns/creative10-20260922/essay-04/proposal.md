# Proposal p1: The losses caused by a truthful correction

## Exact thesis

A relay of testimony can be epistemically negligent despite transmitting only truths, preserving current justification, and later promptly issuing an accurate correction. The distinctive failure occurs when it unnecessarily destroys a cheap distinction needed to tell which of its messages a foreseeable source-specific defeater affects. The result is **collateral loss of usable support**: downstream listeners must suspend reliance on unaffected testimony as well as affected testimony. Under specified conditions, a duty to correct therefore imposes an earlier duty to preserve *correction-relevant distinctions*, not merely a later duty to utter a retraction. This is an audience-facing, defeasible norm for responsible relaying, not a proposed constitutive definition of assertion.

## Argument and controlled example

A community information desk relays two independently checked reports: the north footbridge is open (p) and the south footbridge is open (q). One report comes from inspection team A and the other from equally reliable team B. There are two possible histories: H1 assigns A to p and B to q; H2 reverses the assignment. The desk knows the assignment when reporting but destroys it. Its downstream report and retained record are otherwise identical in the two histories. At that point both claims are true, both sources are competent, and the recipients have equally good current warrant for each. Team A subsequently discovers that its route-assessment procedure on that occasion was defective. Its information is now unreliable, but this does not establish that its conclusion was false. B's unaffected report remains good evidence. The bridge conditions have not changed.

The desk immediately and truthfully announces A's evidential defect. It cannot reconstruct which report was A's; the teams' detailed records are now unavailable for independent reasons known in advance. It has fully retracted any commitment to the reliability of A's contribution. Yet its audience cannot identify which particular bridge retains unaffected support. A precise pair of withdrawals is available if the assignment was retained: remove reliance on p in H1, and on q in H2. Any correction policy restricted to the desk's retained information must produce the same message in H1 and H2. It therefore cannot tell recipients the correct assignment in both. A cautious policy guaranteeing that no report is treated as independently supported when it is not must flag both p and q. The collateral loss is not falsity produced by correction; it is loss of the ability to exploit one intact piece of testimony selectively. The disjunction that at least one report has intact support can remain known.

**Formal bounded result.** Let h range over a specified set of possible testimonial histories; r(h) is the information retained by a relay and its reachable audience; d is a later defeater notice; T(h,d) is the set of report tokens whose support the notice defeats. An exact support-withdrawal procedure F(r(h),d)=T(h,d) exists only if whenever r(h1)=r(h2), T(h1,d)=T(h2,d) for every admitted d. Conversely that condition is sufficient for a well-defined F in this idealized finite model. When it fails, a no-missed-defeat policy must flag the union of the possible T sets; its avoidable extra flags can remove useful reliance. This is an elementary information constraint, not a new theorem of belief revision or an algorithmic discovery.

Normative bridge: a relay inviting consequential reliance, foreseeing this defect class and the loss of reconstruction, and able to retain the needed distinction at negligible cost without overriding competing duties, should not impose the predictable burden of avoidable overcorrection on recipients. This principle concerns an avoidable loss created by one's own representation choice. It does not infer an unlimited duty to record from mere physical possibility. It is defended by comparison with retaining the distinction, with no difference in original truth, initial justification, eventual honesty, privacy, or effort relevant enough to justify its destruction.

## Defended increment, beyond earlier rejected formulation

The initial question's claim that retraction is not uniform belief deletion is rejected as a contribution: MacFarlane already allows continued belief on independent grounds, and Egan explicitly rejects simply reversing common-ground intersection. Dependency tracking likewise belongs to established truth-maintenance and provenance research. The proposed increment is the combination of (i) a precisely stated criterion for which distinctions matter, (ii) a paired-history counterexample in which impeccable original and corrective content still causes avoidable collateral epistemic loss, and (iii) a norm assessing the *relay before error is discovered* by the selectivity of the corrections it leaves possible. The distinguishing result is that truthfulness now plus sincere correction later can fail to discharge a relay's audience-facing epistemic responsibility, even without deceptive assertion, stubbornness, or failure to broadcast correction.

## Closest comparisons

- John MacFarlane, *What Is Assertion?* (2009 manuscript; published 2011), pp. 6–7: retraction need not intend disbelief where a hearer has independent grounds. Accepted background, not novelty. My case holds truth and initial entitlement fixed but removes the ability to locate the independent ground later.
- Andy Egan, *Assertion, Retraction, and Communication* (2023 handout), pp. 3–4; later chapter *Assertion and Retraction* (2024, abstract only): context reversal loses intervening information; enduring endorsed constraints offer a better model. My proposal concerns information lost by a relay that prevents selective correction, not what the illocutionary effect of retracting must be. His handout explicitly leaves changes in common ground complicated and open; I do not claim to refute his account.
- Jon Doyle, *Historical annotations and humble databases* (1979; substantial search excerpt only, direct PDF failed): records enable correction and responsibility to sources. This is strong adverse overlap. I cannot claim novelty for a general obligation to retain provenance. The proposed advance is the exact distinction criterion and collateral-loss counterexample separating original and corrective truth from preservation of later selective entitlement. This may prove too modest; independent critic must decide.
- Cheney, Ahmed and Acar, *Provenance as Dependency Analysis* (2007/2009), introduction and sections 1.1–1.2: dependency correctness links outputs to relevant inputs; minimal provenance can be uncomputable. I offer no improved provenance calculus, complete-history rule, or computational minimality result. I ask what a speaker owes recipients when loss of a small, foreseeable distinction shifts avoidable epistemic costs to them.
- Matthew Vermaire, *Retraction and testimonial justification* (2020; abstract only): canceled illocutionary responsibility need not remove testimonial justification. My account likewise separates normative retraction from epistemic result; its target is avoidable loss across relayed messages, not the assurance view.

## Objections to develop if released

The precautionary guarantee is not the universal norm of ordinary belief: recipients can instead make probabilistic decisions. State this restriction, and show that loss of selective support persists even when certainty is not required. The desk may recheck bridges; then the informational impossibility no longer applies, but rechecking is a cost the lost record can make avoidable. Full source names are unnecessary: anonymous stable labels plus a retained mapping or trusted intermediary suffice. Privacy may defeat the duty; it does not show every correction-relevant distinction identifies a person. No complete personal archive or endless preservation is implied; the admitted defeaters, stakes, costs and duration bound the norm. A completely unconditional relay guarantee would be indefensible.

Russian summary: даже правдивый пересказ и честное последующее исправление могут подвести слушателя, если пересказчик заранее стёр простую связь, позволявшую исправить только повреждённую часть сообщения. Но общий совет хранить происхождение сведений давно известен; новизну более узкого результата ещё должен проверить независимый критик.
