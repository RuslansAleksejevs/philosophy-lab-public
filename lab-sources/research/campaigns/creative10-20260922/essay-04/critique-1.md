## Reconstruction and verdict

The strongest version of the argument is comparative. A relay supplies initially warranted testimony and invites consequential reliance. An inexpensive mapping, under its exclusive control, would let it localize a foreseeable source defect. Losing that mapping does not make its original assertions false, and its later disclosure can still be entirely honest. Nevertheless, the loss can prevent recipients from identifying surviving grounds that they would otherwise be able to use. Under a suitable responsibility principle, the relay owes reasonable precautions against creating this avoidable burden.

The information argument in §3 establishes a conditional limitation: exact targeting cannot be recovered from inputs that conflate histories with different targets. It does not by itself establish the normative premise. The draft expressly recognizes this, which is a strength. Its numerical example also correctly shows how uncertainty about the assignment can lower the highest available route-specific confidence without requiring total disbelief.

**Recommendation: revise and defend.** The stipulated bridge case supports a plausible, limited duty. I find no fatal internal contradiction. The principal weakness is that the argument for a duty owed to recipients remains less precise than the information argument. Its general formulation moves too easily from a clean, virtually costless case to a standard for ordinary relays. Repair should preserve the earlier, audience-directed responsibility while making its basis and limits explicit.

## 1. Major: inviting reliance does not yet distinguish obligation from worthwhile assistance

Location: §4, especially “The norm is directed toward recipients because the relay has chosen to make its report useful as a substitute for their separate inquiries.”

Many voluntary acts make another person's inquiry unnecessary without creating an obligation to preserve future usefulness. Suppose an unpaid walker passes on the same two warranted reports, knowing that the listener may act on them. The walker can retain a small mapping in memory or a note. Does giving this assistance create the same directed obligation as an organized information desk? Simply intending one's communication to be useful seems insufficient to explain the difference, if there is one. If it is sufficient, the resulting norm reaches much further into ordinary helpful speech than the institutional example suggests.

The draft adds exclusive control, cheap retention and foreseeable consequences. These establish a powerful reason to retain the distinction. They do not yet establish why a particular recipient has a claim against the speaker, rather than why the speaker has an undirected reason to improve a beneficial service. “It would be unfair” names the disputed judgment but does not fully explain it. Nor does the listener's failure to accept every avoidable risk automatically create a right against whoever could reduce that risk: acceptance is not required for every permissible imperfection in an unsolicited benefit.

There are at least two defensible routes. An undertaking account would explain why inviting reliance on a bulletin through a specified period commits the desk to reasonable maintenance of its evidential usefulness. An induced-dependence account would explain how the desk's invitation foreseeably substitutes for recipients' own investigation and therefore creates responsibility for manageable repair costs. These routes have different scopes. In particular, the latter needs more than the fact that the report is useful; it needs warranted dependence or displacement of reasonable precautions. Choose a route, state its normative premise, and defend it with the strongest informal-relay case. It is legitimate to defend the wider norm, but the wider commitment should be acknowledged.

This repair need not erase the contribution. The distinctive application remains that reasonable maintenance can require preserving a relation between reports before any defect is discovered, even when original assertion and later correction are faultless in their specified respects.

## 2. Major: the relation to a duty to correct needs a clear verdict

Location: proposal's claim that “a duty to correct therefore imposes an earlier duty”; draft §§4 and 6.

The final manuscript is more careful than the proposal: it identifies an additional responsibility and says a later broad correction may fulfill the duty then applicable. That is defensible. But it should explicitly settle whether the preservation duty is derived from a duty to correct or from the independent duty of reasonable care just discussed.

Consider a relay whose acknowledged corrective undertaking is to announce every defect it learns about, with enough information to prevent unwarranted confident reliance. After losing the mapping, it does exactly that through a broad notice. Nothing about this undertaking alone establishes a further requirement to preserve every unaffected opportunity for reliance. The latter is a richer service. The desk might owe it, but that requires the additional premise; it is not contained in honesty or disclosure as such.

Conversely, the same preservation concern would arise if the later event were an upgrade of one source's reliability rather than a defeater: losing the mapping could prevent recipients from finding a newly well-supported option. That suggests the underlying responsibility concerns maintaining the usable discrimination supplied by a communicative arrangement, of which correction is one important case.

The most economical repair is a sentence identifying the duty as a defeasible precaution required by reasonable stewardship of invited reliance, rather than a logical entailment of the obligation to retract. Explain why correction cases expose this duty particularly well. No enlarged theory of all evidence updates is necessary. This makes the philosophical payoff honest: assertion-time warrant and later honesty are insufficient dimensions of assessment, not inconsistent norms that the paper has refuted.

## 3. Major for generalization: foreseeability and low unit cost do not settle a retention policy

Location: §4's boxed norm and the claim that the desk takes an avoidable risk even when no defect occurs.

The bridge example stipulates away any relevant offsetting benefit to erasure. In that case, retention is easy to defend. But the general norm speaks of a “foreseeable evidential defect” and “cheap retention.” These are not jointly sufficient without a policy-level proportionality judgment.

Imagine a relay issuing thousands of reports daily. A source-level defect, instrument-level defect, occasion-level defect, training-cohort defect and interpretation-specific defect are each recognizable possibilities. Every extra classification is individually inexpensive to retain. Their collection and verification can nevertheless be costly, and a mistaken mapping can misdirect later correction. A duty to preserve each cheap distinction can thus produce an unreasonable total requirement. Alternatively, a very improbable defect may have only a tiny expected effect even though its type is foreseeable and the invited decision is consequential.

The “competing responsibility” exception does not obviously cover ordinary time and resource costs, or the need to select a workable policy. The surrounding discussion partly does. Incorporate that discussion into the norm itself: reasonable total costs, foreseeable likelihood and severity, feasible retention policies, and the cost of reliable retrieval matter. No numerical expected-utility theory is required. The needed distinction is between a clearly costless omission in the controlled case and a presumption applicable under real tradeoffs. Avoid describing every recognizable defect as one against which inexpensive unit precautions are owed.

## 4. Repairable technical issue: notices must be compatible with histories

Location: §3, condition quantified over every admitted notice.

The equivalence condition should apply only to histories compatible with both the retained information and the received notice. A truthful notice can itself eliminate a possible history. For example, a notice that A's north-bridge inspection used a defective procedure already identifies a target, despite the earlier erasure. The current definition of retained information as everything still usable when the notice arrives might absorb this point, but then the separation between retained information and notice becomes unclear.

State that two histories require the same answer only if they could yield the same retained record **and the same notice**, and define the conservative union over those jointly compatible histories. This leaves the bridge example intact because its notice deliberately supplies no assignment. It also prevents the formal result from seeming to demand retention of distinctions that the later signal itself would restore.

Do not add elaborate formalism. The draft's elementary factorization argument is adequate once its domain is explicit.

## 5. Scope of the loss: preserve the careful formulation

Location: §§1, 3 and 5.

A critic should not object that the unaffected report remains true, or that recipients can retain probabilistic confidence. The manuscript already distinguishes truth, surviving original support, and accessible present grounds. The paired-history case concerns the last of these, and the probabilistic example supplies a coherent decision-relevant loss.

Nevertheless, the norm should retain the qualification “unnecessarily depriving recipients of access” rather than drift into a general duty to preserve all original support relations. A surviving independent source or an inexpensive fresh check may make the old mapping irrelevant to every invited use. In those cases, the formal target sets still differ while the morally relevant loss disappears. Thus failure of the §3 condition is a diagnosis of unavailable exact targeting, not a sufficient test of epistemic negligence. The paper largely says this; one explicit sentence connecting §§3 and 4 would prevent misreading.

## Revision priorities and remaining uncertainty

1. Explain the directed obligation through a clear undertaking or induced-dependence premise, and test its reach with an informal helpful relay.
2. Say explicitly that correction alone does not entail the preservation duty; identify the additional responsibility principle.
3. Make retention proportional at the level of feasible policies, not merely cheap individual distinctions.
4. Restrict the targeting condition to histories compatible with the same notice.

These revisions preserve the promising claim instead of retreating to “records can be useful.” The strongest final version would show why recipients can complain about a specifically avoidable impairment of their reliance, while allowing faultless broad correction and reasonable limits on preservation.

Unresolved uncertainties: this review does not establish the thesis's originality, independently verify the cited passages, or resolve externalist accounts on which inaccessible historical support can sustain some justification. The latter need not defeat the access-based conclusion. The central unresolved philosophical issue is the normative basis and scope of the recipient's claim. On that issue the author should defend a bounded premise rather than present the information argument as its substitute.
