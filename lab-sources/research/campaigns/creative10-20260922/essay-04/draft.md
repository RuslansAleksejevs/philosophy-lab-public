# What a Truthful Relay Can Owe Its Audience

An information desk reports two facts. Both are true, and its evidence reasonably supports both. Later it discovers a defect in one of its sources. It promptly tells everyone who received its report. It conceals nothing, resists no challenge, and leaves no known error unannounced. Has it fulfilled its epistemic responsibility to its audience?

Not necessarily. It may earlier have destroyed the distinction needed to identify which part of its report the defect affects. Its correction then leaves recipients unable to use testimony whose original support remains intact. The desk has discharged one responsibility while making another unnecessarily difficult to discharge.

I argue for a limited responsibility of testimonial relays: when they invite consequential reliance, they should preserve inexpensive distinctions needed for foreseeable selective correction, unless adequate alternatives or competing reasons justify losing them. The failure this norm identifies is neither lying nor refusing to correct. It is imposing an avoidable loss of selectively usable support through an earlier choice about how information is passed on and retained.

## 1. Two reports and one lost distinction

Suppose a community desk issues a morning bulletin: the north footbridge is open, and the south footbridge is open. Team A inspected one bridge; equally well-regarded team B inspected the other. The desk receives the reports separately and knows their assignment. Recipients know the bulletin combines one report from each team. The reports are true. The available evidence reasonably warrants accepting each team's assessment.

The desk keeps the two conclusions but erases the assignment of teams to bridges. Retaining that assignment would require almost no effort. The desk knows that the teams' temporary working records will soon become inaccessible, so later reconstruction cannot be taken for granted. It nevertheless invites recipients to rely on its bulletin throughout the relevant period.

Later that morning, team A discovers an unnoticed fault in the procedure used for its assessment. The fault defeats reliance on that assessment, without showing its conclusion false. Team B's procedure is unaffected. The bridges themselves have not changed. The desk immediately circulates an accurate notice explaining the problem. However, neither the desk nor its recipients can now establish which bridge A inspected.

Compare two histories. In the first, A reported on the north bridge and B on the south. In the second, the assignment was reversed. The original bulletin, remaining records, and later notice are the same in both. If the assignment had survived, the desk could identify the report requiring reassessment while retaining the other as supported by the unaffected inspection. Now it cannot do so.

Several losses must be kept apart. There is no stipulated loss of truth: both bridges can remain open. There is no destruction of B's original assessment. There is a loss of the ability to identify a particular conclusion as supported by that assessment. Knowing that one of two reports retains such support does not tell a recipient which route has it. A person needing a supported choice of route can be left with two doubtful options where a better-preserved record would supply one.

Nor should we describe the correction itself as an epistemic wrong. Concealing A's problem would not restore the missing distinction. It would leave recipients unaware of an evidential defect. The correction exposes the practical importance of a loss produced by erasure together with the source defect. The desk should still issue it.

The example therefore separates three achievements: having adequate grounds when transmitting information, honestly reporting a later defect, and preserving the means of localizing that defect. The first two do not guarantee the third.

## 2. What is inherited, and what remains to argue

Problems about lost sources are not new. Matthew Frise's discussion of preservationism includes two subjects who have forgotten that their matching beliefs came from different textbooks. The same later news discredits one textbook. He uses the resulting difficulty about defeat to challenge an account of memory justification (2017, §III, pp. 492–494). The paired-history structure here is indebted to that precedent. My recipients, however, know that one of their two reports came from the discredited source. Their problem is locating a known defect within a specified pool, not discovering an unnoticed connection to a forgotten source. The question I pursue is what responsibility a relay bears for making that localization impossible.

Retraction also need not demand wholesale disbelief. MacFarlane explicitly allows an audience to retain a retracted claim on independent grounds (2009, pp. 6–7). Egan rejects treating retraction as simply restoring the possibilities removed by an earlier assertion: intervening contributions matter. His alternative tracks enduring endorsements of constraints on a conversational context (2023, pp. 3–4). These points explain why successful withdrawal of one's endorsement cannot be equated with a uniform change in everyone's beliefs.

The general importance of preserving provenance is older still. Doyle's *Historical Annotations and Humble Databases* connects historical records with the ability to correct information and trace responsibility. Its available excerpt also gives that preservation a social, not merely technical, motivation. I therefore claim no discovery that future correction can justify present recordkeeping. Cheney, Ahmed and Acar likewise develop dependency-sensitive provenance, including the difference between precise dependency information and conservative approximations (2009, §§1–1.2).

The narrower issue is a particular responsibility to recipients: avoiding unnecessary loss of access to *unaffected* support when a correction becomes necessary. A relay's original and corrective statements can be impeccable in the respects specified above, while its treatment of distinctions between reports falls short. Establishing this requires more than recommending useful records. We need to identify the loss precisely and explain why its avoidance is owed rather than merely desirable.

## 3. A limit on selective correction

Consider a fixed set of possible histories and a fixed range of later notices about evidential defects. Let a history include which original report depended on which source. Let the retained information include whatever the relay and its reachable audience can still use when a notice arrives. It includes usable memory and access to other records, not just the desk's own file.

For a history *h* and notice *d*, write *T(h,d)* for the set of original report tokens whose original support the notice undermines. This concerns support relations, not the complete present justification of every belief. Once uncertainty spreads, recipients' total entitlement may change more widely than *T* does.

An exact procedure for identifying *T* from retained information is possible only under this condition:

> Whenever two histories leave the same retained information, every admitted defect notice must target the same set of report tokens in both histories.

The reason is elementary. A procedure given identical retained information and the same notice must produce the same answer. If the correct target sets differ, that answer cannot identify both exactly. Conversely, if target sets agree whenever the inputs agree, an exact answer is well-defined for each input. This is a simple condition on what an information representation preserves, not a new mathematical result or an efficient algorithm.

In the bridge case, the condition fails. The same remaining record and notice target the north report in one history and the south report in the other. A policy guaranteeing that it will flag every report whose original support is affected must flag both. More generally, it must include every target appearing in a history compatible with its information. This can include reports whose original support is actually unaffected.

Calling those extra reports “unaffected” does not mean that recipients remain fully entitled to believe them. The ambiguity itself can give recipients reason for doubt. The point is comparative: retaining a cheap distinction would have prevented that additional uncertainty. The lost good is identifiable access to surviving support, not a mysterious entitlement that recipients must somehow exercise without being able to find it.

The condition also limits how much history is needed. If all admitted notices would affect two histories in exactly the same way, preserving the difference between those histories is unnecessary for this task. Conversely, retaining a source's name may be useless if what matters is which inspection occasion supplied which conclusion. The relevant unit is whatever distinction separates the effects of the foreseeable notices.

Exact targeting is not always required. A bulletin may legitimately promise only rough guidance, or broad withdrawal may impose negligible costs. The result concerns what information is needed *if* selective withdrawal is to be available. It does not yet establish a duty to preserve that information.

## 4. From a useful distinction to a responsibility

Why should the desk preserve the assignment? Perhaps a detailed archive would be useful, but usefulness alone does not create an obligation. We constantly discard information that someone might later want.

The proposed duty rests on a more specific relation. The desk invites others to treat its compressed report as a basis for consequential decisions. It controls a distinction that recipients cannot preserve for themselves because it has not passed that distinction on. It can cheaply retain the distinction or provide an adequate means of recovering it. A recognizable class of source defects would make the distinction relevant during the period of invited reliance. Erasure then transfers a predictable burden to the audience: reassess two reports, repeat investigation, or give up a supported option, when one report alone required reassessment.

The desk need not promise perfect evidence. Its undertaking is compatible with mistakes, uncertainty, and subsequent withdrawal. But permission to provide fallible information is not permission to make the resulting uncertainty unnecessarily indiscriminate. A recipient who accepts that one inspection may fail has not thereby accepted every avoidable enlargement of that failure's consequences.

This distinction matters to the familiar defense, “They knew the information could be wrong.” That answers a complaint about any risk at all. It does not answer a complaint about adding a separable, cheaply avoidable risk to the process by which errors will be handled. The relevant comparison holds constant the evidence, the original claims, and the later disclosure. Only preservation of the assignment changes. In that comparison, erasure supplies no offsetting benefit yet predictably makes repair less selective.

This supports a defeasible norm:

> A relay inviting consequential reliance should preserve or make recoverable distinctions whose cheap retention would prevent a foreseeable evidential defect from unnecessarily depriving recipients of access to unaffected support, unless an adequate alternative or a competing responsibility justifies their loss.

The norm is directed toward recipients because the relay has chosen to make its report useful as a substitute for their separate inquiries. That choice helps explain why the burden is its responsibility. It would be unfair to invite reliance on a compression, keep sole control of what is needed to repair it selectively, and then treat the resulting repair costs as entirely the audience's problem. This is not an unlimited obligation to maximize the audience's information. It concerns additional burdens generated by a feature of the relay's own communicative arrangement.

A listener might ask why this is an epistemic norm rather than ordinary care applied to a recordkeeping service. It can be both. The loss concerns which reasons people can responsibly use, and the responsibility arises through organizing testimonial dependence. Nothing requires every norm of responsible communication to be unique to communication. The philosophical payoff is identifying a failure that assessments restricted to assertion-time warrant and later corrective honesty leave unresolved.

Notice also the timing. In a world where no source defect emerges, the desk may never cause anyone to abandon a supported option. It still takes an avoidable risk with the audience's future access to its evidence. Whether that is culpable depends on what the desk could reasonably foresee, not on whether the source later happens to fail. The later event reveals the cost of the choice; it does not retroactively invent the reason to preserve the distinction.

## 5. Three boundaries

**Probabilistic reliance.** The targeting argument used a guarantee against overlooking affected reports. Ordinary rational belief does not always require such a guarantee. If recipients assign a high enough probability to each bridge being open, they might reasonably continue believing both claims or choose a route without further inquiry.

This does not refute the comparative loss. In a deliberately simple numerical illustration, suppose identifying a report as B's would give confidence of 0.99, whereas identifying it as A's after the notice would give 0.50. Suppose the two assignments are equally likely and nothing else bears on them. Without the mapping, each bridge receives confidence of 0.745. With the mapping, one receives 0.99. These stipulated numbers illustrate a loss of discriminating information; they are not measurements of testimony or thresholds for knowledge. At some costs of error, the difference changes which actions are reasonable. At other costs, it will not. The norm therefore depends on the uses the relay invites, and not on universal suspension of belief.

A further objection is that more information is sometimes distracting or costly. True. The claim is not that every detail should be displayed to every recipient. A retained mapping that is consulted only when needed can preserve selectivity without burdening the original bulletin. Where even that arrangement is costly, the balancing question returns.

**New inquiry and changed circumstances.** The desk could inspect both bridges again. If it can do so promptly at negligible cost, keeping the old mapping may be unnecessary. Recoverability matters more than literal retention. The impossibility result excludes new evidence that distinguishes the histories; it does not rule out obtaining that evidence.

But the mere possibility of new inquiry does not erase responsibility. If the desk's avoidable erasure forces recipients to pay for repeated inquiry, or leaves them without useful guidance until it is finished, the same burden has taken another form. Nor must every old distinction survive indefinitely. When conditions change so much that neither old inspection is fit for the invited purpose, retaining their assignment no longer preserves the relevant usable support. The duty's duration is bounded by the role the report is reasonably expected to play.

**Privacy and source protection.** Preserving distinctions need not mean disclosing identities. The desk can attach anonymous report labels and retain a secure mapping, or use an intermediary that can return which labels are affected by a defect. What matters to a recipient may be that report 17 requires reassessment, not who supplied it. The informational condition demands distinctions between correction targets, not a complete biography of the testifier.

Sometimes those distinctions themselves endanger a source, or the mapping cannot safely be retained. Such a competing duty can justify erasure. The implication is then that a more limited corrective service is available. The relay may appropriately narrow the reliance it invites, arrange inexpensive fresh checks, or explain that a source problem will require broader withdrawal. The norm does not demand that privacy always yield to traceability. It makes explicit a cost that must be assigned rather than wished away.

## 6. What a retraction leaves unfinished

A critic may finally say that the desk has already warned recipients about everything it knows, so nothing further can be required of it. At the moment of correction, that may be right about what it can now accomplish. An accurate, appropriately broad notice might be its best available response. But the best response after erasure need not make erasure responsible. An agent can satisfy its present obligation under constraints it should earlier have avoided creating.

This is why the argument should not be framed as a more demanding formula for retraction. No clever choice of words can recover a distinction that nobody can now establish. The desk might successfully withdraw its endorsement of A's contribution while failing to supply the audience with a selective route through the resulting uncertainty. Those are different achievements.

The proposed responsibility is correspondingly modest. It does not require preservation of every reason behind every claim, identify a new speech act, or settle how forgotten evidence justifies belief. It asks relays to take responsibility for the avoidable spread of uncertainty caused by their own compression of evidence. Where a small retained distinction would let recipients continue using an unaffected report, truthful assertion followed by truthful correction is not the whole of responsible communication.
