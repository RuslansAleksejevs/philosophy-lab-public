# What a Good Explanation Can Prevent Us from Checking

A critic calls a mystery's ending surprising but well prepared. Asked why, she identifies three descriptions that seem incidental on first reading but jointly support the solution. Her explanation is accurate. The reader follows it, reads the story, and finds all three descriptions exactly where she said they were. He can now explain how the ending fits. Has he become less dependent on her judgement?

In one respect, yes. He can inspect the descriptions and consider their connections. In another respect, something has been lost. His ability to notice the clues after being told their significance does not show how they would have struck him beforehand. The explanation that enables one check removes the opportunity for another.

I will argue that this is a limited but important complication in the relation between critical explanation and independent aesthetic judgement. Accurate guidance can improve understanding while reducing access to evidence about a distinct premise of the evaluation. The resulting dependence is not necessarily a defect, and the reader need not become more credulous. The primary change concerns which routes to checking the critic remain available. A difference in actual reliance follows only under additional conditions about the evidence the reader retains and uses. A good explanation can leave us better able to understand a verdict without leaving us better able to check every part of it.

Consider the mystery more carefully. This is an invented case, not an assessment of any particular novel. A description of a broken latch, a passenger's habit of changing gloves, and an apparently mistaken departure time jointly explain how the crime was committed. Assume that the story presents these details before its solution, that the critic explains their connections correctly, and that the ending achieves the balance she praises. These stipulations make her explanation accurate; they do not grant the reader independent warrant for accepting all of it.

The judgement has at least three components. First, the earlier descriptions support the eventual solution. Second, their presentation does not make that solution obtrusive to an appropriately attentive reader who does not already know it. Third, this combination serves a worthwhile artistic purpose. The first concerns relations among details. For definiteness, understand the second as a claim about a tendency among readers with a specified level of genre experience and attention, not an ideal reader or an exceptionless prediction. It concerns those details as encountered under restricted information. The third evaluates the result. Their combination might justify praise, but none is identical to the others.

Calling a mystery fair sometimes means only that its solution is coherent in retrospect, and sometimes means that readers have a reasonable chance of solving it beforehand. I need not settle that dispute. Even someone who accepts the weaker sense can distinguish preparation from concealment, and ask whether a story has achieved both. Nor is concealment always good. An ending can be surprising because a writer withheld indispensable information; another can be predictable but dramatically powerful. My argument concerns evaluations for which the handling of an uninformed audience is a relevant premise, not a universal standard for successful narrative.

Such premises are not philosophical inventions. Sarah J. Link's analysis of Agatha Christie describes how lists, omissions and categorization guide readers' attention and expectations. The same information can be present without occupying the place in a reader's thinking that it acquires once a solution is known. The distinction between initial and retrospective interpretation is established material, not the contribution of this essay. [Link 2023, sections on form and attention, relevance and visibility.](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-33227-2_4)

Now compare two orders of inquiry. In the first, the reader hears the critic's initial assessment, reads the story without her explanation, and then receives it. In the second, he hears the same assessment and the explanation before reading. Hold fixed his background competence, his available time and the guidance not at issue. In both cases he eventually receives the explanation and examines the story. Only the first gives him an encounter in which he does not already know which descriptions solve the crime. That difference creates an opportunity for evidence; it does not guarantee useful evidence at the end of the inquiry. The story itself eventually reveals its solution. We must distinguish undergoing an uninformed episode, preserving sufficiently reliable evidence of it, and describing it correctly afterward. A retrospective note may already be distorted. The first order can preserve evidence generated before disclosure; the second never generates that particular uninformed episode.

The first order does not deliver an infallible test. Perhaps he guesses the solution by luck. Perhaps he overlooks the latch because he is tired. Perhaps the critic's bare praise itself primes him to look for a twist. These possibilities limit the evidence. They do not erase the difference between the two orders. The comparison does not require an entirely unconditioned reader. It requires a reader who lacks the particular information whose effect is being evaluated, and whose response would have some evidential value for that inquiry.

Suppose the reader has grounds, independent of this critic, for treating his response as some evidence about the specified group. For example, previous comparisons with other readers have established that his early guesses and failures to guess are informative about their responses. This assumption is stronger than merely calling him competent. It makes the proposed observation relevant: different accounts of the work's concealment would make his response more or less expected. A single episode can then support a limited update, even though it cannot settle a population tendency. Without such grounds, the first encounter may tell him only what happened to him.

To specify a possible useful record, suppose he habitually jots down his leading suspect at a pause before the solution. This practice is held fixed across the two inquiry orders, and the response category being assessed allows such pauses. In the first order, his note records a guess formed without knowing the solution or the critic's detailed explanation. A correct early guess may count against unusually effective concealment; a plausible wrong guess may lend it modest support. The inference still depends on his independently established relevance to the audience category. The note neither proves artistic merit nor records every fluctuation of attention. It simply preserves an observation that later knowledge need not erase.

In the second order, finding the descriptions easy to recognize has a different evidential role. It supports their presence and may support the stated explanatory connections. It does not, without further premises, support a claim about how conspicuous they are to someone who lacks the explanation. The observation comes from an altered condition. Conversely, his failure to be surprised does not refute the claim that the ending is surprising to readers without advance guidance. He cannot test that claim by making the very informational change it excludes and treating the result as though nothing relevant had changed.

The inferential problem is quite precise. Let the critic's claim concern responses under condition U: encountering the story without knowing the solution and the role of its clues. The reader's guided recognition occurs under condition G: encountering it with that knowledge. Evidence obtained under G can support a conclusion about U, but it needs a bridge. An account of attention, comparable readers, a reliable memory of earlier experience, or other evidence may supply one. Recognition under G is not itself an observation under U. Calling it first-hand acquaintance does not eliminate the difference.

This is why the case can bear on critical authority. Compare the completed inquiries, not the reader's confidence before and after a single conversation. Suppose that after the first sequence he accepts the critic's response premise with some support from his preserved, independently relevant early guess, as well as her testimony. After the second sequence he accepts the same premise, but his guided guess supplies no comparable support about uninformed uptake. Stipulate that he lacks an adequate independent replacement. His acceptance in this second sequence then rests on the critic's testimony to a greater extent than it does in the first. He need not be completely independent in either sequence.

Other outcomes must be kept separate. He could suspend judgement about the response premise, in which case the lost check produces no actual reliance. Or he could lose the observation while continuing to rely on the critic exactly as much as before. The unconditional result of the specified disclosure is loss of an opportunity. Changed reliance on this critic is a conditional consequence, demonstrated by the comparison just given rather than entailed by every such loss.

The qualification matters. Someone who already accepts a thin verdict entirely on testimony cannot necessarily become more dependent in the sense of placing a greater share of current confidence in the speaker. My claim does not require that increase. It compares opportunities for subsequent checking under different disclosure orders. Nor does every lost opportunity increase dependence: perhaps the reader has ample independent audience evidence already. The claim applies where the unguided encounter would provide relevant evidence and where the explanation removes it without supplying an independently adequate replacement for the response premise.

At the same time, the explanation may reduce dependence elsewhere. The reader can now identify the latch passage, locate the timing discrepancy and work through alternative interpretations. If challenged about the connection among those details, he may answer from the text and his own reasoning. If challenged about their original unobtrusiveness, he may still need to defer. Thus a single successful explanation can improve the checks available for one premise while removing an available check on another. Where the reader continues to accept both premises and lacks an adequate replacement for the lost evidence, the completed inquiries can also differ in actual reliance on the critic. The hearer is neither simply dependent nor simply independent; the relevant question is what he can now check, and on what basis.

For contrast, pointing out a painting's symmetry need not change the conditions relevant to checking whether its shapes are symmetrically arranged. Guided inspection can then provide the required check. The complication arises when a premise concerns response without the particular guidance being given, not whenever a teacher makes something easier to notice.

This does not overturn an account of aesthetic understanding. Alison Hills argues that aesthetic understanding involves being able to respond to reasons oneself, including in further cases. Critical descriptions and explanations can guide the development of that ability; acquaintance and emotional responses can contribute to it. Her claims about guidance are qualified, and my case is compatible with her account. It identifies a circumstance in which guidance helps develop one ability while changing the conditions needed to exercise another evidential capacity. [Hills 2022, sections 5–6.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nous.12344)

Nor is continuing dependence despite understanding a new discovery. Allan Hazlett defends the possibility of receiving understanding through explanation while depending intellectually on the speaker. He distinguishes that situation from one in which supplied information enables the recipient to grasp a connection without depending on the speaker for it. The further point here is causal and comparative: an explanation can remove an available source of checking one premise even as it enables a less dependent grasp of another. [Hazlett 2025, section 2 of the accessible author manuscript.](https://philpapers.org/archive/HAZTUA.pdf)

An obvious objection remains: this is just hindsight bias in literary dress. Baruch Fischhoff's original discussion already connects outcome information with changed assessments of relevance, difficulty reconstructing ignorance and weakened testing of interpretive hypotheses. He even compares backward reasoning with solving a maze from its end and consequently overlooking where a forward solver could get lost. Both the salience distinction and its significance for testing are antecedents that the present argument inherits. [Fischhoff 1975, pp. 297–299.](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Fischhoff_1975_Hindsight_is_not_equal_to_foresight.pdf)

The present result does not depend, however, on the reader making that mistake. Imagine a reader who understands the limitation perfectly. He refuses to infer that the clues were obvious before he learned their significance. His judgement is admirably calibrated. Still, he has no record of encountering this story without the explanation, because that encounter never occurred. The reader in the alternative sequence might have such a record, provided it was generated before the story's own revelation and remains trustworthy. Awareness prevents an invalid inference; it does not produce the missing observation. He may compensate through other methods, but the need for compensation remains.

The contribution is therefore narrower than discovering a cost of hindsight, and different from simply warning critics to correct a bias. It concerns the distribution of available checks within a successful act of critical teaching, with changes in actual testimonial reliance only where the further evidential conditions obtain. A reader can genuinely learn the structure of the work, correctly recognize the limits of the resulting evidence, and nevertheless become less able to replace one part of the critic's testimony with his own encounter. Accurate instruction and rational uptake are sufficient for the case. Neither deception nor cognitive failure is required.

Another objection is that the discussion merely redescribes the epistemic loss caused by spoilers. Casey Rebecca Johnson's early workshop draft already argues that valuable reveals can have epistemic benefits, including benefits for inquiry about aesthetic matters, and that cooperative environments help preserve access to them. Her account also allows consensual disclosure. Neither the epistemic value of an unspoiled encounter nor its compatibility with permissible spoiling should be claimed as new here. [Johnson, early draft, sections 4–6.](https://www.ou.edu/content/dam/cas/philosophy-new/events/mew-2025/Johnson%20MEW.pdf)

The narrower issue is the warrant for an assessment communicated by the person who gives the explanation. This issue arises even if the encounter would contain no rewarding revelation. Suppose instead that the critic condemns a mystery because its solution is telegraphed. An explanation naming all the signals can prevent the reader from forming and preserving an unguided early guess that might help distinguish successful criticism from a critic's excessive hindsight. Seeing the signals now does not by itself establish that they were conspicuous beforehand. The relevant loss is an opportunity to check a negative evaluation, rather than the pleasure or educational benefit of a successful reveal. Johnson's approach can accommodate that loss; the present account specifies how it affects critical authority.

It also survives voluntary disclosure. A reader may enthusiastically request the explanation and enjoy the story more because of it. There need be no interpersonal wrong and no net loss of aesthetic benefit. What remains is the distinction between what his subsequent inspection can establish about structure and what it can establish about unguided uptake. This is not a rival general explanation of why people dislike spoilers. Neil Levy's account connects that dislike with resistance to other agents determining how we acquire knowledge of fiction. My question concerns the evidential consequence even when the reader welcomes that determination. [Levy, online 2025.](https://doi.org/10.1080/29960126.2025.2527423)

Could an experienced critic simply reconstruct the uninformed perspective? Sometimes, yes. Experience of other narratives may support excellent predictions. The text can also rule out proposed accounts: a supposed clue may actually occur after the revelation, or an allegedly hidden detail may occupy an entire chapter. Comparison with readers who lack the explanation can supply further evidence. There is no requirement that only the surprised may judge surprise.

But these replies vindicate a bridge from informed inspection to the relevant conclusion, not the unrestricted sufficiency of informed inspection. A well-supported reconstruction may be superior to one person's first impression. Where it is, losing the first encounter may be a small cost or no consequential cost at all. Where the reconstruction is uncertain, acknowledging its evidential basis matters. A critic cannot establish the adequacy of her model merely by guiding a reader into the response the model predicts for someone with that guidance.

What if experiencing the work without guidance teaches the reader so little that the lost evidence is worthless? That is another legitimate limit. Someone who cannot follow the language, distinguish the characters or remember the relevant events may need instruction before an encounter can inform the assessment. More explanation may then improve both structural understanding and evidence about appropriate response. The case for delaying explanation holds only where the unassisted encounter is an available, relevant source of evidence. It should never turn ignorance into a virtue regardless of its function.

The practical consequence is consequently modest. When inquiry specifically concerns how a work manages restricted information, the order of instruction can matter. It may be worth delaying an explanation that identifies the relevant device until an encounter has generated evidence about the response at issue. Where feasible, a brief contemporaneous note before the work's own revelation can preserve such evidence; a later account requires an assessment of memory reliability. Such an account should record an episode, not announce a verdict on all competent audiences. Even keeping a record can alter attention, so this is a useful option, not a demand for an experiment each time someone reads a story.

When the explanation has already been given, the alternative is not silence or permanent agnosticism. It is to locate the remaining dependence accurately. One can say: I see how the earlier details support the ending; my judgement that they were initially unobtrusive also relies on the critic's report or on this account of how readers allocate attention. That statement expresses a more discriminating understanding than claiming that everything has been checked because everything can now be seen.

These conclusions concern a possible evidential situation, not its frequency in ordinary criticism or the overall effects of explanatory teaching. Establishing either would require further inquiry.

Art criticism often succeeds by changing what we notice. For many purposes, that change makes the critic's reasons more available for our assessment. When the evaluation itself concerns what could be noticed before the change, however, the benefit comes with a limitation. The explanation can teach us to inspect the work while removing one condition under which part of its merit was to be inspected. Understanding a critic's reasons and gaining independent grounds for every premise are different achievements, and the path toward one can close a particular path toward the other.
