# Research question and alternative candidates

Author: fresh creative_author_12. Date: 2026-09-22. Area: aesthetics and artistic understanding. This is a public research rationale, not a manuscript.

Question: When can an accurate critical explanation remove a reader's opportunity to check an aesthetic judgement, even while increasing the reader's understanding of the explanation?

Candidate A: spoilers destroy epistemic value, not just pleasure. Rejected as a general contribution: actual searches locate Casey Rebecca Johnson's early draft *Narratives, Spoilers, and Cooperative Epistemic Environments*, expressly defending epistemic reasons against spoilers; Chasid and Levy also address imagination and epistemic autonomy. Any surviving proposal must add a more specific argument, not rename this claim.

Candidate B: restoration changes the reference point for judging authenticity. Not selected: initial search finds an established literature on material/conceptual/aesthetic authenticity and competing interpretations of artist intention. No defensible new inference emerged at this stage. This is an unadvanced candidate, not a comprehensive refutation of its prospects.

Candidate C: agreement with a recommender cannot establish aesthetic expertise rather than shared taste. Rejected as a contribution: initial retrieval points to Wallbank and Nguyen's existing monitoring problem. No new solution proposed.

Selected investigation: the apparent epistemic improvement from thin aesthetic verdict to rich explanation to first-hand inspection can fail for assessments of a work's management of an uninformed audience. A critic can make a formerly inconspicuous feature easy to notice by explaining its importance. Later inspection then verifies its presence but not the earlier accessibility relevant to the evaluation. The result sought is a restriction on when critical explanation provides a route to independent confirmation, not a ban on testimony or spoilers. Fair-play detective fiction supplies a test case, but the familiar contrast between forward and retrospective clue reading is not claimed as new.

The independent critic must assess whether this narrower inference improves on Johnson, Hills and Hazlett sufficiently. Preserve a negative result if it does not.
