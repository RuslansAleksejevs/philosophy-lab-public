## Charitable reconstruction

The critic's assessment includes both a structural premise (earlier details support the solution) and a response premise (those details do not conspicuously announce that solution under specified restricted information). An explanation can improve the recipient's access to the first while preventing a personally uninformed encounter relevant to the second. Guided recognition is not itself an observation of uninformed response. A rational recipient may know all this and still lose that opportunity. Thus an improvement in understanding need not improve every available route to independent checking.

That modest conclusion is sound given a suitably specified case. The essay does not infer aesthetic merit directly from surprise, does not confuse testimony with a defect, and explicitly permits informed reconstruction and audience evidence. Those qualifications answer several tempting but unfair objections. In particular, saying that an experienced critic can sometimes reconstruct an uninformed response does not refute loss of one opportunity.

There is no fatal contradiction. The main problem is the distance between a persuasive opportunity-loss argument and the stronger language of redistributed dependence on a particular critic.

## Main repairable defect: lost independent evidence need not become dependence on this critic

Location: paragraphs beginning “This is why the case bears on critical authority,” “The qualification matters,” and “At the same time, the explanation may reduce dependence elsewhere.”

The draft correctly says that losing one route need not increase dependence where ample other evidence is available. But the move to “redistribute dependence” remains stronger than the actual example establishes. Three outcomes need separating:

1. The reader loses one additional independent observation but retains exactly the same evidential reliance on the critic as before.
2. The reader leaves the response premise unsettled and so does not defer to the critic about it at all.
3. The reader continues to accept the response premise, and the only adequate accessible warrant for that acceptance is the critic's testimony.

Only the third supports the proposed local account of reliance, and even it does not demonstrate a temporal increase over an earlier state of wholly testimonial acceptance. The essay acknowledges much of this, but alternating between actual dependence, an opportunity to escape it, and independence relative to another inquiry order can leave the conclusion sounding stronger than the qualification allows.

Make the comparison explicit at the point where authority is introduced: after both inquiry sequences have finished, which proposition does each reader accept, and what supports that acceptance? A concrete version could stipulate that the unspoiled reader's sufficiently well-preserved response gives some independent support, whereas the explained-first reader accepts that same response premise solely on the critic's report. No claim of complete independence in the first sequence is needed. Alternatively, consistently describe the result as a reduction in opportunities for independent checking and present reliance on the critic as a conditional consequence.

This repair preserves the useful point. It does reduce the rhetorical force of “redistribution”: the argument establishes an asymmetry in evidential opportunities more directly than a changed distribution of actual warrant.

## Second repairable defect: the relevant first encounter has an internal order too

Location: the comparison of two inquiry orders; “Still, he has no record of encountering this story without the explanation”; and the practical paragraph recommending a brief account of prior salience.

The mystery itself eventually tells the reader the solution. Both sequences therefore normally end with an informed reader looking back on earlier attention. Encountering the work before the critic's explanation is not sufficient to guarantee accessible evidence uncontaminated by knowledge of the ending. A note written after finishing can already be a retrospective reconstruction. This does not defeat the argument, but it matters because the critic's disclosure is presented as the event that closes the relevant route.

Distinguish (a) having undergone an uninformed episode, (b) retaining reliable memory or contemporaneous evidence of that episode, and (c) later correctly describing it. The first order creates the possibility of (a); it yields useful subsequent evidence only if enough of (b) survives. The second order prevents (a) under the relevant conditions. That is a real asymmetry, but it should not be silently inflated into automatically reliable post-reading comparison.

A tightly specified case can use an ordinary spontaneous reaction before the reveal, or a brief contemporaneous note, while acknowledging the draft's existing point that deliberate monitoring changes attention. The negative-evaluation case is particularly helpful: a reader independently forms and preserves an early guess, which is evidence relevant to telegraphing without requiring pristine memory of all prior salience. Present this as a possible case, not empirical proof about ordinary readers.

## Third repairable defect: specify what one reader's response tests

Location: “Suppose he is ordinarily attentive and experienced with the genre” and “Each supplies an instance against which an account of their experience can be assessed.”

The relevant critic claim concerns an “appropriately attentive reader.” That can mean a population tendency, an idealized standard of attention, or this particular reader under suitable conditions. A single experience has a different evidential role in each. An ordinary attentive reader's early guess need not challenge a claim about a typical audience; its force depends on how expected such guesses are under the competing accounts. If “appropriate” is an idealized norm, actual response might be evidence only through an additional account of why this reader approximates it.

The draft already calls the evidence defeasible, but fallibility and relevance are different. Specify the modest relevance assumption rather than using “defeasible” to cover both. For example: the reader has independently established grounds to regard their response as some evidence about the response category at issue. Then allow only a limited update. This matters to independence: if the reader knows they are representative only because this critic says so, the supposedly independent test can retain another dependence on the same source.

## Significance and an objection worth resisting

The G/U distinction alone is a familiar conditional-evidence point: changing the tested condition changes what an observation directly shows. The essay's potential philosophical value is its application to successful critical instruction, where the teacher can simultaneously improve access to structural reasons and remove an available test of a response premise. Keep that two-premise contrast central. More repeated qualifications about spoilers do less argumentative work than one fully specified comparison of the two readers' final evidential positions.

Do not strengthen the essay by asserting that explanation generally undermines criticism or that unassisted judgement has special authority. Those changes would be unsupported and would erase the most defensible feature of the argument: first-hand experience can be worth preserving without being a privileged or sufficient test. Conversely, the fact that another source could supply better evidence does not make the lost opportunity unreal. The author may defend the conditional result against that objection.

## Recommendation

**REVISE, with the core argument retained.** State the opportunity-loss conclusion consistently; make any consequent reliance on the critic explicitly conditional; distinguish a first encounter from preserved evidence of it; and specify why the particular reader supplies relevant evidence. These are local repairs rather than a change of thesis. They leave a modest, coherent contribution about instruction and checking, not a general challenge to testimonial understanding.

Unresolved: whether this narrow application adds enough beyond the acknowledged literature; whether the source characterizations are accurate; and how often the specified evidential situation occurs in actual criticism. None is settled by this conceptual review. The revised essay should retain conditional scope rather than suggest that the invented case answers these questions.
