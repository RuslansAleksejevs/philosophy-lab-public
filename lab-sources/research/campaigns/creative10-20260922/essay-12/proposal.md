# Proposal p1: When explanation prevents checking

This is a pre-draft proposal for independent review, not a manuscript.

## Exact thesis

For some aesthetic judgements about how a work guides an initially uninformed audience, an accurate explanation can increase a recipient's grasp of the critic's reasons while removing a distinctive route to checking whether those reasons apply. Consequently, replacing thin testimony with rich explanation and subsequent first-hand inspection does not invariably reduce epistemic dependence on the critic. In the relevant cases it can increase dependence concerning one premise while reducing it concerning another. Critical pedagogy should separate these premises and preserve first-encounter evidence before disclosing the explanation when independent checking of that premise is the aim.

This is a limited result about evidential dependence, not an objection to the possibility of testimonial understanding, a general explanation of spoiler aversion, or a demand that all art be encountered unaided.

## Argument to be defended

1. Some aesthetic merit claims partly concern how available information becomes salient under an audience's restricted knowledge: a hint is delicately concealed, a transition is initially unobtrusive, or a reveal is adequately prepared without being telegraphed. Here a fact about the feature's presence and a fact about its availability to an uninformed audience are distinct premises. Whether the effect is meritorious additionally depends on appropriate artistic aims; actual surprise alone establishes no merit.
2. An explanation can teach the feature and its role by naming or isolating it. The hearer then recognizes it when inspecting the work. This warrants greater confidence in the feature's presence and can yield understanding of its structural function.
3. But recognition after that explanation is not a fresh sample of the feature's salience without it. Even accurate guidance changes the relevant conditions. Treating the recognition as confirmation of the original accessibility claim conflates two propositions; treating failure to be surprised as its refutation makes the converse error.
4. Because disclosure cannot simply be withdrawn, a hearer who wants to check the first-encounter premise may now have to rely on prior reports, reliable recorded reactions, comparison audiences, or a justified model of attention. The explanation therefore need not be a step from deference toward autonomous confirmation, even when combined with actual acquaintance and improved counterfactual reasoning.
5. The proper repair is local and temporal: where feasible, encounter first, record the relevant response modestly, then examine the explanation. Where that ordering is unavailable or undesirable, distinguish knowledge of structure from evidence about unguided response and acknowledge the remaining testimonial premise. This is not a recipe requiring lab experiments for ordinary criticism.

## Test case and controls

Imagine a reader who has never encountered a short mystery. The critic accurately identifies three peripheral descriptions that jointly support its solution and says the placement makes the ending both prepared and surprising. Before reading, the reader is shown every connection. On inspection all three descriptions are clearly present. That confirms preparation in one sense, but the reader's ease of noticing them cannot confirm that their placement was unobtrusive, nor can the reader's lack of surprise disconfirm it. Use an invented minimal work to avoid pretending to have established an empirical judgement about Christie. Fair-play can mean weaker retrospective coherence or stronger prospective solvability; state the intended sense rather than assuming one correct genre norm.

Control A: an explanation of a painting's visible symmetry often enables checking under the very conditions relevant to the judgement; no general suspicion of guidance follows. Control B: an unaided first reader's surprise may stem from inattention; it is defeasible evidence about an episode, not authority over appropriate audiences. Control C: rereading can generate new insight and skilled reconstruction; the claim is loss of one evidential route, not permanent impossibility of justified evaluation. Control D: an explanation can be fully understood on testimony; epistemic dependence is not itself a defect.

## Closest works and defended increment

Alison Hills, *Aesthetic testimony, understanding and virtue*, Noûs 56 (2022), 21–39, especially sections 5–6: rich explanation should guide development of one's own responsiveness to aesthetic reasons; acquaintance and emotional reactions often support that development. Agree with the general ability account. Proposed increment: explain a case in which the very guidance that improves responsiveness changes the conditions needed to test one of the critic's premises. Her qualified typicality claim is not refuted. The contribution is an articulated exception and its evidential consequence, not a claim that Hills universally prohibits trust.

Allan Hazlett, *Testimony, Understanding, and Art Criticism* (2025), accessible prepublication PDF sections 1.3 and 2: testimony and acquaintance are compatible; explanation can transmit understanding. Agree. Proposed increment: this compatibility leaves open an important asymmetry within the reasons transmitted—explanation may improve a hearer's assessment of structural relations while reducing access to response evidence. This is not offered as a counterexample to Hazlett's possibility claim.

Casey Rebecca Johnson, *Narratives, Spoilers, and Cooperative Epistemic Environments*, public early draft from the 2025 workshop, sections 2, 4–5: valuable reveals can carry epistemic benefits, supported by cooperative environments. This defeats a broad claim to novelty about epistemic spoiler losses. Proposed increment: a specific inference concerning warranted confirmation of a critic's assessment, which can concern a failed or inappropriate artistic effect, rather than securing a valuable reveal as such. Consent to guidance can remove interpersonal wrongdoing without restoring the lost comparison. Johnson could accept the result; compatibility is not itself novelty.

Sarah J. Link, *Manipulating Readers: The Novels of Agatha Christie* (2023), sections on form/attention, relevance/visibility and fair play: narrative arrangement directs attention and makes retrospective interpretation diverge from initial processing. These distinctions are prior art, not mine. Proposed increment is the asymmetrical consequence for testimonial dependence and critical explanation, not a new theory of detective fiction.

Neil Levy, *The Real Spoilers Puzzle* (online 2025; journal volume 2026): epistemic autonomy explains aversion to others controlling acquisition. The present claim concerns evidential checking even with chosen, welcomed explanations and no loss of enjoyment, so does not depend on unchosen control.
