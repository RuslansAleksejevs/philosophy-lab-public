# Sincere, forgiven, and still unacceptable

This is a pre-draft proposal for a short critical essay, not a manuscript.

## Exact thesis and target

Oliver Hallich's argument in section 2 of “Apologies—A Critical View” (2025) does not establish that an apology ineliminably requests forgiveness. Its treatment of content-related rejection excludes a live alternative: a recipient can refuse to ratify an inaccurate allocation of responsibility while granting the speaker standing, believing the speaker wholly sincere, and maintaining forgiveness for everything the speaker actually did. The proposed contribution is a targeted repair to this argument, not discovery that apology and forgiveness differ.

## Main argument and controlled case

An organizer, Ada, negligently omits Bea from an invitation list. An assistant, Cal, independently notices and culpably chooses not to correct the omission. Bea learns both facts, and forgives Ada's full culpable contribution before any apology; that forgiveness is not conditional on receiving one. Ada knows of her own negligence, sincerely regrets it, sincerely undertakes reform, and is entitled to address Bea. She mistakenly believes Cal never saw the omission. She apologizes for excluding Bea, presenting the exclusion as entirely her own fault and expressly clearing Cal. Bea believes her sincerity but replies: “I have forgiven your part. I cannot accept that apology as our account of what happened: Cal also knew. Please correct it.” After the evidence is shown, Ada revises the allocation of responsibility without reducing her admission of her own negligence; Bea accepts the corrected apology. No forgiveness for Ada is newly granted or withdrawn between those responses.

The example is constructed to avoid three easy dismissals: Ada is not innocent, the victim does not doubt sincerity, and the contested content concerns the explanation of the very wrong apologized for. The exclusion is one episode, not an unrelated offensive remark appended to an otherwise settled apology. Accepting Ada's revised acknowledgment settles what counts as a satisfactory answer to Bea about this episode; it need not restore affection, waive Cal's obligations, or settle every reparative duty.

The inference has four steps. (1) Judging an avowal sincere concerns whether the speaker owns its commitments; judging its responsibility attribution adequate concerns whether those commitments correctly describe the wrong. (2) These judgments can diverge even when the speaker admits a real wrong. (3) Consequently, uncertainty about the second can sustain hesitation about accepting an apology after uncertainty about the first is resolved. (4) An argument that rules out alternative meanings one component at a time cannot infer that the remaining work must be done by forgiveness: a combined acknowledgment account can explain both refusal and eventual acceptance. This is a local counterexample to an alleged exhaustion of alternatives, not a universal analysis of every ordinary use of “accept.”

The positive result is a constraint on apology skepticism: before treating a request for acceptance as pressure to relinquish blame or hostile emotion, establish what settlement is being requested. A request to assess and correct an account of responsibility can give the recipient something to accept or reject without asking her to forgive. This does not establish that all such requests are harmless, that victims must participate, or that every rejection is content-related.

## Closest-work comparison and novelty claim

Hallich (2025), section 2, especially publisher lines 90–115, treats hesitation after belief in sincerity as a difficulty for that analysis of acceptance, and treats content rejection as collapsing into sincerity. His own earlier acknowledgment of the verdictive dimension makes the collapse contestable. The intended increment is a controlled counterexample to this particular transition and its exhaustion structure; simply repeating the majority view would not suffice. His section 2 also treats the directive claim as linguistic rather than an inference from the speaker's actual motives. The proposed objection therefore concerns intelligible acceptance practices, not merely a benevolent motive.

Nick Smith (2017), numbered criteria 1–7, already separates factual record, blame, standing, harms and moral principles. These are inherited resources, not this essay's invention. The increment is using their independence to defeat the specific semantic inference above while preserving sincerity and forgiveness across a correction. Smith's book was not read in full.

Fabre, “The Duty to Accept Apologies,” accessed manuscript pp. 15–17, explicitly distinguishes acceptance and forgiveness. That general distinction is established background. This proposal does not depend on her disputed duty to accept or claim to originate that distinction.

Roadevin (2017), concluding section, already answers an earlier Hallich by appealing to acknowledgment, respect and moral solidarity. The proposed essay must credit that antecedent. Its narrower addition concerns the new explicit elimination of content-based rejection in the 2025 discussion, with a case where actual forgiveness stays fixed.

“Risk, Calamity and Apology” (2019), section 3.1, explicitly recognizes mistaken apology and the possibility that reconciliation calls for explaining innocence instead of acceptance. The selected case retains genuine culpability and standing, so it challenges a broader collapse than an innocence example alone would challenge.

## Objections that the draft must answer

First, Bea might reject only a proposition, not the apology. The reply must defend why uptake of an episode-specific apology can depend on accepting its account of culpability, while conceding that Bea could explicitly accept one component. Second, acceptance may be polysemous: that concession limits the target's universal linguistic inference rather than proving every use means ratification. Third, this may merely repackage Smith and Roadevin; the independent check should reject it if the precise inferential diagnosis is judged too slight. Fourth, telling the offender to correct facts can be burdensome, and the proposal creates no victim duty to do so. Fifth, none of this disproves Hallich's separate moral objections to requests actually seeking forgiveness.
