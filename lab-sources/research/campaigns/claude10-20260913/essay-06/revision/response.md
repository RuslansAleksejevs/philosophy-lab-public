# Response to the critique of `draft-001.md`

This file records which criticisms I accepted, which I rejected and why, and what changed materially between `draft-001.md` (sealed, essay-06-author) and the selected manuscript `final-002.md`. `final-001.md` is an intermediate full-length revision, preserved but superseded on length grounds (see `final-selection-001.json`).

The critique was unusually good. It found three substantive problems and six citation defects, and I independently re-retrieved every disputed source before acting. In two places the critic was more right than it claimed; in one place I think it overreached. I say so below.

---

## 1. Criticisms accepted in full, with the change made

### 1.1 “Authorship” was the wrong concept (critique §2)

**Accepted, and this is the most important change in the revision.** The critic is right that two sentences of the draft were simply false of my own case: Aurum could *not* have secured Mara's 2024 certification at will, since it required four acceptances she was entitled to make and could have declined. The draft's rigged-ballot analogy had the opposite causal structure (unilateral exclusion) from the case it was meant to illuminate.

I took the critic's own recommended repair: promote what was the closing conjecture of §9 into the organising principle. The revision replaces the Comparative Triviality Principle with an **Independence Principle**: consent supports A's justification only to the extent that the poverty of B's alternative is *not explained by A's conduct*. The revision states the three-way distinction the critic drew — (a) A removes options, (b) B narrows her own prospects, (c) A offers options whose acceptance narrows B's prospects — concedes that Aurum is (c) and not (a), and then argues that the evidential defect does not depend on causal unilaterality: a comparison is self-serving when the party citing it is the party whose conduct explains why one side of it is low, and mediation through B's rational acceptances is part of that explanation rather than a break in it. The electoral analogy is retained but re-described as a conflict-of-interest objection, which makes it exact. A bonus the critic anticipated: because explanation admits of degrees, Inverse Reliance now falls out as a graded consequence instead of being stipulated.

### 1.2 The §7 criterion did not discriminate (critique §3)

**Accepted.** All three counterexamples land. Every internal labour market satisfies the old conditions (i) and (ii); so does the firm that cuts wages because it is failing; and “characteristically” was doing work no criterion can do. The critic also correctly identified the bind: condition (ii) needed an intentional notion that §8d had forsworn.

I adopted the critic's preferred repair and developed the one dropped sentence about fiduciary forbearance. The criterion in the revision is **positional, not behavioural**: A occupies the baseline-capture position when its course of dealing predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A, and one who occupies that position may not invoke B's consent as a *complete* justification for terms B's prior reservation value would not have permitted. This is intention-free, so it still reaches designerless platform and visa cases, and it dissolves the counterexamples rather than excluding them: the failing firm also loses the consent defence, and rightly, because what it should say is “we were insolvent”, which is a justification benchmarked to something other than the agreement. I bite the internal-labour-market bullet explicitly in the text, and argue that the resulting picture — such arrangements as a standing site of justificatory burden — is exactly why we regulate them in the ways §9 lists. The marriage case gets the same treatment and, I think, the intuitively correct verdict.

### 1.3 §3 “The toolkit acquits” was inaccurate (critique §6 partly, §8 in full)

**Accepted in every particular.** I re-retrieved all four entries and read the separately saved extractions, not fetch summaries. The critic was right on each count, and in one case understated the problem.

- **Coercion.** The draft quoted only the consequent. The full sentence begins “If the offering party here has *created the conditions* that make even very unattractive offers still the best available…” (Anderson 2023, §2.4), and Aurum satisfies that antecedent on my own telling. The revision quotes the whole sentence, concedes the antecedent, and rests the distinction on the consequent instead (“actions that *prevent*”, which Aurum performs none of). It also reports the two adjacent observations the draft omitted: Zimmerman's concession that a non-causing offeror may still exploit, and that “sometimes coercion is used to create or maintain one's bargaining advantages”.
- **Republican non-domination.** The critic is right that this was the most serious unmentioned rival, and right that I had a good answer available. The revision adds a paragraph on Pettit-style domination and Garnett's freedom-as-nonsubjection, both as reported in Anderson 2023 §3.3 (verified verbatim), and argues that domination is a relation of *power to interfere* which Aurum never acquires; that baseline capture is therefore possible where domination is not, including in competitive markets with free entry; and that the two theses have different objects, non-domination concerning a condition bad for the subject and mine concerning the offeror's standing to justify. Garnett is named as my nearest neighbour rather than sidelined.
- **Manipulation.** The draft's claim that manipulation “has not generally been theorised as a property of extended sequences” does not survive contact with Noggle 2026 §§4.1–4.3, as the critic said. I verified Riker's heresthetic (“structuring the world so you can win”, 1986: ix), Warwick and Kelman, Barnhill 2014: 53, Mills 1995: 97, the intent-free and algorithmic discussion in §4.2, and the vulnerability-compounding remark in §4.3. The revision reports all of this and then uses the critic's own suggested differentia — Barnhill's situational category requires that the change in options “thereby change[s] her attitudes”, and Mara's attitudes are unchanged. I added one thing the critic did not mention and which I think is the best single line in the section: Raz's contrast, quoted in Noggle §2.1, that “manipulation, unlike coercion, does not interfere with a person's options. Instead it perverts the way that person reaches decisions” (1988: 377). That dichotomy is exhaustive only if all interference with options is coercion, and my case falls between its two chairs.
- **Exploitation.** The draft's report of the literature was wrong and the critic's correction improves the paper. I read the *Exploitation* entry (Zwolinski, Ferguson and Wertheimer 2022) and confirmed Steiner's prior-injustice condition and its $2/hour illustration (§2.2.2.2), Roemer, Sample, and the Goodin/Vrousalis/Zwolinski dissent. The revision restates the originality claim as the critic proposed: the ratchet is a counterexample to the *historical* accounts, exhibiting baseline degradation with no antecedent injustice anywhere in the chain.
- **Nonworseness claim.** Verified verbatim, including the dropped first condition and the credits (Wertheimer 1996, 2011; Zwolinski 2009; Powell and Zwolinski 2012), and the sentence “Most exploitation theorists are skeptical that the NWC is correct.” The revision states the claim in full, concedes that a result against a widely rejected claim yields less dialectically, and takes up the critic's suggestion that the dropped “right not to interact” condition is a second and independent route to my conclusion.
- **The §2/§3 contradiction, and the fair-market-value point.** Accepted, and this was the sharpest thing in the critique. The revision now stipulates competitive pricing only for steps (1)–(3) and says explicitly that step (4) is not competitively priced. It further concedes that on Wertheimer's fair-market-value test (verified at §2.2.2.1, quoting Wertheimer 1996: 232) step (4) looks exploitative — and argues that this is a demand for my thesis rather than a rival to it, because the test is silent about *which* hypothetical competitive market, 2016's or 2024's, and nothing internal to it chooses. Baseline capture supplies the missing index.

### 1.4 Mill (critique §7)

**Accepted on both counts.** I re-read the Gutenberg text. The “far wider application” clause is semicolon-joined to what precedes it and is not a retreat; the revision says so and withdraws the charge. More importantly, Mill's reliance passage at pp. 196–97 — “When a person, either by express promise or by conduct, has encouraged another to rely upon his continuing to act in a certain way…” — is verbatim as the critic reported it, and it does show Mill seeing the structure. I have dropped “the part Mill did not see” and replaced it with a continuity claim: Mill's principle constrains the party who would *withdraw*; the ratchet asks what is owed by the party who *benefits* from the reliance; the two are complements, and the second is the Commitment Condition. I also dropped the implication that the gradient reading is unnoticed, and now cite Olsaretti 2004: 139 (verified verbatim in Anderson 2023 §3.3) and Feinberg 1986: 233–244 (as cited in Eyal 2025 §5.3).

### 1.5 The Commitment Condition (critique §5)

**Accepted on (a), (b), (c) and (d).**

- *(a) Unpriceability was asserted, not argued.* The critic is right that agents price others' future discretion all the time. I withdrew the claim rather than defending it, and replaced it with a better one that the essay's own machinery already supports: **pricing a risk is not authorising what eventuates.** A discount is money; consent is a permission; and at t₁ there is no determinate proposal to permit, only a range from which A will later choose. Insurance against an outcome does not license it.
- *(b) §8g begged the question.* Conceded, and this produced the largest concession in the revision. If Aurum had *bound itself* in 2016 to the whole trajectory, terms included, Mara's consent then would have been given against a baseline Aurum's conduct did not explain, and it would justify. What Aurum did instead was buy an option and keep the discretion. So the compensation objection, pressed properly, converges on the Commitment Condition rather than refuting it.
- *(c) State the stronger thesis.* Done, verbatim in the critic's formulation: consent is justificatorily effective only over option sets the party invoking it cannot subsequently reshape, or has bound itself not to reshape. It is now the stated conclusion of §7.
- *(d) The counterintuitive implication.* Faced rather than qualified away. The account governs the availability of a defence, not a ranking of characters: the binder who extracts within the bound has a justification other than “she agreed”, and the non-binder who behaves decently is not condemned but merely required to argue.
- *Locating remark.* Accepted: precommitment and resolute choice (Bratman, Gauthier, McClennen, in Andreou 2025 §2.4) are named as the single-agent cousin, with the difference stated.

### 1.6 What is the justification a justification *of*? (critique §4)

**Accepted; this was a real hole.** The revision names the object: the **terms** Aurum obtains at step (4), specifically its share of the surplus that the fall in Mara's reservation value made available. Making a proposal needs no counterfactual justification; taking a larger share because the other party's exit has become expensive does.

The critic's follow-on dilemma about the remedy is also sound, and I abandoned the draft's test. Pricing Mara's 2016 generalist talents ignores productivity Aurum financed; pricing her present talents on a hypothetical open market asks what a non-existent market would pay for skills that exist only because of the relationship. The revision replaces both with a **division-of-surplus** benchmark: Aurum must show its share is one it could have obtained while Mara's reservation value was still independent of the relationship. That is a question about a division, not a phantom wage, and it also removes the misleading appeal to reliance damages, which as the critic noted compensate the investing party.

### 1.7 The fourth horn and the smuggling analogies (critique §6)

**Accepted.** The structural-injustice response is added as objection (c) and answered by distinguishing blame from answerability: losing the consent defence is not being blamed, it is owing a justification, and one can owe a justification without having done wrong; the structural reading leaves nobody answerable, whereas the case is built around a determinate party who benefits and can act.

The critic is also right that “each coin toss fair while the game is rigged” and “each shot legal while the fight is fixed” both presuppose a hidden wrongful act that the case excludes, so they help the objector. Both are gone. They are replaced by evidential analogies with no hidden act: correct measurements made with an instrument the interested party calibrated, and a witness whose every statement is true but who selected which events to witness.

### 1.8 Title and subtitle (critique §6, §9)

**Accepted.** “Wrongs Without Wrongful Parts” presupposed a wrong the argument does not deliver. The manuscript is retitled *Baseline Capture: Why Consent Stops Justifying When Its Beneficiary Has Shaped the Alternatives*, and §1 now says explicitly that the claim is about defeated justifications assembled from sound parts, not wrongs assembled from innocent ones.

### 1.9 Apparatus (critique §9)

**Accepted.** σᵢ is now defined as the total gains from trade at stage *i*. “Monotone capture” is gone, replaced by a corollary that claims only what is shown — capture without a single unacceptable offer — with an explicit disclaimer that no monotonicity result is asserted. The failure-of-composition result is promoted and given the display it deserves.

### 1.10 The hold-up antecedent (critique §11)

**Accepted and strengthened.** The critic was right that a reader who recognises the structure and finds it unacknowledged will discount the paper. Rather than a bare mention, I retrieved and read the primary source — Klein, Crawford and Alchian 1978 — and used three things from it: their definition of quasi rent as value in excess of “its value in its next best use” (298), which is a reservation value in all but name; their observation that “an appropriable quasi rent can occur with no market closure or restrictions placed on rival assets” (299), which independently vindicates the claim that lock-in needs no fence; and their framing of the problem as efficiency loss (301), which marks off what is new here. Their opportunism “by definition refers to unanticipated non-fulfillment of the contract” (300), and Aurum breaches nothing.

### 1.11 Citation defects

All corrected; details and verification method are in `sources.md`. In summary: the Wood quotation is **deleted** (not merely de-quoted), together with the false claim about the literature it supported; the Cohen page problem is avoided by quoting the p. 9 sentence, as both the author and the critic recommended; “Andreou 2023, §1.4” becomes Andreou 2025, §1.3, twice; “Noggle 2022” becomes Noggle 2026; “Eyal 2019” becomes Eyal 2025; the silently altered Susser quotation is removed in favour of direct quotation of the entry's own wording elsewhere, and Susser et al. 2019: 4 is credited properly where used.

## 2. Criticisms accepted with a qualification

### 2.1 “The honest headline is *Consent Without Justificatory Force*” (critique §6)

Accepted as to substance, not as to wording. I agree the argument delivers a defeated justification rather than a demonstrated wrong, and the essay now says so in §1. I did not adopt the proposed title because “baseline capture” names the mechanism and the phenomenon, and the subtitle carries the disclaimer.

### 2.2 The *Informed Consent* §5.3 warning (critique §8, last item)

The critic flagged this as something that “needs checking before the originality claim is restated”, and treated it as a risk. Having read §5.3 myself, I think it is better than a risk: it is the sharpest available foil, and it belongs in §1 rather than in a footnote. The proposal that consent is insufficiently voluntary “only when the patient's options are unfairly curtailed *by the offer itself*” is credited in the entry to **Miller and Wertheimer 2010, 92, 97** — an attribution the critique did not supply — and its index on *the offer itself* is precisely the synchronic assumption my case is built to break. The revision opens with it. I also accepted the warning about Millum and Garnett's “good of independent decisionmaking” and now cite it in §9 as an antecedent for my closing conjecture rather than presenting the conjecture as unoccupied ground.

### 2.3 Word count (critique §9)

Accepted, and the critic's estimate proved closer to the truth than the author's. Neither of us could measure. The intermediate `final-001.md` was measured mechanically at 7340 word units excluding bibliography, well over the ceiling; `final-002.md` is a substantially compressed rewrite. See `process.md` §4 and `final-selection-001.json`.

## 3. Criticisms not accepted, or accepted only in part

### 3.1 That §3's inaccuracy is “fatal to §3 as currently written” because “the whole originality claim rests on it”

I accept every factual correction and reject the diagnosis of what they cost. The originality of the thesis never rested on the literature having overlooked the phenomenon; it rests on the claim that consent's *justificatory* force is history-sensitive in a specific way. Situational manipulation, republican non-domination and Steiner's historical exploitation are all better foils than the ones the draft constructed, and each of them, once correctly reported, still gives a different verdict or asks a different question about Aurum. The revision is stronger for the corrections, but its central claim is unchanged, and I did not treat §3 as requiring reconstruction from the ground up.

### 3.2 The suggestion that the fiduciary route and the intentional route are alternatives

The critic offered a disjunction: either accept a thin intentional element, or accept a positional account. I take only the second horn. A thin intentional element (“A's terms are explained by the lowered reservation value”) would reintroduce exactly the hostage to intention that the platform and visa cases are meant to escape, and it would make the account turn on a motivational fact that is usually unknowable from outside. The positional account carries a cost the critic did not press — it makes the duty very widely distributed — and I pay that cost openly in §7 rather than trimming the criterion to preserve a narrower extension.

### 3.3 The Laitinen priority risk

The critic recommends downgrading it substantially, on the basis of catalogue and search material it declines to treat as a source. I have kept the risk on the record at its original weight, in §9 of the manuscript and in `process.md`. PhilArchive returned 403 to the author and to the critic, and I did not retry; neither of us has read the chapter, and a description is not a reading. I agree the described question looks different from mine. That is not the same as knowing.

### 3.4 Pallikkathayil and the Kantian paragraph

Partly accepted, by deletion rather than repair. The critic reports that Pallikkathayil's manuscript is a sustained refutation of the possible-consent interpretation, which would weaken the draft's use of Korsgaard's criterion as a live benchmark. I did not verify that report and did not want to rest on it. In `final-001.md` I replaced the paragraph with a short, verified note drawn from the *Treating Persons as Means* entry (Kerstein 2023, §§3.2–3.3), which the critic recommended consulting: the Kantian debate divides into accounts asking whether the other *could* consent (O'Neill 1989: 113) and accounts asking whether she *has* (Nozick 1974; Scanlon 2008; Kleingeld 2020), and neither branch asks who made refusing so costly. In `final-002.md` that note was cut entirely for length, since it was illustrative rather than load-bearing. Korsgaard and Pallikkathayil no longer appear in the bibliography.

## 5. Disagreements that remain open

1. Whether the positional criterion is too wide. I bite the bullet for internal labour markets; a reader may reasonably think the bullet too large to swallow.
2. Whether “valid but justificatorily idle” is stable. The critic did not press this; the author flagged it. I still think it is defensible — the power really is exercised, and no battery occurs — and I still have not defended it at length.
3. Whether the division-of-surplus benchmark is operable. It is better specified than the draft's, but it remains a counterfactual, and §9 says so.
4. Priority. Neither the author, the critic nor I has read Laitinen, and the novelty searches remain weak evidence at best.
