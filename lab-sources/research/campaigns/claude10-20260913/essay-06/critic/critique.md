# Critique of `draft-001.md`, "The Author of the Alternatives"

**Stage:** independent critic, essay-06. **Object assessed:** the complete manuscript `draft-001.md` together with `sources.md` and `process.md` from essay-06-author.

I read the whole manuscript and then re-retrieved, independently, every source the essay cites that I could reach, plus one entry the essay does not cite but should. Verification results are folded into the substantive sections and summarised in §10. I have not attempted to write a revision, and I make no claim to judge the essay against any competition standard.

---

## 1. Overall assessment

The essay has a real idea. The core claim — that consent certifies only a comparison, and that the certification goes evidentially slack to the extent that the party invoking it produced the thing compared against — is clean, correctly identified as the load-bearing move, and supported by a case that has been carefully engineered to block the obvious dismissals. The decision to make the steps large, discrete and perceptible (recorded in `process.md` §3c) is the right one and pays off: the essay cannot be filed under sorites, self-torturer, or money pump, and it says so convincingly. The electoral analogy in §5 is the best single paragraph in the paper.

The essay is also unusually honest about its own defects, and the audit in `sources.md` §F is accurate as far as it goes. I confirmed the Cohen page error independently; I also closed one of the author's flagged uncertainties in the author's favour (the *Coercion* entry really is by Scott Anderson, 2023) and found that two others resolve against the manuscript.

Against that, the essay has three problems that I think are serious rather than cosmetic, and one that is fatal to §3 as currently written:

1. **The notion of "authoring the alternatives" is never analysed**, and the case as told does not obviously instantiate it. Aurum removes nothing; the narrowing of Mara's option set runs through Mara's own four acceptances. That mediation is the whole interest of the case and it is also the whole difficulty, and the essay treats it as unproblematic by leaning on an analogy (the rigged ballot) that has precisely the opposite structure (§2 below).
2. **The discriminating criterion in §7 does not discriminate.** As written, it catches every internal labour market and most durable relationships (§3).
3. **§3 "The toolkit acquits" is not accurate**, and this matters because the whole originality claim rests on it. Two of its four verdicts are contradicted by the very Stanford Encyclopedia entries the essay cites, and a third rests on a version of the exploitation literature that the literature does not hold (§6, §10).

The Commitment Condition is the most original thing in the paper and the least defended (§5). Fixing (1)–(3) would, I think, make the paper stronger rather than weaker, because in each case the literature the essay currently waves past turns out to supply a sharper foil than the one it constructs.

## 2. The central problem: authorship, or merely causal contribution?

Everything turns on the claim that Aurum "authored" Mara's 2024 alternative. §5 says: "*any* offeror who can lower the alternative can make the certification come out true", and §8g says the certification "is still one Aurum could have secured whatever it offered."

Both sentences are false of the case as the essay tells it. Aurum could not have secured that certification at will. It required four acceptances by Mara, each of which she was right to make and any of which she could have declined. Aurum's acts are necessary but nowhere near sufficient. Contrast the election: the candidate who excludes rivals from the ballot does so unilaterally, and the voters' cooperation is not required for the exclusion.

This is not a quibble, because the disanalogy tracks exactly what is philosophically interesting. Consider three ways B's outside option can fall:

- **(a)** A removes options B would otherwise have had (Zimmerman's boat-preventing employer).
- **(b)** B's own choices narrow her option set (the generalist who becomes a harpsichord specialist).
- **(c)** A offers B options whose acceptance predictably narrows B's future option set, and B accepts.

Nobody thinks (b) undermines the justificatory force of B's later consent; that is just what it is to have a life with a shape. Everybody thinks (a) does. The case is (c), and the essay needs an argument that (c) inherits the normative profile of (a) rather than of (b). It does not give one. §7's condition (i) — "predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A" — is satisfied by (b) as well, whenever B's specialisation happened to be encouraged by A.

The honest reply available to the essay is that the *evidential* point does not care about mediation: however the comparison came to be degraded, if A's conduct explains the degradation then A cannot cite the comparison as evidence in its own favour. That reply is good but it has a price, which is §3 below: it makes the principle indiscriminate. My recommendation is to make the mediation explicit and own it, and to argue that what matters is not who performed the narrowing act but **whether the party invoking the consent stands in a relation to the option set that makes the consent self-serving as evidence**. The essay already has this formulation — it is the closing conjecture of §9, "a theory of when a normative power's exercise is *independent* of the party who stands to benefit from it." That is the real thesis. It should be promoted from the last paragraph to the organising principle, because it makes the election analogy exact (the objection to a rigged election is a conflict-of-interest objection, not a causal one), it yields a graded notion rather than a binary one, and it connects to a familiar epistemology of self-serving evidence.

## 3. The criterion in §7 does not do the work assigned to it

> A's course of dealing with B **ratchets** iff (i) it predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A, and (ii) A uses the lowered reservation value to obtain terms more favourable to A than B's prior reservation value would have permitted.

`process.md` §5.5 records the author's own doubt about (ii). The doubt is justified and the draft does not address it. Three counterexamples:

- **Every internal labour market.** A firm trains a worker, her market wage rises more slowly than her internal productivity, and the firm does not raise her pay to what a portable-skilled worker would command. That satisfies (i) and (ii) on the letter. If the essay concedes that all such arrangements defeat the consent defence, the conclusion is vastly more radical than §9's modest list of portability and vesting rules suggests.
- **The failing firm.** A firm that specialised a worker and then cuts wages *because it is failing* uses the lowered reservation value to obtain terms her prior reservation value would not have permitted. It has done nothing objectionable. (ii) catches it.
- **The marriage.** §7 says benign entanglements "characteristically" fail (ii). "Characteristically" is not a criterion. A spouse whose partner gave up a career, and who then insists on staying in the city he prefers, obtains terms the partner's prior reservation value would not have permitted. If the answer is that he does not *use* the dependence, the essay needs a notion of "using" that is not intentional — and §8d has explicitly forsworn intention.

That last point is the bind. §8d wants the criterion to be intention-free so it can reach designerless platform and visa cases. But (ii) is doing intentional work: the difference between the ratcheting firm and the failing firm is what explains the firm's conduct, and explanation-by-bargaining-advantage is very close to an intentional notion. Either accept a thin intentional element (something like "A's terms are explained by the lowered reservation value"), or accept that the criterion picks out a structural position and that occupying it generates duties regardless of conduct — which is the fiduciary line §7 gestures at in one sentence and then drops.

That dropped sentence — "a fiduciary duty of *forbearance from baseline capture*" — is the most promising thing in the paper after the Comparative Triviality Principle, and it has a real and supportive antecedent in fiduciary law, where a party who acquires discretionary power over another's interests is barred from using it for self-gain without regard to intent or to whether the beneficiary consented. Developing that would solve the intention problem and the overgeneralisation problem together. One sentence is not enough.

## 4. Validity without justificatory force: what exactly needs justifying?

§5 insists Mara's consent is valid (no battery, no trespass) but justificatorily idle, and `process.md` §5.4 anticipates the objection that this is unstable. I do not think instability is the problem. The problem is that the essay never says what Aurum is doing that requires justification.

A consent defence is a defence *to something*. At step (4) Aurum makes a proposal and accepts an acceptance. Making a proposal is not normally an act standing in need of justification against a counterfactual baseline; accepting labour offered is not either. §8c then rejects agglomeration, which relocates the objectionable item to the *course of dealing* — but a course of dealing is not a thing for which anyone offers a consent defence, so "the consent defence is unavailable" becomes a claim about nothing in particular. The essay needs to specify the object: is the burden-shifted justification owed for (a) the terms of step (4), (b) the making of the step-(4) proposal, or (c) the whole course of dealing? Different answers give different remedies and different §9 institutions.

Relatedly, the remedy in §5 has an unnoticed cost. The test is whether Mara is "paid what someone with her talents would command had she never dealt with Aurum." But her *current* talents are firm-specific partly because Aurum paid for the training in step (2). So the test either (i) prices her 2016 generalist talents, in which case Aurum must pay a wage unrelated to her present productivity and Mara keeps the specific capital Aurum financed, or (ii) prices her present talents on a hypothetical open market, in which case it is asking what a market that does not exist would pay for skills that only exist because of the relationship. Neither is obviously right and the essay should say which it means. §9 waves at reliance damages, but reliance damages compensate the *investing* party — here that is arguably Aurum.

## 5. The Commitment Condition is the paper's best claim and its thinnest argument

CC deserves the prominence the author gives it in `process.md` §3e. Four problems, in increasing order of seriousness.

**(a) The unpriceability premise is asserted, not argued.** §7: informing Mara about Aurum's future discretion gives her nothing to price, because the discretion "at that moment does not exist as a fact." But agents price others' future discretion continually — that is what at-will discounts, risk premia, and reputational pricing are. Mara can price a distribution over Aurum's future conduct. The essay needs a reason why *this* uncertainty is uninsurable, not merely that its object is a discretion.

**(b) §8g's reply to the compensation objection begs the question.** "Payment does not undo staging" is asserted, but the staging complaint was grounded in the *evidential* failure of the 2024 comparison. A fully-priced 2016 bargain over the whole trajectory is itself a comparison made against an unauthored baseline, and the essay has given no reason why a consent at t1 to a priced trajectory is not exactly the fix it says is unavailable.

**(c) CC reduces the diachronic problem to a synchronic one and does not notice.** Binding is a normative act performed at t1 — when Mara's baseline is, ex hypothesi, unauthored. So CC's answer is: get the right thing done at t1. But that is structurally the trajectory-consent proposal the author reports abandoning (`process.md` §3e). If a *commitment* at t1 restores justificatory force, why not *disclosure plus consent* at t1? The essay's implicit answer must be that a commitment removes the later discretion rather than describing it — which is a real distinction, and yields a stronger and more interesting thesis than the one stated: **consent is justificatorily effective only over option sets the offeror cannot subsequently reshape.** Say that.

**(d) CC has a counterintuitive implication the essay should face.** An employer that binds itself in 2016 and then extracts exactly the step-(4) terms in 2024 is fully justified; one that does not bind but behaves better throughout is not. Given that the essay's whole complaint is that formal compliance can float free of how someone is actually treated, this is an awkward result and should be either defended or qualified.

One locating remark: CC is other-directed precommitment, and the single-agent version of it is the resolute-choice/precommitment literature (Schelling; Elster; McClennen), which occupies §2.4 of the *Dynamic Choice* entry the essay already cites. Naming that antecedent would *strengthen* the novelty claim by showing precisely what is new — the commitment runs to another party's benefit and is a condition on the committer's standing to invoke consent.

## 6. §8c's trilemma is narrower than advertised; the analogies smuggle

§8c offers: reject (i) each act is permissible, or reject (ii) permissibility agglomerates, or reject (iii) the course of dealing is permissible; and argues that rejecting (i) concedes Historicity, so the standard picture falls either way.

There is a fourth response the essay does not consider, and it is the one I expect most readers to take: keep (i) and (ii), and deny that "the course of dealing" has a deontic status over and above its parts, while holding that the *outcome* is unjust and criticisable in non-deontic terms. That is the standard structural-injustice move, and it is available precisely because §8d has already conceded "blameless ratcheting". If the wrong can be blameless and agentless, it is unclear why we should describe it deontically at all rather than as a defect in the distribution or in the institution. This also puts pressure on the subtitle: "Wrongs Without Wrongful Parts" presupposes a wrong, but the argument delivers only a defeated justification. `process.md` §5.3 flags this; I agree with the flag and think it is more pressing than the author allows. The honest headline is *Consent Without Justificatory Force*.

The three analogies in §8c are also unequal. "Each belief justified while the set is inconsistent" is a property that is definitionally set-level, so it proves nothing about action. But "each coin toss fair while the game is rigged" and "each shot legal while the fight is fixed" both presuppose a *further hidden wrongful act* — the rigging, the fix — which is exactly what the Aurum case is built to exclude. As they stand the analogies help the objector.

## 7. Mill: the reading is good, verified, and unfair to Mill in one specific way

I retrieved the Gutenberg text independently. The block quotation in §6 is **verbatim**; the inline marker `[Pg 195]` falls inside "consideration for his liberty", so the citation "pp. 194–95" is right; the "necessities of life" clause is on p. 195, as cited.

Two corrections, one small and one substantive.

**Small.** The "retreat" is not a separate move. Mill's text runs: "...are evidently of far wider application; yet a limit is everywhere set to them by the necessities of life..." — one sentence, semicolon-joined. "Announces ... and then immediately retreats" overstates the distance, and the separate page citation makes it look like a separate passage. Also, read in context the clause is not a retreat from the generalisation at all: Mill is saying that the necessities of life require us to accept *some* limitations of freedom, i.e. that the wider application cannot be pushed to void every constraining engagement.

**Substantive, and I think valuable.** The essay says: "this is the part Mill did not see — his second premise is *exploitable*." Mill's very next sentences, which the author read past (they are the continuation of the Humboldt passage set aside in `process.md` §3i), say this:

> "When a person, either by express promise or by conduct, has encouraged another to rely upon his continuing to act in a certain way—to build expectations and calculations, and stake any part of his plan of life upon that supposition, a new series of moral obligations arises on his part towards that person, which may possibly be overruled, but cannot be ignored."

That is a course of conduct, not a single act, generating obligations because of the reliance it induces — which is the essay's own structure, and in the essay's own idiom (§9 reaches for reliance damages by analogy). Mill did see it; he simply located it on the other side, as a constraint on the *withdrawing* party rather than on the party that induced the dependence. The essay should claim continuity with Mill here rather than a blind spot: "Mill's reliance principle applied to the party who benefits from the reliance" is a better and more defensible framing than "the part Mill did not see", and it makes §6 do real work instead of rhetorical work.

Finally, the gradient reading of Mill's evidential premise is not new. Feinberg's treatment of voluntariness as variable and Olsaretti's analysis ("A choice is voluntary if and only if it is not made because there is no acceptable alternative to it") are both squarely in this territory, and Olsaretti is quoted in §3.3 of the *Coercion* entry the essay cites. The essay should not present the gradient as an unnoticed consequence of Mill.

## 8. §3 "The toolkit acquits": three inaccuracies, one internal contradiction

This section is where the originality claim is cashed, and it is the weakest section. I verified each verdict against the entries the essay itself cites.

**Coercion — the quotation is accurate but truncated in a way that hurts.** The *Coercion* entry (§2.4) reads: "**If the offering party here has created the conditions that make even very unattractive offers still the best available**, then the coercion, if there is any, seems to be vested in the offerer's actions that prevent the recipient from improving her situation or finding better offers elsewhere." The essay quotes only the consequent and reads "prevent" as requiring a fence. But the antecedent is *created the conditions*, which Aurum satisfies on the essay's own telling. The essay owes an argument that creating-by-giving is not creating. The same section also records Zimmerman's concession that a non-causing offeror may still exploit, and the observation that "sometimes coercion is used to create or maintain one's bargaining advantages."

**The unnamed rival: republican non-domination.** §3.3 of the same entry sets out Pettit-style domination ("her choices must always be made with an eye towards retaining the favor of those in a position to interfere") and Garnett's freedom-as-nonsubjection, of which the entry says: "Garnett holds that such subjection can be found in some cases of offers, where the offeror takes advantage of the offeree's lack of an acceptable alternative." Mara in 2024 is close to a paradigm of non-domination's target. Non-domination is diachronic, structural, intention-free, and already holds that a formally free choice made under another's controlling power is defective. It is the most serious competitor to the essay's thesis and it is not mentioned once. The essay must say why baseline capture is not simply domination — and it has a good answer available (domination concerns A's *power to interfere*, whereas Aurum's power is constituted entirely by benefits conferred and never by any capacity to interfere), but it has to give it.

**Manipulation — the claim is false as stated.** §3 says the leading accounts all work through a defect in the target's mental states, and that "manipulation has not generally been theorised as a property of extended sequences." `sources.md` §B1 concedes this rests on a model summary rather than on reading. It does not survive contact with the entry. §4.1, "Manipulating Persons versus Manipulating Situations", surveys exactly the category the essay says does not exist: Riker's *heresthetic*, glossed as "structuring the world so you can win"; Warwick and Kelman's "environmental" versus "psychic" manipulation; Barnhill's category of manipulation that "changes the options available to the person or changes the situation she's in, and thereby changes her attitudes"; and Mills's A-changes-the-external-features-of-B's-choice-situation. "Structuring the world so you can win" is a fair one-line description of Aurum. §4.2 further covers manipulation without conscious intent (Baron, Manne) and algorithmic manipulation with no designer — which is §8d's platform case. §4.3 notes that some manipulation "work[s] to increase the target's vulnerability to further manipulation", i.e. is explicitly sequential. The essay can still argue that situational manipulation is the wrong diagnosis here (Mara's attitudes are not changed at all, which is the differentia Barnhill's formulation supplies). But it cannot say the category is missing.

**Exploitation — the characterisation of the literature is wrong, and correcting it helps.** §3 says "in the exploitation literature the victim's want of acceptable alternatives is characteristically treated as something the exploiter *finds*." The *Exploitation* entry, which the essay does not cite, reports the opposite as a major position. Hillel Steiner's account: exploitation occurs when A gains more and B less "than they would have were it not for the existence of a prior injustice" — the entry's illustration is A paying B \$2/hour "only because A (or someone else) has previously unjustly deprived B of alternative sources of labor." Roemer and Sample are in the same family; Goodin is the explicit dissenter who holds the source of vulnerability irrelevant; Vrousalis and Zwolinski argue antecedent injustice is not necessary. There is a named, live debate about exactly the question the essay says nobody asks.

This is recoverable and, I think, an opportunity. Steiner's condition requires a *prior injustice*, and Aurum commits none — its contributions were gifts. So the ratchet is a genuine counterexample to the historical accounts: baseline degradation with no antecedent injustice anywhere. Stated that way the originality claim is sharper, better located, and no longer depends on a false report about the literature.

**The nonworseness claim is overweighted, and one condition is dropped.** The canonical formulation is: "Interaction between A and B cannot be worse than non-interaction **when A has a right not to interact with B at all**, and when the interaction is mutually advantageous, consensual, and free from negative externalities", credited to Wertheimer 1996 and 2011, Zwolinski 2009, and Powell and Zwolinski 2012. Two consequences. First, the essay's "standardly credited to Alan Wertheimer" is thin, and "the discussion of exploitation has been dominated by" is misleading: "most exploitation theorists are skeptical that the NWC is correct." Observation 1 is a genuine and nice result about NWC, but it is a result against a claim most of the field already rejects, which lowers its dialectical yield. Second — and more interesting — the dropped condition is one the essay should *want*: it is arguable that by step (4) Aurum no longer has a clean right not to interact with Mara at all, precisely because of what steps (1)–(3) did. That is a second, independent route to the same conclusion, and it is sitting in the formulation the essay truncated.

**Internal contradiction.** §3 says "Here each transaction was competitively priced at the time it was made." §2 stipulates competitive pricing only for steps (1)–(3), and describes step (4) as longer hours, no raise, no advancement if refused. So the exploitation acquittal does not cover step (4) on the essay's own facts — and on Wertheimer's fair-market-value test (a fair price is one at which neither party "takes special unfair advantage of ... special vulnerabilities in the other party's situation") step (4) looks like a candidate for straightforward exploitation. Worse for the framing: the burden-shifted test the essay proposes in §5 — what Mara's talents "would command had she never dealt with Aurum" — *is* a hypothetical-competitive-market test, i.e. Wertheimer's account with a counterfactual baseline. The essay dismisses exploitation theory in §3 and then reinvents it in §5. Either acknowledge that the positive proposal supplies the diachronic component that fair-market-value accounts lack, or explain the difference. As it stands this is the most obvious line of attack on the paper.

**One more straw-man risk.** §1 asserts that on "the dominant picture" the synchronic conditions settle validity and validity settles justification, citing the *Informed Consent* entry. That entry's §5.3 is titled "'No choice' situations" and is devoted to whether option-poverty undermines voluntariness, citing Feinberg 1986: 233–244, Millum and Garnett 2019 on "the good of independent decisionmaking", and — directly on the essay's point — the proposal that "consent is insufficiently voluntary only when the patient's options are unfairly curtailed **by the offer itself**". The essay cites §1 of this entry for the list of conditions and does not mention §5.3, which is about its own question. "Independent decisionmaking" as a good distinct from voluntariness is, on its face, the essay's §9 conjecture already in print. This needs checking before the originality claim is restated.

## 9. Apparatus, framing, and length

- **Undefined symbol.** §4 defines u_i and r_i and says "A takes the rest". Observation 2 then says "A's share at stage i is σ_i − (u_i − r_i)", but σ_i is never introduced. Either define total surplus σ_i or drop the symbol. As the only formal claim in the paper, this should not contain an undefined term.
- **"Monotone capture" is mislabelled.** Observation 2 shows that a falling r_i permits A to hold u_i down; it does not show monotonicity of A's share, which would need assumptions about σ_i. `process.md` §3g records that the full model was cut; that was probably right, but then the heading should not promise a monotonicity result.
- **Observation 1 is the stronger of the two and is buried.** It is the sharpest thing in §4 and could carry the section alone.
- **Length.** `process.md` §4 estimates ~5,300 words excluding bibliography and warns the figure is unmeasured. I counted independently, also by estimation, and got roughly **5,900–6,300** words excluding the bibliography — materially higher, and straddling the 6,000-word cap. Neither of us has a machine count. Whatever else happens, a reviser must obtain an actual count before this is treated as compliant; the margin is not safe. If trimming is needed, §8 (~1,150 words) is the most compressible: (b) is three lines of cross-reference, and (a) and (e) could each lose a third.
- **Title.** "Wrongs Without Wrongful Parts" is not what the paper argues (see §6 above).

## 11. Priority

`process.md` §5.1 records Laitinen's "Normative Powers, Agency, and Time" as the author's top unresolved risk. PhilArchive returned **HTTP 403** to me as well, so I could not read it either. From catalogue and search-summary material only — which I do not treat as a source — the chapter appears to be about whether agents can effect *direct* normative change and whether normative powers ever produce genuine normative transformation, published in Bagnoli (ed.), *Time in Action* (Routledge, 2022). On that description the overlap with §7 looks low: Laitinen's question is whether normative powers work at all, not whether their justificatory yield can be captured by a counterparty. I would downgrade this risk substantially, while recording that neither of us has read the chapter.

The priority risks I would raise in its place, in order:

1. **Republican non-domination** (Pettit; Garnett 2018), for the reasons in §8.
2. **Steiner's historical account of exploitation**, which is the closest structural analogue and against which the essay's originality is best stated.
3. **"Independent decisionmaking" in the bioethics literature** (Millum and Garnett 2019, as reported in *Informed Consent* §5.3), which may already be §9's closing conjecture.
4. **The hold-up problem** in the economics of relational contracting. `process.md` §5.2 concedes this and is right that the normative claim is what is new. I would add that the concession should be *in the essay*, not only in the process file, and that making it explicit is cheap and protective: a reader who recognises the hold-up structure and finds it unacknowledged will discount the whole paper.

I did not find, in the searches I ran, a philosophical treatment of the specific claim that consent's justificatory force degrades with the consent-receiver's contribution to the option set. That is weak evidence of novelty and no more; the coverage gaps above are large enough that I would not describe the thesis as unprecedented.
