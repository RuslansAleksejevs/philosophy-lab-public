# Critique of essay-07-author, `draft-001.md`

**"What Asking Does: Requests and the Second Dimension of Practical Reason"**

---

## 0. What I did, and what I could not do

I read the complete manuscript, `sources.md` and `process.md`. I then went back to the primary literature myself rather than taking the essay's reports on trust.

**Read in full, independently retrieved:**

- Daniel Weltman, "What Makes Requests Normative? The Epistemic Account Defended," *Ergo* 9 (2023) — complete text including all 45 endnotes and the reference list.
- Peter Schaber, "The Reason-Giving Force of Requests," *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* 24:2 (2021) — complete, including footnotes and references.
- Thomas Reid, *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, Essay I ch. 8, "Of Social Operations of Mind" — complete chapter, in the same Woozley abridgement the author used.
- Kenta Sekine, "In Defence of Second-Order Reasons," *Canadian Journal of Law & Jurisprudence* 39:1 (Feb. 2026), 200–223 — complete, including notes. The author did not have this; it matters a great deal for §5 and I explain why below.

**Could not obtain (so my checks on these remain second-hand, as the author's were):** Enoch 2011 (SSRN and quod.lib.umich.edu both returned 403), Lewis 2018 (PhilArchive returned 403 on two attempts, although the file is indexed and clearly present), Gläser 2019 (an apparently open-access copy exists in the Zurich repository ZORA but sits behind a JavaScript bot-challenge that my retrieval could not pass), Lance and Kukla 2013, Raz's *Practical Reason and Norms* and *The Morality of Freedom*, Darwall 2010, Heuer on exclusionary reasons (PDF would not extract), Mossé forthcoming.

So on the two papers the author most wanted checked — Enoch and Lewis — I can offer only a third and fourth independent report rather than the originals. That said, Weltman's §2 gives a fuller summary of Enoch than Schaber's §6 does, and the two agree; I report the one discrepancy in §9 below.

**Headline verdict.** This is a good essay, better written than most work in its neighbourhood, and its central reorientation — from "does asking add to the scales?" to "what does asking do to deliberation?" — is worth pursuing. Every quotation I was able to check is accurate to the word; I found no misquotation anywhere. But the essay substantially overstates what its central argument establishes, its positive thesis as stated does not entail the datum it was built to explain, and it has three literature gaps that a specialist reader will notice immediately, one of which is visible in a paper the author says they read in full. None of these is fatal. All of them are fixable, and some of the fixes make the essay stronger rather than weaker.

## 1. The architecture proves much less than the essay claims

The Attention–Weight Dilemma (§4) is presented as the load-bearing argument. §1 says the dispute "has been conducted on the wrong axis"; §4 concludes "Either way the Weight Model loses a datum"; §8 says the view "earns its place by explaining a profile no weight-based account can capture."

It does not establish this, and the essay half-knows it. §4 defines a Weight Model as any view on which "the distinctive contribution of a request is a first-order pro tanto reason of some positive weight *w*." The definite article is doing all the work. Enoch, Lewis and Schaber are answering the question *whether requests create reasons*; none of them is committed to the claim that creating a reason is *all* a request does. A hybrid — request adds weight *and* puts the question on the agenda — is available off the shelf, and the dilemma cannot touch it, because the second conjunct answers D2 while the first answers whatever D5 fails to rule out.

That this is not a merely notional position is confirmed by Schaber's own text. He explicitly allows a request to do more than create the constant-weight, request-dependent reason: it can also trigger a background conditional obligation of gratitude, so that "[r]equests create non-obligatory reasons in a robust sense. However, they can create obligatory reasons in a non-robust sense, by triggering conditional obligations" (§4). Schaber's requests are already multi-tasking.

The essay concedes the point at the second reply in §4: "The only remaining question is whether there is *also* a first-order weight — and D5 suggests there is not." That sentence is the true shape of the argument. The dilemma is an **incompleteness** argument: weights alone cannot deliver D2. The eliminative claim, T1, rests entirely on D5 — one intuition pump plus a universal generalisation (§8 below). The framing should be brought into line with this, and the essay should stop advertising a refutation it has not performed. Doing so costs rhetoric but no substance, and it removes the easiest way for a hostile reader to dismiss the paper.

## 2. Horn 2 misdescribes the position it names (verified)

This is the most concrete argumentative error I found.

Horn 2 says: "suppose *w* is very small — an epsilon … This is the natural retreat, and something like it is suggested by Schaber's claim that request-created reasons always have the same, modest, constant weight."

Schaber says: "The reasons requests create have always the same weight." He nowhere says the weight is small, modest, or epsilon-sized. "Modest" is the essay's interpolation, and it is precisely the interpolation Horn 2 needs.

Worse, the dilemma's governing assumption — that cheap refusal (D1) must be explained by low weight — is exactly what the targets deny. Schaber and Lewis explain discretion by **non-obligatoriness**, which Schaber defines in deontic, not scalar, terms: "A reason is non-obligatory if it would not be blameworthy for one not to act on it, and obligatory if it would be blameworthy not to act on it" (§4, following Lewis). A reason can be weighty and non-obligatory; that is the whole point of the category. So the inference "refusal is cheap, therefore *w* is tiny, therefore the reason is idle, therefore ignorable" never gets started against Lewis or Schaber.

The essay does not use the word "non-obligatory" once, and does not engage the obligatory/non-obligatory distinction that organises the Lewis–Schaber exchange. This is a real hole in the dialectic, and it is in a paper the author read completely.

What the essay should do: either (a) argue that non-obligatoriness is not a primitive and must reduce to low weight — which is a substantive and interesting claim, and would restore the dilemma; or (b) redirect the dilemma, granting that the opponents have a non-scalar explanation of D1 and arguing that it still leaves D2 unexplained. Option (b) is safer and is closer to what the essay actually needs.

Relatedly: §4 lists "Enoch, Lewis, Schaber and Cupit" as Weight Model adherents. Cupit's paper is called "How Requests (and Promises) Create Obligations," and Schaber's n. 8 cites it under "reasons why requests could be duties." On the available evidence Cupit is an obligation theorist, i.e. a Horn 1 occupant at most. The author has not read him. Either read him or drop him.

## 3. The Agenda Thesis does not entail D2

This is the deepest structural problem, and I do not think the author has noticed it.

The Agenda Thesis has two clauses: (i) S is required to consider φ-ing under the description "what A has asked of me"; (ii) S must either φ, or not-φ for a reason S *could* avow to A. The essay is emphatic that (ii) is counterfactual: "This is not a demand for actual speech — often it would be absurd to explain oneself out loud."

Now take the corridor case. S hears the request, silently considers it, silently decides not to read the draft because S is on deadline — an eminently avowable reason — and walks on without a word. Clauses (i) and (ii) are both satisfied in full. Yet this is, by the essay's own §1 description, the wrong of looking through your colleague "as though nothing had been said."

So the theory as stated does not deliver D2, the datum it was constructed to explain. D4 ("that liability is discharged by speech, not by restitution"), §6.6 ("In (a), S must respond: help, or decline and say something. Silence is not an option") and §6.5 ("To ask and not to be attended to is not a defective transaction; it is a failed one") all require a third clause — a duty to *answer* — that the Agenda Thesis does not contain and that avowability was explicitly designed not to be.

The author cannot simply bolt the clause on, because the two commitments pull apart. If counterfactual avowability suffices, silence is permitted and D2 is false. If actual response is required, then avowability is not the operative condition and §5's careful work on pretexts and coin flips is doing something other than what it advertises. The essay needs to decide, and the decision has downstream consequences: an actual-response requirement makes the account look much more like a norm of communicative uptake, which in turn makes the reduction objection (§6.1) harder, not easier, to answer.

## 4. The second dimension has no structure

The essay's case against weights is that one dimension cannot capture the phenomena. Fair enough. But an added dimension needs its own logic, and none is supplied. Three questions go unasked:

**Defeat.** Is the requirement to consider defeasible? It must be — I may ignore a request while performing surgery, or while my child is running into traffic. But defeated by what? If by weighing the costs of attention against the claim, weight has re-entered at the second order and the reduction objection is vindicated. If not defeasible, the thesis is false. §6.1 commits to the stronger horn ("it persists where considering is costly, and where the outcome is foreordained"), which I think is simply not true of costly-enough cases. Note too that §6.1 says in one breath that the costs of considering "are almost always trivially small" and that the requirement "persists where considering is costly"; these clauses pull in opposite directions and the argument needs the second.

**Conflict.** Ten people ask me ten different small favours on the same morning. Attention is, as §6.4 rightly insists, "the scarcest thing we have." Agenda items therefore compete for a scarce resource, and competition for a scarce resource is ordered by something. Whatever orders it will look a great deal like weight. `process.md` §6.4 concedes that multi-requester aggregation is asserted rather than argued; I think it is worse than that — it is the place where the second dimension either acquires a weight-like structure or fails to be action-guiding.

**Interaction.** How do inclusionary and first-order reasons combine to determine what S ought to do? Raz's exclusionary reasons have a clear and much-discussed answer (pre-emption). The Agenda Thesis has none. A reader sympathetic to the project will want at least a sketch.

## 5. T1 and Answerability are in tension

T1 says asking adds *no* weight. Answerability says S must φ unless S has an avowable reason not to. Consider the trivial case: A asks S to pass the salt; S has no competing reason of any kind. The Agenda Thesis apparently entails that S must pass the salt. But that is a practical requirement on the requested act, generated by the request, in a case where before the request there was none. Calling this "no weight" starts to look like a verbal victory.

The escape is that "I'd rather not" is itself an avowable reason, always available. But then Answerability places almost no constraint on what S does, and the entire felt force of a request is carried by the response duty — which, per §3 above, the thesis does not contain. Either horn needs owning.

The hedge in T1 makes this worse: "adds no weight — *or no weight worth the name*." The second disjunct is precisely the epsilon that Horn 2 was built to destroy. If the author's own view is permitted an epsilon, the author's own view is on Horn 2. The hedge should go. `process.md` §3 reports that the thesis deliberately hardened from "very little weight" to "no weight" and says "I regard that as an improvement: it is falsifiable in one move." I agree — so let the manuscript say it without the escape clause.

## 6. D5 is under-tested, and it now carries the whole eliminative claim

Given §1 above, D5 is the sole support for T1. It is one case plus a universal generalisation: "Take any case in which refusing a request seems seriously wrong, and remove the request while holding fixed the underlying facts and your knowledge of them: the wrong of not acting remains, undiminished."

The chosen case is the worst possible test. The drowning swimmer is a case where the underlying reason is overwhelming, so any increment is swamped, and where — as §5 itself says — "emergencies conscript attention without anyone's help." A case constructed so that the request is redundant cannot show that requests are always redundant.

Three tests the essay does not run, in ascending order of danger:

**Near-indifference.** Two strangers need a lift home; one asks, one visibly needs it but says nothing. I can take exactly one. Does the asker have any stronger claim? Many will say yes. The Agenda Thesis says only that the asker's case is on my agenda and the other's is not — which, since I have noticed both, is no difference at all.

**Withdrawal.** A asks; A then says "actually, please don't." If asking added no weight, withdrawing should subtract none, and my reasons should revert to what my information supports. But withdrawal seems to do more than that — it can make φ-ing positively objectionable. The essay's standing paragraph gestures at this without addressing it.

**Content-creating requests.** "Please don't tell anyone." Telling after being asked not to is a betrayal. Telling when I had merely surmised she would prefer silence is thoughtless. That looks like a difference in the gravity of the wrong, not merely in whether I owe her an account. The essay's available reply is that these are consent rather than request — but `process.md` §6.3 candidly admits that this reply was "taken over from Weltman rather than defending it." Since T1 now rests on D5 alone, and the consent manoeuvre is what protects D5 from its hardest counterexamples, the manoeuvre needs to be defended in the author's own voice.

## 7. The Eavesdrop Test: three problems, and a better case the essay already has

The author names this as the argument they most want attacked (`process.md` §3). Here is the attack.

**(a) It varies common knowledge, not information.** In case (a) A knows S heard, S knows A knows, and so on up. In case (b) A does not know S overheard. Silence in (b) is therefore not a communicative act at all: it carries no meaning to A, and so of course it cannot wrong A in the way ignoring does. The epistemic theorist gets the asymmetry for free — a request creates common knowledge, and common knowledge of a need, plus ordinary norms of conversational uptake, generates a reason to respond. Higher-order information is still information. The test therefore does not isolate address.

**(b) Eavesdropping is a confound.** In (b), S obtained the information by overhearing something not addressed to S. There are independent norms against acting on, and especially against revealing, what one has overheard. S's permission to say nothing may be fully explained by the impropriety of disclosing the eavesdrop. The variants offered (A is shy, it never occurs to A, A is unconscious) do not help: if A is unconscious there is no addressee to be answerable *to*, so the asymmetry is over-explained rather than explained.

**(c) Weltman has already conceded a difference of this shape and bracketed it.** In §5.1 he writes: "when I request something, and someone acts on my request, I am to some extent responsible for this, whereas when someone acts on a wish, I am not." He calls it "a big difference." And in the attached note: "It is also a normatively important difference, but not in the sense of the normativity we are interested in when we ask why requests are normative." So the ready-made reply to the Eavesdrop Test is: *yes, there is an asymmetry in interpersonal responsibility; I grant it; it is not an asymmetry in the reasons you have to φ, which is what the question was.*

This is the single most important unanswered objection in the paper, and it is available in a source the author read completely. The essay must argue that answerability belongs to practical normativity rather than changing the subject. §8 asserts this beautifully ("the most important thing another person can do to you, short of obliging you, is not to tip your scales but to make you answer") but does not argue it against someone who has explicitly declined to count it.

**A better test, which the essay already possesses.** Footnote 10 quotes Raz via Schaber: "It is possible … to tell a person that, while I would like him to do something for me, I am not asking and am not going to ask him to do it." Say this *to S's face*. Now there is full information, full common knowledge, a genuine addressee, and no eavesdropping impropriety — and no request. Does S owe a response? This isolates address from information cleanly in a way the Eavesdrop Test does not. I should add that it may not go the author's way: being told "I'm not asking" plainly does put one on the spot. But that is what it is to have a real test.

## 8. Literature and originality — the most serious problems

### 8.1 Gläser is the closest rival and is not engaged

Weltman's note on Lewis's account reports: "Gläser classes Enoch, Lewis, and Lance and Kukla together (along with some other views) because they all give what he calls a '*mere addition account*' of requests (Gläser 2019: 32)." And: "because Gläser thinks requests do not give reasons, his account is compatible with mine, although I cannot show this here (Gläser 2019: 47)." Schaber's n. 1 adds: "Gläser, on the other hand, argues that requests do not create additional reasons, they rather put the requestees under an obligation."

Put together: the family the essay calls the Weight Model already has a name and a published critic; and Gläser already holds a negative claim about added reasons combined with a positive claim about a distinct deontic change. That is structurally T1 plus T2. He may well be wrong, and the *content* of his positive claim (obligation) differs from the essay's (agenda plus answerability) — but the essay cannot leave him as a name in a list. This is visible in two sources the author read in full, which means a reader will class it as a gap in reading rather than a gap in access.

Two further notes. First, the manuscript's §2 lists Gläser among those who say the request "contributes something to the first-order balance," which is the opposite of what the author's own sources report. Second, `sources.md` item 7 and `process.md` both state that the essay mentions Gläser "as holding that requests obligate rather than add reasons." It does not — that sentence is not in `draft-001.md`. The source record and the manuscript have drifted apart; worth repairing, since this is exactly the sort of thing a methodology check catches.

A practical lead: an open-access copy of Gläser 2019 exists in the Zurich repository (zora.uzh.ch), reachable at a bitstream URL I located. It is behind a JavaScript proof-of-work challenge that my retrieval could not pass; a differently configured retrieval, or the ZORA landing page rather than the bitstream, may succeed. It is worth one more attempt.

### 8.2 T3 will not survive contact with the second-order reasons literature

The essay says the second species of Raz's genus "has no settled name" and that requests "occupy the slot his own definition left open." I checked this against Kenta Sekine, "In Defence of Second-Order Reasons," *CJLJ* 39:1 (2026), 200–223, which I read in full.

The settled name is **positive second-order reasons**. Sekine's opening states the received view flatly: "Like all reasons for action, they think, second-order reasons are either positive or negative: They are either reasons for acting for certain reasons, or reasons against acting for certain reasons." He then offers a four-way typology of positive second-order reasons (contingent vs. constitutive, same vs. different further value) and uses it to locate the entire moral-worth literature — Arpaly, Markovits, Korsgaard, Herman — inside it. The slot is not empty. It is furnished.

(Sekine does not use "inclusionary." So the essay's n. 7 disclaimer is aimed at the wrong risk: the *label* may well be free; the *category* is thoroughly occupied.)

This is not merely a citation problem, and here I want to suggest that the fix improves the philosophy. Raz's genus is *reasons to act, or refrain from acting, **for** a reason*. Look at the Agenda Thesis's two clauses against that definition:

- Clause (ii) — S must not-φ only for an avowable reason — **is** a second-order reason in Raz's sense. It constrains the reasons for which S may act. It is a genuine positive second-order reason, and its nearest kin are Sekine's constitutive Type C/D reasons.
- Clause (i) — S is required to *consider* φ-ing — **is not**. Considering is not acting for a reason. A requirement to put a question on the agenda is a requirement on *deliberation*, not on the reasons for which one acts.

So "inclusionary reason" as the essay uses it covers two different things, and only one of them is the positive counterpart of exclusionary reasons. Untangling them would cost the neat symmetry of T3 — but it would yield a more interesting claim: that requests reveal a *third* position that Raz's disjunctive definition does not mention at all, a reason bearing on deliberative uptake rather than on acting-for-a-reason. That claim is both more defensible and more novel than the one currently made.

Three further items the essay must at least acknowledge:

- **The coherence of second-order reasons is under live attack.** Daniel Whiting, "Against Second-Order Reasons," *Noûs* 51:2 (2017), 398, argues they are incoherent (the Credit Argument). See also N. P. Adams, *Philosophical Studies* 178 (2021), 235; Sophie Keeling, "Responding to second-order reasons," *PPR* 109:3 (2024), 799; Sekine 2026; and the earlier wave (Moore, Perry, Regan, *S. Cal. L. Rev.* 62 (1989); Alexander; Hurd; Schauer; Gur 2018). An essay that grounds its taxonomic claim on this framework should say, in a sentence, why the Agenda Thesis survives if Whiting is right — or, better, that it does not need Raz at all.
- **Raz's own second thoughts.** The 1990 edition the essay cites contains "Postscript to the Second Edition: Rethinking Exclusionary Reasons," beginning at p. 178. Citing that edition at p. 39 through Piller while ignoring the postscript in the very same volume is exposed, given that the claim at issue is about what Raz did and did not develop.
- **Darwall.** Stephen Darwall, "Authority and Reasons: Exclusionary and Second-Personal," *Ethics* 120:2 (2010), 257–278, with Raz's reply, "On Respect, Authority, and Neutrality: A Response," *Ethics* 120:2 (2010), 279. That exchange is precisely this essay's intersection — second-personal address versus Razian second-order reasons. §6.6 invokes "the tradition that treats second-personal address as sui generis" without naming anyone; `process.md` says Darwall was not read, which is honest, but a specialist will regard an essay whose positive thesis is "address makes you answerable" as owing Darwall an engagement. I should add that "answerability" is also a term of art in the responsibility literature (Watson; Angela Smith; Shoemaker's attributability/answerability/accountability trichotomy); using it unglossed will import associations the essay does not intend.

### 8.3 An unsupported claim about a large literature

§7 asserts that "one-dimensional views keep failing to account for" procedural rights. This is a sweeping claim about a substantial body of work on the non-instrumental value of procedure — dignitary theories of due process, the literature on the right to be heard — with no citation. `process.md` §6.5 already flags it. It is the essay's most exposed sentence: either support it or reduce it to "this is a puzzle the two-dimensional picture is well placed to address."

## 9. Source accuracy: what checked out

This should be said plainly, because it is unusual. **Every quotation I could verify is exact.**

- Weltman: the abstract's "does not create any reasons and thus does not have any special normative power"; §6's "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking"; §5.2's "there is nothing normatively special about the request" and "The only normative strength is in the reasons that are attended to (which already exist) and the reasons to attend to those reasons (which also exist prior to the request)"; and n. 40 in full — all verbatim, all correctly located. The essay's reading of n. 40 as the concession-and-dismissal on which its whole project turns is fair.
- Schaber: "The reasons requests create have always the same weight" (§4); the partner/neighbour case and its diagnosis in terms of request-independent reasons (§4); the Lewis "places sufficient discretionary value" condition (§5); the Raz *Morality of Freedom* 36 quotation in n. 10; the Lance and Kukla 460 quotation in n. 4 — all verbatim and correctly attributed as quoted-through.
- Reid: all four quotations are exact, including "the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain." The pagination claim (pp. 48–51 of the Woozley abridgement) is correct, and the editor's note confirming that ch. 7 was omitted is where the author says it is. Reid is used as a diagnosis rather than an authority, exactly as `process.md` promises.

Two small discrepancies:

1. **Enoch's conditions.** The essay follows Schaber's three-item version. Weltman's §2 gives four: "X must intend to give Y a reason, **communicate this intention to Y**, intend Y to recognize this intention, and intend Y's reason to depend on Y's recognition of X's intention (Enoch 2011: 15)." Not an error — but since both reports cite the same page, the essay could note that the communication condition appears in one summary and not the other.
2. **Laskowski and Silver's date.** Weltman's reference list dates *Oxford Studies in Normative Ethics* vol. 11 to **2021**; the essay and `sources.md` say 2022. Since the work is cited through Weltman, follow Weltman or verify independently.

And one correction I can supply from Sekine's n. 6: **Piller's paper is published**, not an author's manuscript — Christian Piller, "Kinds of Practical Reasons: Attitude-Related Reasons and Exclusionary Reasons," in Sofia Miguens, João Alberto Pinto and Carlos E. Mauro (eds.), *Analyses: Proceedings of the 2nd National Meeting for Analytic Philosophy* (University of Porto Press, 2006), 98. The bibliography should be updated.

Finally, a reassurance on the author's stated worry (`process.md` §5) that Enoch might be nearer their position than allowed. On Weltman's fuller report, Enoch's triggering works "via rendering true the antecedent of a conditional along the lines of 'if X asks Y to Z, Y has a reason to Z'" — so the thing triggered *is* a reason to Z, a first-order reason. Classifying Enoch under the Weight Model is defensible. The essay should nonetheless note that on the triggering account the normative work sits in the background conditional, which is exactly Schaber's §6 complaint against him.

## 10. Smaller points, in manuscript order

1. **§5, standing.** The Agenda Thesis is conditioned on "if A has the standing to do so," but the standing paragraph then says that where standing is absent the requester "*has imposed* an agenda item they had no right to impose." If standing is a success condition, nothing was imposed and there is nothing to complain of. If something was imposed, standing is a permission condition, not a success condition. Pick one. (Schaber faces the analogue and picks the success condition: requests "only create reasons if they are respectful with respect to their form as well as their content.")
2. **§5, avowability.** As stated the condition is either psychological — in which case the bigot avows, as §6.2 concedes, and it screens off almost nothing — or normative, in which case it is doing moral work the essay disclaims. The actual work in §5 is done by a *sincerity* condition: that the reason S acts on be the reason S would offer. That is a different and better condition; name it as such.
3. **§5, coin flips.** "I had two equally good uses of Saturday and picked by lot — sorry" looks like a perfectly adequate account. The theory over-predicts a failure here.
4. **§5, non-aggregation.** Idempotence predicts that a second asking changes *nothing*. D3 says repetition "if anything degrades" the case. The degradation is then explained by a separate story about pressure and irritation — which is precisely the sort of supplement the essay calls "ad hoc" when the weight theorist offers a diminishing-returns function. Also, asking again after six months, or after circumstances change, does seem to renew the claim; pure idempotence denies this.
5. **§6.1.** The claim that the request abandons the Weight Model's commitment is wrong: a weight theorist can hold that the request adds a reason to φ *and* a reason to consider. Showing the second does not retire the first.
6. **§6.2.** "The bigot who refuses to your face wrongs you differently, and in one respect less thoroughly, than the bigot who declines to register that you spoke." This is a striking claim and I think it is right, but it deserves a sentence of defence rather than being the closing line of an objection reply.
7. **§7, questions.** "A question creates an obligation to answer that is … impossible to ignore." Many questions are permissibly ignored — rhetorical ones, hostile ones, a stranger's intrusive one. The standing apparatus is needed here and is not deployed.
8. **§5, Laskowski and Silver.** The essay says the Agenda Thesis "gives a home to" their observation. Per Weltman's n. 6, they explain the wrongness of certain requests via *trust*, and Weltman thinks their account supports his own. The essay is offering a *rival* explanation of their datum, not a home for their view. Say so.
9. **D4.** "If I refuse a request I owe, at most, an account of myself." Sometimes more is owed — help finding another helper, or an apology for a curt refusal. Not load-bearing, but overstated.

## 11. What is genuinely good

I want to be as specific in praise as in criticism, because three things here are better than the surrounding literature.

- **§6.4 is the best passage in the essay.** The argument from what we build defences against — do-not-disturb settings, gatekeepers, conventions of indirection whose function is to let a request be withdrawn before it lands — is a genuinely novel form of evidence, and the inference ("small weights are not worth defending against") is sharp. I have not seen it made. If anything it is under-exploited; it is a better argument for the two-dimensional picture than the dilemma is.
- **The D1/D2 contrast is a real explanandum, well framed.** "Cheap to refuse, expensive to ignore" is a genuine profile that the existing debate does not squarely address, and putting it at the front is the right editorial decision.
- **The prose.** Clear, controlled, unhurried, free of throat-clearing. The opening two paragraphs do more work in 150 words than most papers do in a section.
- **The honesty.** Notes 1, 2, 6, 7 and 8 disclose citation chains and access limits without special pleading, and `process.md` records abandoned approaches — including the epsilon-plus-attention view and the discontinuity argument — with unusual candour. §6 of `process.md` correctly identifies four of the five weakest points before any critic saw the paper. That is the mark of a serious author, and it should be preserved in any revision.

## 12. Priorities

If only three things can be fixed:

1. **Read and engage Gläser 2019** (§8.1). He is the nearest rival, he named the target, and his absence is the clearest signal of incomplete engagement.
2. **Reframe §4 as incompleteness rather than refutation, and repair Horn 2's misdescription of Schaber and Lewis** (§§1–2). The eliminative claim then needs D5 to be defended properly against the withdrawal and confidentiality cases (§6).
3. **Fix the gap between the Agenda Thesis and D2** (§3) — decide whether an actual answer is owed, and say what the second dimension's defeat conditions are (§4).

If a fourth: replace or supplement the Eavesdrop Test with a common-knowledge-preserving non-request, and confront Weltman's bracketed concession about responsibility (§7).

If a fifth: rebuild T3 around the distinction between reasons bearing on deliberative uptake and reasons bearing on acting-for-a-reason, in explicit dialogue with the positive second-order reasons literature (§8.2). I think this is where the essay's best unrealised idea is.

## 13. Remaining disagreements and uncertainties on my side

- I have not read Enoch, Lewis, Gläser, Darwall, Lance and Kukla, or Raz's books. My criticisms of the essay's treatment of Enoch and Lewis rest on Weltman and Schaber, i.e. on the same kind of evidence the author used, with one extra report. A reader with the originals should check §2 and §8.1 first.
- On §8.2 I am confident about the existence and shape of the positive second-order reasons literature, having read Sekine in full; I am less confident about whether anyone has specifically theorised a reason to *put something on the deliberative agenda*. My searches turned up nothing on point, but absence of search results is weak evidence — the same caution the author rightly applies to their own originality claim.
- I may be wrong about §3. If "could avow to A" is meant to carry an implicit publicity condition — that the reason be one S is prepared to have A know S acted on — then something closer to a response duty may follow. The manuscript does not say this, and if that is the intention it needs stating.
- I disagree with the author's confidence in the Eavesdrop Test but agree with their instinct that something in the vicinity is right. The asymmetry between overhearing and being addressed is real. My claim is only that this particular pair of cases does not isolate it.
