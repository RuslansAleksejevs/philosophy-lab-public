## 2. Order of work

I read the task, brief and rules; then the author's manuscript, source record and process notes; then the critique and the critic's records. Only then did I do source work, and only then did I write.

I read the critique before doing my own verification, which is unavoidable at this stage but has a cost worth recording: I came to the primary texts already told what to look for. I tried to offset this in two ways. Where the critic reported a quotation, I re-retrieved the source and read the surrounding section rather than confirming the sentence in isolation — which is how I found Weltman's bridge analogy in note 34, the part of his bracketed concession that the critique quotes but does not analyse, and which turned out to be where the objection breaks. And I treated the critic's own unverified claims as claims: the Sekine article, which the critic read in full and I could not, is flagged in my `sources.md` as summary-only rather than absorbed on trust.

## 3. Source work

The full record is in `sources.md`. The honest summary: I re-retrieved and read completely the two papers everything turns on (Weltman 2023, Schaber 2021), read Reid's chapter complete again and checked every quotation word by word, read the relevant sections of Piller and of Moore's Raz lecture, and read the Southampton repository record for Whiting. Enoch, Lewis, Gläser, Raz's books, Lance and Kukla, Darwall, Laskowski and Silver, Cupit and Shoemaker remain unread.

Three things are worth recording as process rather than bibliography.

**The retrieval asymmetry.** WebFetch and the hook's separate download disagreed repeatedly and in both directions. WebFetch got a bot-challenge page for Weltman while the hook got the full article; WebFetch got HTTP 403 for the Southampton record while the hook got HTTP 200; WebFetch produced a usable summary of Sekine while the hook's download failed entirely. Two consequences. I learned to try a source even after a WebFetch failure, which is how Moore, Whiting and Piller were obtained. And I learned not to treat a successful WebFetch summary as evidence that a source was read, which is why Sekine is flagged.

**Gläser.** The critic's first priority, and I failed at it. Four attempts, all blocked by the same JavaScript proof-of-work challenge; the saved artefacts record the block rather than the article. I did find a search summary describing his positive view and deliberately did not use it, since the whole point of the criticism was that a snippet is not engagement. What I did instead was to engage the two published *reports* of his view philosophically: if he holds that requests obligate rather than add reasons, then he and I agree on the negative claim and disagree about what is obligatory, and the disagreement is testable against cheap refusal. That is a real engagement with a view I have not read, offered as such.

**The Raz citation chain.** The author flagged Piller's quotation of Raz 1990: 39 as the place a critic should check; the critic could not check it either. I have now read Piller's text directly and can confirm that Piller says what he was reported to say. That closes one link and leaves the other open: nobody in this lineage has read Raz's book. A complete text circulates on a file-sharing site. I found it and did not use it, following the policy the author set when he declined a re-upload of Lance and Kukla. The postscript at pp. 178ff., which the critic rightly says raises the stakes, therefore remains unread, and the manuscript says so rather than implying otherwise.

## 4. How the argument changed, and what I tried that failed

The substantive accounting is in `response.md`. Here I record the shape of the work and the approaches I abandoned.

**The productive move was to stop defending the dilemma and start using the opponents' own category.** My first attempt at §2 tried to save the original argument by arguing that non-obligatoriness must reduce to low weight — the critic's option (a). I drafted it and dropped it. It requires a general thesis about the relation between deontic categories and scalar ones that I could not defend in a paragraph, and it is not needed: the deontic retreat *concedes* the point once you notice that blame has migrated from the act to the attention. Turning an objection into the third horn is better than answering it.

**Two repairs of the Agenda Thesis were tried before the one that worked.** The first was to add a fourth clause — "and S must reply" — which is exactly the bolting-on the critic said could not be done, and he was right: it explains nothing and predicts none of the exceptions. The second was to make avowability publicity-involving (the reason must be one S is prepared to have A know she acted on), which the critic floats in his §13. That is closer, but it still yields no duty to speak, only a constraint on motives. The version I kept derives the duty from the fact that answerability is a *relation* whose discharge depends on what the other party can know. Its merit is that it predicts the cases where nothing need be said, which a postulated reply-duty cannot.

**A defence of the Eavesdrop Test was drafted and discarded.** I tried to save it by stipulating that the overheard speaker is unaware and indifferent, so that no norm against eavesdropping bites. It does not work: as the critic says, if there is no addressee there is nobody to be answerable to, so the asymmetry is over-explained. Replacing the test was the right call, and the replacement is better than a repair would have been, because Raz's "I am not asking" case holds common knowledge fixed by construction.

**I nearly kept the term "inclusionary reason" with a narrowed definition.** The temptation was real, since the symmetry with exclusionary reasons is the essay's most memorable line. I dropped it because the narrowed definition no longer picks out the positive counterpart of anything — the thing I care about, deliberative uptake, is not a species of Raz's genus at all — and a label that advertises a symmetry the view has abandoned is worse than no label. "Agenda reasons" is duller and accurate.

**An extension I drafted and cut: promising.** I sketched an argument that the Agenda Thesis explains why a promise must be accepted, or at least not rejected, to bind. It is suggestive and it is the obvious next case, but I could not make it more than suggestive without sources on promissory acceptance that I did not have, and the length limit was already pressing. The related point that survives — that requests are the ordinary gateway to obligation because a question opened by a request can be closed by tacit acceptance — is the part I could defend.

**A formal apparatus was not attempted.** The author considered and rejected one; the critic considered and rejected a formal reconstruction of his own. I agree with both. The structural claims in §3 of the final (defeat, deferral, queueing, composition) are the closest the view comes to a logic, and stating them in English costs nothing and overstates nothing.

## 5. The length problem, and what it cost

The first complete revision, `final-001.md`, was the manuscript I would choose on philosophical grounds. It was measured as over the limit, and four successive condensations followed, each preserving the argument and cutting exposition. `final-005.md` is the selected final and is very compressed: it keeps the puzzle, the three theses, all three horns, the defences argument, the full Agenda Thesis with the derivation of the account clause, one of the three tests of the negative claim, the Disclaimed-Request Test, the reply to Weltman's bracket, and the taxonomic correction.

What was lost is worth stating plainly, since a reader comparing versions will notice. `final-005` states the five data compactly inside the argument rather than setting them out as a numbered list; it keeps only the confidentiality case of the three counterexamples to the negative claim, dropping the lift and the withdrawal cases; it compresses the survey of the literature to a sentence apiece; and it carries eight notes instead of fourteen. Nothing was cut because it was hard to defend. The fuller treatment of the dropped material is in `final-001.md`, which is preserved for exactly that reason, and the version lineage below records which is which.

One process error of mine is worth recording: an early attempt at a fifth version was returned with an unescaped line break and was not saved, which cost a cycle. The content was resent correctly.

## 6. What I think is still weakest

1. **The negative claim.** It is a universal negative defended case by case. Three hard cases come out right, but that is not a proof, and the confidentiality reply — that gravity tracks acceptance rather than address — carries a lot of weight for a paragraph. If someone produces a case where an undertaking is plainly absent and the asking still seems to make the balance heavier, the claim goes.

2. **The queueing concession.** Granting that weight orders the agenda queue is honest and, I think, forced. But it puts weight-like structure inside the second dimension, and someone could press that the line between ordering a queue and deciding its membership is less sharp than I make it. I do not have a principled account of why membership is insensitive to weight while order is not, beyond the observation that unanswered requests leave residues and outweighed reasons do not.

3. **The derivation of the account clause.** It turns on the requester's epistemic position, which makes the duty to speak contingent on facts about what people can know about one another. I think that is the right result — it explains the exceptions — but it means the duty is not a bare consequence of being asked, and a reader who wants answerability to be more robustly deontic will find it thin.

4. **Gläser, still.** The nearest rival remains unread by anyone in this lineage. My engagement with him is an engagement with two sentences quoted by others.

5. **Sekine, and the originality claim.** I rely on a machine summary for the shape of the positive second-order reasons literature, with Whiting's abstract as the part I actually read. More importantly, neither I nor the critic could establish whether anyone has previously theorised a reason bearing specifically on deliberative uptake. My searches, like theirs, returned nothing on point, and absence of search results is weak evidence. The originality claim in the final is deliberately narrow — that the standard taxonomy lacks the category — rather than a claim that nobody has had the thought.

## 7. Remaining disagreements

With the critic: I resist the claim that the negative claim is probably false, the withdrawal case as a counterexample, the salt case as showing that the positive thesis wins only verbally, and the suggestion that the account might drop Raz altogether. Reasons are in `response.md`. I accept the rest, including the criticisms that cost the essay its two most quotable formulations.

With the literature: against Weltman, I accept his negative claim and reject his conclusion that requests are normatively inert, and I now argue against his bracketing of interpersonal responsibility rather than merely noting it. Against Schaber, I accept that requesting is a normative power and deny that it produces a first-order reason of constant weight; I also reject his treatment of respectfulness as a success condition. Against Enoch and Lewis, as reported, I think the discretion they explain by weak or attitude-dependent reasons is better explained by a burden of production. With Gläser, agreement on the negative half and an unresolved disagreement about what the request makes obligatory. With Reid, agreement — but he is used as a diagnosis of a blind spot, not as an authority, and he did not hold my thesis.
