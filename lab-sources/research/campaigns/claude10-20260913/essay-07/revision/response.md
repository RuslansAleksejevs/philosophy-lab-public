# Response to `critique.md` — essay-07-revision

What follows records which criticisms I accepted, which I resisted, and what changed in the manuscript. Section numbers in bold are the critic's; the manuscript referred to is `final-005.md` (the selected final), with `final-001.md` the full-length version of the same revision.

## 1. Accepted in full

**§1 — the architecture proves less than the essay claimed.** Accepted without reservation. The Attention–Weight Dilemma is an *incompleteness* argument: it shows that weights alone cannot deliver costly silence, not that a weight theorist is refuted. The manuscript now says so explicitly and drops the claims that the debate has been "conducted on the wrong axis" as a refutation and that no weight-based account "can capture" the profile. The hybrid view (weight *plus* agenda) is acknowledged as available off the shelf. This cost some rhetoric and no substance, and the critic is right that it removes the cheapest way to dismiss the paper.

**§2 — Horn 2 misdescribed Schaber.** Accepted, and this was the clearest error. Schaber writes that "[t]he reasons requests create have always the same weight"; he nowhere says the weight is small. My draft's "modest" was an interpolation, and it was exactly the interpolation Horn 2 needed. I verified the passage directly in this stage. The manuscript now flags the interpolation as mine (note 2 of `final-005`).

**§2 — the obligatory/non-obligatory distinction was missing.** Accepted, and it turned into the best thing in the revision. Schaber, following Lewis, defines a non-obligatory reason as one it would not be blameworthy to fail to act on. I took the critic's option (b) — grant the opponents a non-scalar explanation of cheap refusal — but did not stop there. The retreat concedes my thesis: if it is *never blameworthy not to act on* a request-created reason, and yet blameworthy not to *attend* to it, then on a weight-based account the criticism of inattention has lost its normal ground, since inattention is criticisable only because it risks acting wrongly, and here there is no wrong act to risk. This is now Horn 3 and is the argument I would most like to be judged on.

**§2 — Cupit.** Accepted. He is dropped from the Weight Model list; his title and Schaber's n. 8 place him among obligation theorists, and I have not read him.

**§3 — the Agenda Thesis did not entail costly silence.** Accepted; this was the deepest problem and I had not seen it. Counterfactual avowability is satisfied by the addressee who silently considers, silently declines on deadline, and walks on without a word — which is the very wrong the essay was built to explain. I resist only the critic's claim that the clause cannot be added without wrecking the machinery (see §2 below).

**§4 — the second dimension had no structure.** Accepted. The manuscript now gives defeat conditions (incapacity and more urgent conscriptions of attention, not proportionality), the prediction that a defeated agenda item leaves *deferred* rather than extinguished answerability, a conflict rule (weight orders the queue, not its membership), and an interaction rule (composition rather than addition). I also withdrew the overreach that the requirement "persists where considering is costly," which contradicted the neighbouring claim that the costs are trivial.

**§5 — the hedge in T1.** Accepted. "No weight — or no weight worth the name" is gone. T1 is now the flat claim, which is falsifiable in one move, as it should be.

**§6 — D5 is under-tested.** Accepted. The drowning swimmer is a badly chosen case, since it is built so that any increment is swamped. `final-001` §7 runs the critic's three counter-cases; `final-005` retains the hardest of them.

**§7 — the Eavesdrop Test.** Accepted and replaced. The critic is right that it varies common knowledge rather than information, and that eavesdropping is an independent confound. The replacement is the critic's own suggestion — Raz's "I am not asking" case, which the essay already had in a footnote — now run as the Disclaimed-Request Test.

**§8.2 — T3 will not survive the second-order reasons literature.** Accepted. "Positive second-order reason" is the settled name and the category is occupied. I have dropped my coinage "inclusionary reason" and the symmetry it promised, and taken the critic's suggested repair: clause (ii) of the Agenda Thesis *is* a positive second-order reason; clause (i) is not, since considering is not acting for a reason. The surviving claim — that requests reveal *agenda reasons*, bearing on deliberative uptake, which Raz's disjunctive definition does not reach — is more defensible and, I think, more interesting. The essay also now notes Whiting's challenge, notes that clause (i) survives it, and cites Darwall as an unpaid debt.

**§8.3, §10.6, §10.7, §10.9 — overstatements.** Accepted. The procedural-rights claim is reduced to "a puzzle the picture is placed to address"; the remark that refusing to your face wrongs you "less thoroughly" is cut rather than left undefended; the questions paragraph now deploys the standing apparatus; D4 concedes that sometimes a little more than an account is owed.

**§10.1 — standing.** Accepted as a forced choice, resolved against Schaber: standing is a *permission* condition, not a success condition. The ill-standing request lands, which is why it is an imposition and why "you have no business asking" is apt rather than idle.

**§10.2 — avowability.** Accepted. The operative condition is *sincerity*: the reason offered must be the reason acted on. Named as such.

**§10.3 — coin flips.** Accepted; the theory over-predicted. Choosing by lot between equally good options is an adequate account, since the lottery was itself adopted for a reason. What fails is drift, not randomisation.

**§10.4 — non-aggregation.** Partly accepted. Idempotence is now relative to *the question*, so a renewed asking after changed circumstances renews the claim. I also withdrew the jibe that a diminishing-returns function is "ad hoc"; the fair comparison is that one model derives idempotence and the other fits a curve.

**§10.8 — Laskowski and Silver.** Accepted. The essay now offers a *rival* explanation of their datum rather than "a home" for their view, and records Weltman's report that their account supports his.

**§9 — citation corrections.** Accepted. Laskowski and Silver are dated 2021, per Weltman's reference list, which I re-read. Piller is a published 2006 chapter, not an author's manuscript. Enoch's condition-count discrepancy between Weltman and Schaber is noted.

**§7 (process note) — the record/manuscript drift.** Accepted as a fact. The author's `sources.md` said the essay described Gläser as an obligation theorist; the draft did not. Both are repaired here: Gläser is described accurately in the manuscript and accurately in my `sources.md`.

## 2. Accepted with a different remedy

**§3 — the dilemma between counterfactual avowability and an actual response duty.** The critic says the author "cannot simply bolt the clause on," because if actual response is required then avowability is not the operative condition and the work on pretexts is doing something other than it advertises. I agree about the diagnosis and disagree about the remedy.

The response duty is not an extra clause but a *consequence* of answerability under ordinary epistemic conditions. Answerability is a relation: a standing liability to be called to account by a particular person. A liability is discharged however the circumstances allow; and since the requester cannot see into the addressee's head, the only available discharge is normally speech. Two things follow, and both count in the account's favour. The exceptions are predicted rather than stipulated — nothing need be said where compliance is itself manifest, where the requester has waived reply, or where she is unreachable — which a bare "you must reply" postulate could not explain. And the sincerity condition keeps its job, since it now governs the *content* of the account rather than substituting for it.

The critic adds that an actual-response requirement makes the account look like a norm of communicative uptake and so makes the reduction objection harder. Section 4 of `final-005` answers this: uptake norms apply to *any* address, including the disclaimed non-request, which is precisely why they cannot be what distinguishes a request from it.

**§8.1 — Gläser.** Accepted as the first priority, and acted on without success. I made four independent attempts on the Zurich repository (the bitstream URL the critic supplied, the legacy eprint page, the new entities URL, and a search for an alternative host). Every one returned an Anubis proof-of-work challenge page rather than the article; the saved retrieval metadata records HTTP 200 with a 2.3 KB challenge body. I have therefore done the next best thing: Gläser is now named in the survey as the nearest rival, described accurately from Weltman's and Schaber's quotations (both of which I re-read in full myself in this stage), and engaged philosophically — the disagreement is localised to whether what the request makes obligatory is φ-ing or the taking-up of the question, and it is testable against cheap refusal. I flag him as the place where an informed reader should press hardest.

## 3. Resisted, with reasons

**§5 — "T1 and Answerability are in tension; the salt case makes T1 a verbal victory."** I resist. The critic says that where S has no competing reason, the Agenda Thesis entails that S must pass the salt, generating a practical requirement where before the request there was none. But "before the request" smuggles in an information difference, which is the one control D5 insists on. Hold information fixed: S sees that A wants the salt but A, being scrupulous, does not ask. Must S pass it? I think so; it would be churlish not to. The requirement was already there. What the request adds is that if S does not pass the salt, S must now say something. That is exactly the division T1 and T2 predict.

The critic's follow-up — that "I'd rather not" is always available, so answerability constrains almost nothing — I accept as stated and deny is a problem. Answerability is not a constraint on *what S does*; it is a requirement that S engage and own the outcome. Whether "I'd rather not" is a *good* reason is settled by the first-order balance, which the request did not touch. That the account leaves this entirely to the balance is the point, not a defect.

**§6 — withdrawal as a counterexample to T1.** I resist. The case conflates retraction with counter-request. "Please don't" is a second request with the opposite content, and of course it changes things: it puts not-φ-ing on my agenda. The genuine test is "never mind, forget I asked," and there my reasons do revert to what my information supports, the only residue being that I am released from answerability — after which reading the draft anyway is officious rather than wrong. The asymmetry between the two locutions is itself evidence for the view.

**§6 — confidentiality as a counterexample to T1.** I resist the conclusion while accepting that my draft's reply was borrowed rather than argued. The new reply, given in my own voice, is that the gravity of the betrayal tracks *acceptance*, not address: say "I'm not promising anything — I'm a terrible gossip," and later telling is indiscretion rather than betrayal. T1 says only that the *act of asking* adds no weight; accepted requests create commitments, and commitments are weighty. This turned out to strengthen the account rather than merely to defend it, since it explains why tacit acceptance is possible at all — silence can function as acceptance only where silence is not neutral, and it is the request that makes saying nothing a move. On a weight model, silence before a request carries no more significance than silence before a penny on the pavement.

**§7(c) — Weltman's bracketed concession.** The critic calls this the single most important unanswered objection, and I agree it was. I do not accept that it is unanswerable. I re-read §5.1 and its note myself. Three replies are now in the manuscript: that on the question Weltman poses he is right and T1 agrees with him; that "the normativity we are interested in" is a stipulation about the topic rather than a finding about requests; and — the reply I think decisive — that his bridge analogy misdescribes the structure, since there the culpability attaches to the *revealer* and nothing whatever is required of his opponents, whereas the liability a request creates falls on the *addressee*. One may bracket a speaker's responsibility for a hearer's conduct and still owe an account of a requirement that falls on the hearer.

**§10.5 — "§6.1's claim that the request abandons the Weight Model's commitment is wrong."** Accepted as a criticism of the old text and handled structurally rather than locally: once the argument is reframed as incompleteness, the observation that a weight theorist may hold both a reason to φ and a reason to consider is no longer an objection but the position I am describing. The old reduction-objection paragraph, which claimed otherwise, is gone.

**§8.2 — the invitation to abandon Raz altogether.** Partly resisted. The critic suggests the essay may not need Raz at all. I keep the Razian framing because the *negative* half of the taxonomy is what makes the positive gap visible, and because the received view about requests is stated inside that framework — Moore, whose lecture I retrieved and read in this stage, records that requests are illuminated by the *absence* of exclusionary reasons. That is exactly half the story, and saying so requires the framework. But I now note explicitly that clause (i) survives if Whiting's attack succeeds, so the account is not hostage to the dispute.

## 4. Where the critic changed my mind about my own strengths

The critic thinks the argument from what we build defences against — do-not-disturb settings, gatekeepers, conventions of indirection — is stronger than the dilemma, and should be promoted out of the objection-replies. I agree and have promoted it. In `final-005` it stands as independent evidence at the end of §2, with the reasoning made explicit: nobody builds institutions against small increments to their reasons.

## 5. Material changes, in brief

Dropped: the coinage "inclusionary reason"; the claim that the positive second-order slot is empty and unnamed; the Eavesdrop Test; the reduction-objection section; the hedge in T1; Cupit from the Weight Model; the "modest" gloss on Schaber; the unsupported claim about one-dimensional views and procedural rights; the closing line of the bigot reply.

Added: Horn 3 and the argument from non-obligatoriness; the Account clause and its derivation; the sincerity condition; standing as a permission condition; defeat, deferral, queueing and composition; three tests of T1 including the acceptance analysis of confidences; the Disclaimed-Request Test; the three replies to Weltman's bracket; the agenda-reason taxonomy with Piller, Moore, Whiting and Sekine; explicit engagement with Gläser.

Retained against criticism: T1 in its strong form; the two-dimensional architecture; Permitted Inattention (which the critic tried to break and could not); Reid as diagnosis rather than authority; and the honest disclosure of citation chains, which is if anything more extensive now.
