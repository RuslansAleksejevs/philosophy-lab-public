# Process — essay-07-author, initial manuscript stage

## 1. What this stage was

This was the authoring stage: from a blank brief (no topic, no thesis, no field) through source
research to a finished first manuscript, `draft-001.md`. No critic has seen it. I did not
simulate a critic or a revision, and nothing here anticipates what a critic will say beyond
flagging where I think the argument is most exposed.

Inputs read: `NEUTRAL_BRIEF.md`, `RULES.md`, `task.md`. Nothing else was read from the project.
Outputs: `draft-001.md`, `sources.md`, `process.md`.

## 2. Topic selection, and what was set aside

I began by generating candidate topics rather than candidate phrasings, on the view that novel
wording is not originality. Ten or so were considered; five got as far as a sketched thesis
before being dropped. Recording them, with reasons:

- **Moral indeterminacy and decision.** Thesis sketched: where it is indeterminate whether an
  act is wrong, an agent's own decision plus subsequent commitment can function as a
  precisification, so that deciding partly makes the moral status determinate — a normative
  power operating at the margins, analogous to a court making law in a gap. *Dropped* because the
  attractive version is close to unbelievable (decisions do not change moral facts) and the
  believable version collapses into the familiar claim that indeterminacy leaves a permission.
  I could not find a middle position I was willing to defend.

- **Sunk costs and "completion value".** Thesis sketched: a class of apparently sunk-cost
  reasoning is rational because partial investment creates a good — the completed whole — that
  is not otherwise available, so the reason is forward-looking after all. *Dropped* as likely to
  rediscover existing work on acting for the sake of the past and on narrative value in a life,
  which I could not check adequately without access to the relevant papers.

- **Norms of inquiry (the "zetetic" literature).** Thesis sketched: the tension between
  instrumental norms on inquiry and evidential norms on belief dissolves if inquiry is governed
  by a constraint on the well-formedness of questions rather than by instrumental rationality.
  *Dropped* on access grounds: the central papers are in subscription journals and I judged the
  risk of arguing against a position I could only see through summaries to be too high.

- **Future bias and temporal neutrality.** *Dropped* as crowded, and because my candidate moves
  (linking the bias to deliberative availability rather than to time) seemed likely to be
  already made.

- **Precedent as a source of reasons, bridging legal *stare decisis* and Bratman-style planning.**
  *Dropped* as too large for the length and too dependent on legal-philosophy sources I did not
  expect to be able to read in full.

The topic I kept — what the act of asking does normatively — was chosen for three reasons.
First, it has a live, well-defined debate with identifiable positions. Second, and decisively,
much of that debate is in open-access venues (Ergo, Philosophers' Imprint, an open-access
*Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* article), so genuine primary-source engagement was feasible
rather than aspirational. Third, while reading Weltman I found a footnote (his n. 40) in which
the possibility I wanted to defend is raised and dismissed in a sentence. A gap that a
participant in the debate has explicitly noticed and waved away is a better place to stand than
a gap nobody has noticed, because it is harder to be accidentally unoriginal there.

## 3. How the thesis developed

The first version of the thesis was weaker and, I now think, worse: that requests give a reason
of very small weight *and*, in addition, generate a duty of consideration. Call it the
epsilon-plus-attention view. I abandoned it for two reasons. It is unstable — once you have the
duty of consideration doing the explanatory work, the epsilon is idle, and postulating an idle
quantity is a defect. And it fails the test I eventually built §3's D5 around: when I tried to
find a case where the *bare act of asking* made the case for complying stronger, holding fixed
the underlying facts and the addressee's knowledge, I could not construct one. So the negative
claim (T1) hardened from "very little weight" to "no weight", which is both cleaner and much
more exposed to counterexample. I regard that as an improvement: it is falsifiable in one move.

The central argument (the Attention–Weight Dilemma, §4) went through three drafts in outline.
Version 1 argued that epsilon-weight reasons "cannot change the outcome" and so are ignorable.
That is false as stated — an epsilon can break a tie — and I rewrote it around the notions of
*idleness* and *reasonable belief in idleness*, which survives the tie-breaking objection
(handled as the third reply in §4). Version 2 tried to argue that the transition from "no
competing reason" to "obligated" is discontinuous in a way weights cannot capture. I dropped
that because it is not true: an undefeated epsilon does determine what one ought to do, so the
weight theorist gets the same verdict. I mention this because it is the sort of argument that
looks good for a page and is simply a mistake, and a critic should know I considered and
rejected it rather than never having seen it.

The taxonomic claim (T3) came last, and came from reading Piller's quotation of Raz's
definition. Raz's genus is explicitly disjunctive — reasons to act for a reason, or to refrain
from acting for a reason — and only the second disjunct gets a theory. That is a structural gap
in an extremely well-known framework, and it gave me a name and a place in the literature for
what requests do. It is also the claim I am least able to verify, since I could not read Raz's
book, and since a search snippet suggests the phrase "inclusionary reasons" is already in use
somewhere in the Razian literature I could not access.

The Eavesdrop Test (§6.6) was built specifically to discriminate my view from the epistemic
account, after I noticed that Weltman's replies to the "no new information" objection all work
by finding *more* information in the request. The test inverts that: it loads the non-request
case with more information than the request case, and asks whether answerability follows the
information. I think it does not. This is the argument I would most like a critic to attack.

## 4. Formal apparatus considered and rejected

I drafted and discarded a two-place representation of reasons as ordered pairs of weight and
uptake-demand, with an algebra for how requests, commands and consent act on each coordinate.
It added notation without adding content: everything it expressed was already expressed by the
Agenda Thesis plus the production/persuasion distinction, and the ordering relation on the
second coordinate was doing no work. The brief permits technical apparatus "when it helps the
argument", and this did not.

I also considered a supervaluationist or default-logic rendering of the burden of production
and set it aside for the same reason.

## 5. Source work, and its limits

The full record is in `sources.md`. The honest summary is that I read two contemporary papers
completely (Weltman 2023, Schaber 2021), one historical chapter completely (Reid, *Intellectual
Powers* I.8, in an abridged edition), and located portions of two more (Raz 2006, Piller), and
that the two papers most central to the debate — Enoch 2011 and Lewis 2018 — were unobtainable.
Every host that carries them returned HTTP 403 to every route I tried. I did not use an
unauthorised re-upload of Lance and Kukla that I found.

What this means for the essay: my characterisations of Enoch and Lewis are built from two
independent secondary reports that quote them with page numbers and agree with each other.
That is decent evidence but it is not reading them, and the essay's notes 1 and 2 say so. The
risk is concentrated in one place: I attribute to the whole Enoch–Lewis–Schaber family the view
that a request adds a first-order pro tanto reason of positive weight. Schaber plainly holds
this. Enoch's "triggering" apparatus might be read as making the request's contribution wholly
inherited from a background conditional, in which case he is nearer my position than I allow.
If a critic has the papers, that is the first thing to check.

Two smaller honesty notes. The Reid text is uncorrected OCR of a 1941 abridgement; the page
numbers are that edition's, not the standard ones, and I say so. And I could not establish
whether anyone has already proposed that requests shift a justificatory burden rather than add
weight. Searches on that description returned nothing on point, but absence of search results is
weak evidence, and I would not want the originality claim to rest on it.

## 6. What I think is weakest

1. **Permitted Inattention.** The dilemma's second horn depends on it. It is stated loosely
   ("not open to criticism"), and someone could insist that we are always criticisable for
   inattention, merely excused by our finitude. I think that reading makes deliberation
   impossible, but I do not argue for it at length.

2. **The avowability condition.** It does a great deal of work — it rules out pretexts, coin
   flips and sheer drift — and it is given about a paragraph. In particular I do not say what
   makes a reason "of the right kind" to be avowed, and the permissiveness objection (§6.2) is
   answered by distinguishing discharging a burden from acting well, which some will find too
   quick.

3. **The scope of T1.** "Asking adds no weight" is very strong. The hardest cases are ones
   where asking seems constitutive rather than informative — consent, invitation, the
   paternalism cases Weltman discusses in his §4. I have a reply (those are consent, not
   request), but I took it over from Weltman rather than defending it, and a critic could
   reasonably press that a view on which requests are weightless owes an account of why
   requesting is nonetheless often the *only* permissible route to an outcome.

4. **Aggregation.** D3 concerns repetition by one person. I assert but do not argue that
   requests by many people also fail to aggregate, and the essay does not discuss the case where
   ten different people each ask for a different small favour.

5. **The extensions (§7)** are suggestive rather than argued. The procedural-rights section in
   particular asserts that one-dimensional views "keep failing" to account for such rights,
   which is a large claim resting on no cited work.

## 7. Remaining disagreements with the literature

- Against Weltman: I accept his negative claim about first-order weight and reject his
  conclusion that requests are normatively inert. The disagreement is sharp and localised at his
  n. 40.
- Against Schaber: I accept that requests are exercises of a normative power and deny that the
  power produces a reason of constant weight. His constant-weight claim is, as far as I can see,
  the cleanest statement of the position Horn 2 refutes.
- Against Enoch and Lewis, as reported: I think the discretion they try to explain by making the
  created reason weak or attitude-dependent is better explained by making it second-order.
- With Reid: agreement, but he is used as a diagnosis of a blind spot, not as an authority. He
  did not hold my thesis and I do not say he did.
