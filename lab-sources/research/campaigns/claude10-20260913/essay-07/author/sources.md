# Sources — essay-07-author, draft-001

This file records what I actually read, how I read it, and what I could not get. I distinguish
three levels of access:

- **FULL** — I read the complete extracted text of the original source, saved locally by the
  download hook, and quoted from it directly.
- **PARTIAL** — I read a located portion of the original (chapter, section, or page range) and
  quoted only from what I read.
- **UNAVAILABLE** — the original could not be retrieved. Where I nevertheless cite such a work,
  I cite it *through* a quotation appearing in a source I did read, and I say so in the essay's
  footnotes. No claim in the essay rests on unread text.

Saved-source paths below are relative to the task folder
`batches/claude10-20260913/tasks/essay-07-author/`. Files with a `.original.*` extension are the
unmodified downloaded bytes; the `.txt` files are the hook's text extraction; the `.json` files
are its retrieval metadata (URL, HTTP status, content type, SHA-256, extraction method).

### 1. Daniel Weltman, "What Makes Requests Normative? The Epistemic Account Defended"
- *Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy* 9 (2023). DOI: 10.3998/ergo.3589.
- URL retrieved: https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/ergo/article/id/3589/print/
- Saved: `sources/b827fc24793cc02acf2d-fd66a7ad4f55.txt` (extraction),
  `sources/b827fc24793cc02acf2d-fd66a7ad4f55.original.html`,
  `sources/b827fc24793cc02acf2d-fd66a7ad4f55.json`
- Coverage: **FULL** — read complete, including all endnotes and the reference list.
- Locations used in the essay: §1 (statement of the epistemic account); §1 and §2 (reports of
  Enoch's three intention conditions and of Lewis's discretionary-value condition); §5.2 and
  note 40 (the Laskowski–Silver attention point and Weltman's dismissal of it); §6 ("requests
  aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking").
- Role: principal contemporary opponent for the negative half of my thesis, and the source of
  the footnote (n. 40) that my §2 takes as its point of departure.

### 2. Peter Schaber, "The Reason-Giving Force of Requests"

  Open access (CC BY 4.0), Universität Zürich.
- URL retrieved: https://d-nb.info/1233159097/34 (publisher PDF via Deutsche Nationalbibliothek)

  `sources/79d7693953f884a2198e-c84e702742da.original.pdf`,
  `sources/79d7693953f884a2198e-c84e702742da.json`
- Coverage: **FULL** — read complete, including footnotes and references.
- Locations used: §2 (what requests are; the two Raz quotations); §3 (the value-based account);
  §4 ("The reasons requests create have always the same weight", and the claim that apparent

  requests place requestees under an obligation rather than adding reasons).
- Role: the clearest statement of the constant-weight version of the Weight Model, which
  Horn 2 of my dilemma targets.

- Essay I, ch. 8, "Of Social Operations of Mind." Edition scanned: the abridged edition edited
  by A. D. Woozley (1941); chapter occupies pp. 48–51 of that edition. First published 1785.
- URL retrieved: https://archive.org/stream/essaysontheintel007938mbp/essaysontheintel007938mbp_djvu.txt
- Saved: `sources/73bea62982022bc9239d-eeb60c2d2010.txt` (extraction),
  `sources/73bea62982022bc9239d-eeb60c2d2010.original.html`,
  `sources/73bea62982022bc9239d-eeb60c2d2010.json`
- Coverage: **PARTIAL** — I located and read Essay I ch. 8 in full (lines 5460–5595 of the
  extracted text), plus surrounding context to fix the chapter boundaries. I did not read the
  rest of the work. All four Reid quotations in the essay come from this chapter.
- Caveats recorded honestly: (a) this is an *abridged* edition — the editor states that the
  preceding chapter 7 was omitted — so the page numbers are Woozley's, not those of the
  standard Hamilton or Brookes editions; (b) the text is uncorrected OCR of a 1941 printing,
  and I normalised nothing beyond obvious line-break hyphenation when quoting; (c) Reid's own
  cross-reference in this chapter is to Hume's *Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals*,
  §3 pt. 2, note near the end, which I did not consult.

### 5. Christian Piller, "Kinds of Practical Reasons: Attitude-Related Reasons and Exclusionary Reasons"
- Author's PDF hosted at the University of York (undated on the copy retrieved).
- URL retrieved: https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~cjp7/Recent%20and%20Forthcoming/Attitude-RelatedReasonsandExclusionaryReasons.pdf
- Saved: `sources/2e22e3a0e2ced16f449e-d5bc68960559.txt` (pdftotext extraction),
  `sources/2e22e3a0e2ced16f449e-d5bc68960559.original.pdf`,
  `sources/2e22e3a0e2ced16f449e-d5bc68960559.json`
- Coverage: **PARTIAL** — read §§1–3 (roughly the first half), covering attitude-related
  reasons, Raz's exclusionary reasons, Raz's two illustrative cases (the tired investor; Jeremy
  and the commanding officer), and the comparison of exclusion with outweighing and
  undermining. I did not read the concluding sections.
- Critical role: Piller quotes Raz, *Practical Reason and Norms* (1990 ed.), 39 — "A second-order
  reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is
  a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason" — and also Raz 1990, 38 and 41.
  My taxonomic claim (T3) turns on the first of these: the definition of the genus is
  disjunctive, and Raz develops only the second disjunct. I am relying on Piller's accuracy as
  a quoter, since I could not obtain Raz's book; this is flagged in essay n. 6 and is the single
  most important place where a critic should check my chain of citation.

## UNAVAILABLE (cited only through quotations in sources I did read)

Each of the following was sought and could not be retrieved; every attempt returned HTTP 403
from the host (quod.lib.umich.edu, philarchive.org, philpapers.org, onlinelibrary.wiley.com,
papers.ssrn.com). No OCR or alternative extraction was available to me, and I did not use
unauthorised re-uploads (a copy of Lance and Kukla appeared on a document-sharing site; I did
not use it).

1. **David Enoch, "Giving Practical Reasons," *Philosophers' Imprint* 11:4 (2011), 1–22.**
   Attempted: the journal PDF and text views at quod.lib.umich.edu (several URL forms), the
   author's Hebrew University publications page (retrieved, but it only supplies the same
   blocked PDF link), SSRN, Semantic Scholar, CORE, and the successor platform at
   journals.publishing.umich.edu. Cited in the essay through Schaber (2021, §6), who sets out
   conditions (i)–(iii) quoting Enoch 2011: 15, and through Weltman (2023, §§1–3), who quotes
   Enoch 2011 at 2, 4, 7, 14 and 15. The two reports agree, which gives me reasonable confidence
   in the attribution; I have not seen Enoch's own framing of the epistemic/triggering/robust
   trichotomy in his words beyond the phrases those two authors quote.

2. **James H. P. Lewis, "The Discretionary Normativity of Requests," *Philosophers' Imprint*
   18:20 (2018), 1–16.** Attempted: philarchive.org/archive/LEWTDN, philpapers.org/archive/
   LEWTDN.pdf, and the quod.lib.umich.edu PDF. Cited through Schaber (2021, §5), who quotes
   Lewis at 2, 6, 11, 12, 13 and 14, and through Weltman (2023, §§1.1, 2).

3. **N. G. Laskowski and Kenneth Silver, "Wronging by Requesting," in M. Timmons (ed.),
   *Oxford Studies in Normative Ethics* vol. 11 (OUP, 2022), 49–69.** Attempted:
   philarchive.org and philpapers.org archive copies. Described in the essay only as reported by
   Weltman (2023, §§1, 5.2): the Epistemic-Evidential view, and the claim that a request can
   make an addressee attend to reasons already known. Their positive thesis about trust and
   disrespect I know only from a search-result abstract and do not rely on.

4. **Mark Lance and Rebecca (Quill) Kukla, "Leave the Gun; Take the Cannoli! The Pragmatic
   Topography of Second-Person Calls," *Ethics* 123:3 (2013), 456–478.** Not retrieved. The two
   sentences I quote (at 460 and 470) are taken from Schaber (2021, n. 3) and Weltman (2023, §1)
   respectively.

5. **Joseph Raz, *Practical Reason and Norms* (1975; 1990 ed.)** and **Joseph Raz, *The Morality
   of Freedom* (1986).** Books, not retrieved. Quoted at 1990: 38, 39, 41 through Piller, and at
   1975: 83 and 1986: 36–37 through Schaber (2021, §2) and Weltman (2023, §1).

6. **Geoffrey Cupit, "How Requests (and Promises) Create Obligations," *Philosophical Quarterly*
   44 (1994), 439–455.** Not retrieved; referred to in the essay only as one of the weight-adding
   views, on the strength of the sentence Weltman quotes at 449.

7. **Micha Gläser, "The Normative Structure of Request," in *Oxford Studies in Normative Ethics*
   vol. 9 (OUP, 2019), 29–49.** Not retrieved. Mentioned once in the essay as holding that
   requests obligate rather than add reasons, on the strength of Schaber's n. 1 (citing Gläser
   2019: 42).

8. **Ezequiel Monti, "Against Triggering Accounts of Robust Reason-Giving," *Philosophical
   Studies* 178 (2021), 3731–53.** Not retrieved; not relied on in the essay.

9. **Edna Ullmann-Margalit, "On Presumption," *Journal of Philosophy* 80:3 (1983), 143–163.**
   Not retrieved (paywalled at PDC). Flagged in essay n. 8 as the nearest philosophical treatment
   of presumption-as-burden-shifting that I am aware of, explicitly as something I did not read
   and on which nothing in the argument depends. My use of the production/persuasion distinction
   in §5 is the standard evidentiary one and is not attributed to her.

10. **Milan Mossé, "How Requests Create Reasons," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*
    (forthcoming, 2026).** Located on the author's page and on PhilPapers; the PDF returned 403.
    I read only the publicly displayed abstract, on which the view appears to be that a request
    creates a reason when treating such requests as reasons would help the addressee conform
    better to their duties. This is a further Weight Model and would fall under my §4 dilemma,
    but since I have not read the paper I make no claim about it in the essay and do not cite it.

11. **J. J. Moreso, "On the Exclusionary Scope of Razian Reasons," *Ratio Juris* (2024).**
    Not retrieved (Wiley returned 403). A search snippet from this article is the reason I
    believe the phrase "inclusionary reasons" already has some currency in the Razian literature,
    which is why essay n. 7 disclaims priority in the label. I have not seen how the term is used
    there, and a critic should treat my disclaimer as deliberately cautious rather than informed.

12. **Stephen Darwall, *The Second-Person Standpoint* (2006).** Not retrieved; the essay alludes
    to "the tradition that treats second-personal address as sui generis" without attributing a
    specific claim to Darwall, precisely because I did not read him.
