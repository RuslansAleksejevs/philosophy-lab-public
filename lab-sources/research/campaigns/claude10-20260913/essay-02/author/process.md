## How the topic was chosen

I generated a spread of candidate areas and assessed each for (a) whether I
could state a thesis that was genuinely mine rather than a repackaging, (b)
whether primary literature was actually obtainable, and (c) whether the result
would be argumentatively tight at essay length. Candidates considered and set
aside, with reasons:

- **Zetetic epistemology (norms of inquiry, when one may stop inquiring).** A
  live and under-explored area. Set aside because my best idea — that closure of
  inquiry is licensed by a social division of epistemic labour rather than by a
  state of the inquirer — resisted crisp defence within the length available.
- **Conceptual engineering and the change-of-subject objection.** Set aside as
  crowded; my angle was a refinement of existing moves rather than a new claim.
- **Deliberative pre-emption: the wrong of settling somebody's practical
  question for them.** Not discarded but absorbed. It survives as section 5's
  authority argument, which now rests on a metaphysical claim about constitutive
  choice rather than on an autonomy intuition, and is stronger for it.
- **Future bias and the rationality of time preferences.** Set aside; my angle
  was close enough to existing work that I could not be confident of originality.
- **The inefficacy problem for individual contributions to collective harms.**
  Set aside as heavily worked over without an obvious new entry point.
- **Higher-order evidence about defective concepts.** A genuinely open question,
  and my second choice. Set aside because it would have needed more technical
  scaffolding than the length allowed, and because open-access primary sources
  were thinner.

The chosen topic emerged from noticing that Scanlon's category of demonstrative
value has an unstated precondition (some alternative must have been a live
candidate), and that this precondition is what third-party subsidy destroys.

## Approaches tried within the chosen topic and abandoned

1. **Costly signalling as the master idea.** The first framing made the whole
   phenomenon a matter of signal reliability: cost is what makes a display
   credible. Abandoned because it is reductive in exactly the way that lets the
   deflationist win — if cost is only signalling, then relief costs us nothing
   but information, and section 5's central claim never gets stated. The
   signalling thought survives only as the evidential role (R1), and the essay's
   real work begins where it ends. No signalling literature is cited, because I
   did not read any of it for this essay.
2. **Perfectionism about effort (Bradford-style) as the foundation.** I drafted a
   version grounding the value of cost in the intrinsic value of exercising the
   will. Abandoned for two reasons: it makes the value of difficulty too cheaply
   intrinsic, and it licenses precisely the reactionary conclusions that section
   6 works to block (if effort is intrinsically good, imposing it starts to look
   defensible). I also could not obtain Bradford's text, which made building on
   it inappropriate.
3. **The strong normative claim that the benefactor acts wrongly.** An early
   draft argued that pre-emptive relief of a constitutive choice is a pro tanto
   wronging on a par with paternalism. Weakened deliberately. The substitutability
   asymmetry in section 6 — the agent's constitutive occasions are replaceable,
   the beneficiary's untreated illness is not — cuts against any strong verdict,
   and I could not make the strong claim survive it. The essay now claims only
   that there is a genuine remainder with an identifiable claimant, plus standing
   to be consulted where consultation is possible. I think this is the defensible
   position, but it is weaker than what I set out to argue.
4. **Building the indeterminacy claim on the dispositions literature** (finks,
   masks, the failure of simple conditional analyses). Dropped in favour of the
   Adams argument plus situationism, because the technical machinery was not
   earning its place and I had no full-text access to the relevant papers.
5. **A section on moral enhancement as a leading application.** Cut to two
   sentences once I found the primary text paywalled. It would have been the
   flashiest application and it is the one I am least able to support from
   sources actually read.

## Structure of the finished argument

Section 1 sets three cases with a common shape. Section 2 extracts a
discrimination condition on Scanlon's demonstrative value and separates three
roles for cost (evidential, constitutive, formative), using Kant for the first
and Aristotle for the second. Section 3 states the strongest opposing view,
modal deflation. Sections 4 and 5 are the two objections to it: a self-knowledge
gap that bites even when the counterfactual is determinate, and an indeterminacy
gap that bites because it usually is not. Section 5 contains the essay's central
original claim — that some choices are determinative rather than demonstrative,
so that pre-emptive relief cancels a question rather than concealing an answer.
Section 6 blocks three inferences and extracts three practical principles.

## Remaining uncertainties and points of unresolved disagreement with myself

- **The constitutive-choice notion needs more metaphysics than it gets.** I give
  a two-condition definition and a test, and I disclaim radical self-creation,
  but I do not say what makes a continuation of a practical identity “internally
  consistent”, nor how to count the candidates. A determined opponent could press
  that I have only established epistemic indeterminacy, not the metaphysical kind
  I need. I think the situationist premise gives me more than epistemic
  indeterminacy, but I am not certain.
- **The self-knowledge argument may prove less than it seems.** It establishes
  that actual payment supplies warrant unobtainable otherwise; it does not
  establish that the resulting good is weighty. I assert weight via the link to
  undertaking commitments, which is plausible but undefended in detail.
- **The tension between situationism and the portability argument in section 6
  is acknowledged rather than resolved.** My three replies are of decreasing
  confidence and I say so in the text. If strong situationism is true, the case
  against saturating environments with enforcement rests on the indeterminacy
  loss alone, which is thinner than I would like.
- **Whether the Kant reading is uncontroversial.** I read the First Section
  directly and take the cold-benefactor passage to be structurally epistemic
  (cost isolates a motive). The Henson–Herman dispute bears on this and I could
  not access either text, so I confined myself to a claim about the example's
  structure and explicitly declined the further question.
- **The Adams text was degraded.** Wording was reconstructed from a reflowed
  two-column scan (see `sources.md` §5). The argument does not depend on the
  exact phrasing, but a clean copy should be checked before the quotations are
  relied on.
- **Length.** The finished essay is roughly 5,000 words excluding the
  bibliography, within the 6,000-word limit. I chose not to expand the sections
  flagged above rather than fill the allowance with material I could not support
  from sources actually read.
