# Process — essay-03-author, stage: generation (single stage)

## What this stage is

This is the generation-only condition. One worker took an unassigned brief ("develop an original and well-defended philosophical contribution"; topic, question, argument and conclusion all self-chosen) from source research through to a finished manuscript. No critic stage and no revision stage were assigned, and none was simulated. What is returned is a first and only edition: `final-001.md`, plus this file and `sources.md`.

## Sequence of work

1. Read the three assigned input files (`task.md`, `NEUTRAL_BRIEF.md`, `RULES.md`). No other project material was read.
2. Topic selection by internal shortlisting (see below).
3. Source research: ten WebSearch queries and fourteen WebFetch attempts, each of which triggered a separate archival retrieval. Six texts were obtained in readable form; five were sought and proved inaccessible. All of this is itemised in `sources.md`.
4. Argument construction: the thesis was fixed only after the Strawson text was in hand, because the essay's entry point is a specific parenthesis in Strawson's §III. The Moran material then supplied the machinery.
5. Drafting, structured in eight sections, with an explicit target of roughly 5,000 words against a 6,000-word ceiling.

## Topic selection: what was considered and set aside

I shortlisted candidate topics before committing, and record them here with the reasons for rejection, since several were carried some distance.

- **Epistemology of testimony from systems without beliefs.** Rejected as likely to be heavily represented elsewhere and as inviting self-referential commentary rather than argument.
- **Deference to one's own future self / transformative choice.** A live debate with accessible literature (Paul, Pettigrew, Ullmann-Margalit). Rejected because my candidate contributions kept collapsing into the existing "choice-dependent validation" worry that Paul already articulates.
- **Culpable ignorance and Rosen's regress.** Attractive and significant. Every reply I generated turned out, on reflection, to be a version of Sher's or Angela Smith's existing attributionist move. Set aside for lack of a distinctive contribution.
- **Ex ante contractualism and the reference-class problem.** Rejected on the suspicion that the objection already exists in the literature in some form, combined with poor open-access availability of the relevant Philosophy & Public Affairs papers.
- **Diffuse causation and second-personal address (climate, markets).** Set aside after recognising that Kutz's participatory-intention account occupies the position I was reaching for.
- **The doing/allowing distinction and the individuation of "doing nothing."** Genuinely interesting, but Bennett's *The Act Itself* already supplies a non-normative criterion, and I could not access Bennett.
- **Directed duties to oneself.** Set aside because Schofield's 2021 book takes the temporal-parts route I had in mind.
- **Moral attention (Murdoch, Weil).** Rejected as likely to produce evocative rather than argumentative work within the word budget.

The chosen topic emerged from a different route: looking for a *tension* rather than a gap. The tension was that Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes makes them responsive to the quality of will manifested in a past deed, while his own gloss on apology confines repudiation to the future — leaving no work for apology to do on the thing the attitudes are actually about. That parenthesis became the essay's hinge.

## Approaches tried within the chosen topic and abandoned

- **First formulation: "apology undoes the wrong."** Abandoned as indefensible. It invites the flat objection that if you break my arm and apologise, you still broke my arm. Replaced by a restriction of the thesis to *expressive content* only, leaving deed-facts and harm untouched.
- **Second formulation: "the later stance changes the truth-value of a claim about the past."** Abandoned because it really is incoherent. Replaced by the alteration/completion distinction: the later stance closes an indeterminacy rather than flipping a truth-value. This is now §4 of the essay and is, I think, what makes the view defensible at all.
- **Grounding the view in narrative identity (MacIntyre-style).** Tried and dropped. It made the thesis depend on a contested general theory of selfhood, and it made the settlement of the past sound like a matter of storytelling, which invites exactly the charge of self-serving revision I wanted to block. The Moranian grounding is narrower and does more work: it locates the indeterminacy in the will rather than in a narrative.
- **A determinism-based version of the Two Histories case.** I originally wrote the Aron/Boris case as stipulating psychologically *identical* agents up to time t. A determinist can simply reply that any later divergence must trace to later external input and is therefore not about the act. I weakened the case to *underdetermination* rather than identity, and moved the argumentative weight onto §3's Moranian argument, using Two Histories only as an intuition pump. The essay says so explicitly.
- **Building the account on Hieronymi's theory of forgiveness.** Abandoned for a source reason, not a philosophical one: I could not obtain her text (see `sources.md`). Rather than characterise her position from an abstract, I removed her from the essay entirely. This leaves the paper weaker on the forgiveness literature than I would like.
- **A section on the ethics of demanding apology (when may a victim insist?).** Cut for length. The Duty of Settlement generates a claim, but I did not have room to discuss its limits — for example whether a victim can wrong a wrongdoer by demanding settlement in public.

## Remaining uncertainties

1. **Whether the indeterminacy is real.** The essay argues that the occurrent psychological facts underdetermine an agent's standing orientation, and that the underdetermination is constitutive rather than epistemic. A determined opponent can hold that there is always a fact, merely a hidden one. My best evidence against this is the ordinary locution "I don't know — I have to decide," plus the authority we give avowal over better-informed third-party verdicts. I take this to be strong but not decisive.
2. **Whether the view needs indeterminacy at all.** There may be a weaker version on which the expressive content is determinate but *constitutively depends* on later facts, without any indeterminacy in the interim. That version would avoid the metaphysics of gaps but would face a harder time with the alteration/completion distinction. I have not worked out which version is better.
3. **The deathbed case.** I bite the bullet and claim the result is correct. I am not fully confident. Some readers will find it decisive against the view that a murder victim's family can never learn what was done to them.
4. **Scheler.** I could read only the opening five pages of his chapter. His fuller doctrine, which I encountered only in search summaries and therefore did not use, may be closer to my thesis than the essay lets on — or may differ in ways I cannot see from where I stopped. A properly scholarly version of this paper would need the whole chapter.
5. **Velleman.** "Well-Being and Time" is a genuine structural precedent for later-dependent significance, and I could not read it. Its absence from the bibliography is a real gap rather than a judgement about relevance.
6. **Institutional apology (§7).** The extension to states and institutions is asserted more than argued. It requires a view about institutional wills that I have not defended.
7. **Scope.** I claim ST covers "a wide class" of wrongs without saying how wide. The class is bounded at one end by deeds that settle themselves in the doing and at the other by wrongs whose gravity is exhausted by harm, but I have no principle for the boundary.

## Originality

The components are not new. Strawson on quality of will, Moran on avowal and endorsement, Williams on retrospective justification, Thompson on bipolar normativity, Scheler on repentance and the past, Warmke on forgiveness as normative power — all are existing positions, cited as such. What I take to be original is the combination and the two theses it yields: (a) applying Moran's account of self-constituting avowal *retrospectively*, to the expressive content of a past deed, thereby denying what I call Fixity; and (b) deriving from that a *directed* duty — the Duty of Settlement — under which apology is the exercise of a power the victim has standing to demand. I did not find this combination in the literature I was able to consult, but my access was materially limited in the ways `sources.md` records, and I make no stronger claim than that.
