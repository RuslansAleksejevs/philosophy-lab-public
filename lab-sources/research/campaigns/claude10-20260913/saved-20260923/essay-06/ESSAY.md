# Baseline Capture

## Why Consent Stops Justifying When Its Beneficiary Has Shaped the Alternatives

---

## 1. Two pictures of consent

Consent does remarkable normative work. A surgeon's incision is battery without it and treatment with it. The standard account of when this transformation succeeds is a list of conditions on the consenter: valid consent is “sufficiently capacitated, informed, and voluntary consent” (Eyal 2025, §1), voluntariness being threatened by coercion, undue inducement, and the want of decent alternatives (§§5.1–5.3).

These conditions are *synchronic*. Each concerns the agent and her situation at the moment of consent. History enters only instrumentally, insofar as it bears on whether she is now capacitated, informed and unpressured.

The tradition is not blind to option-poverty. Eyal's §5.3 treats “‘no choice' situations”, and reports a proposal designed to keep them from swallowing consent whole: consent is insufficiently voluntary “only when the patient's options are unfairly curtailed by the offer itself” (Miller and Wertheimer 2010, 92, 97, as reported in Eyal 2025, §5.3). Note the index. The test looks at the proposal on the table and asks whether *it* curtailed the recipient's options. That is still a test on a moment.

I shall argue that a party can decisively curtail another's options while no offer he makes curtails anything — because every one of his offers widens them. The structure I call a **ratchet**: a sequence of transactions, each satisfying every synchronic condition, through which one party obtains terms it could not have obtained at the outset and could not obtain by any single offer. Ratchets are not exotic. They are the normal form of long employment relationships, sponsored migration, platform dependence, franchising, and some kinds of intimate control.

Four claims:

1. **Historicity.** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on properties of the consent but on the causal history of B's option set — specifically, on the part A's conduct plays in explaining it.
2. **Self-serving certification.** Consent certifies a comparison: that what was accepted beat the alternative. It has force because that comparison is normally *independent* of the party invoking it. Where his conduct explains why the alternative was so poor, it is no longer independent evidence.
3. **Inverse reliance.** The justificatory weight A may place on B's consent varies inversely with the degree to which A's conduct explains the poverty of B's alternatives.
4. **Commitment.** A party whose course of dealing predictably impoverishes the other's alternatives can restore the force of her later consent only by having bound itself in advance. Disclosure cannot discharge this.

The conclusion is a burden shift, not a verdict of wrongdoing. I do not claim that ratchets assemble wrongs from innocent parts. I claim something more precise: that they assemble *defeated justifications* from sound ones.

## 2. Vesting

Mara is a software engineer who joins Aurum, a large firm, in 2016. Four episodes follow, each roughly two years apart, each a discrete and plainly perceptible event.

**(1) Relocation.** Aurum offers a substantial relocation payment and a subsidised mortgage to move to the small city where its campus sits. She accepts, and is better off. She is also now a specialist in a labour market with one dominant buyer.

**(2) Specialisation.** A funded two-year programme in Aurum's proprietary toolchain, with a promotion. She accepts, and is better off: more interesting work, higher pay. Her human capital is now substantially firm-specific.

**(3) Deferral.** A large equity grant vesting over eight years, in place of the smaller cash raise she would otherwise have had. On any reasonable estimate it is worth more. She accepts. Leaving now costs her the unvested remainder.

**(4) The ask.** Aurum asks her to take over a team whose product she believes harmful and whose engineering she considers shoddy. Longer hours, no raise. Refusal will not get her fired; it will end her advancement. She accepts, and is right to: given where she stands, accepting beats refusing by a wide margin, and she knows it.

Stipulate that Aurum never lied, threatened, or prevented her from leaving, and that at (1)–(3) it conferred real benefits, competitively priced at the time. Stipulate that Mara was throughout capacitated, informed about the terms on offer, and under no pressure beyond the ordinary pressure of wanting things; that her preferences never changed and were transitive; that she never reasoned badly.

Do *not* stipulate that step (4) is competitively priced. It is not. Step (4) is where Aurum takes the surplus its earlier generosity created; that is the point of the case.

At step (4) Aurum obtains labour on terms Mara would have refused flatly in 2016, without any device consent theory is built to detect. Offered as a package in 2016, the whole sequence would have been declined, and Aurum knew it.

## 3. What the existing vocabulary catches, and what it misses

**Coercion.** On accounts descending from Nozick (1969), a proposal is a threat rather than an offer if, against an appropriate baseline, it worsens the recipient's situation (Anderson 2023, §2.2). Aurum's proposals at (1)–(3) improve Mara's situation on any baseline, and (4) she can refuse while keeping everything. Zimmerman's refinement sets the baseline at what the recipient would face “in the absence of special interference by the proposal maker” (Zimmerman 1981, as reported in Anderson 2023, §2.4) — his case being a destitute islander offered a pittance by an employer who prevents the building of boats.

The encyclopedia's gloss is worth quoting whole: “If the offering party here has created the conditions that make even very unattractive offers still the best available, then the coercion, if there is any, seems to be vested in the offerer's actions that prevent the recipient from improving her situation or finding better offers elsewhere” (§2.4). Aurum satisfies the antecedent: it did create the conditions. It does not satisfy the consequent: it performs no actions that *prevent*. Every contribution was a benefit she accepted. The entry marks the residue itself — Zimmerman “grants that even if the offering party does not cause the recipient to be in the position she's in, we may still want to criticize such offers” as exploitative, and it observes that “sometimes coercion is used to create or maintain one's bargaining advantages”. The open question is what to say when bargaining advantages are created without coercion.

**Domination.** The nearest live rival is republican. For Pettit, the dominated agent's “choices must always be made with an eye towards retaining the favor of those in a position to interfere” (Anderson 2023, §3.3, reporting Pettit 1996); and “Garnett holds that such subjection can be found in some cases of offers, where the offeror takes advantage of the offeree's lack of an acceptable alternative” (§3.3, reporting Garnett 2018). Mara does keep an eye on Aurum's favour, so the resemblance is real. But domination is a relation of *power to interfere*, and Aurum acquires none: it cannot worsen Mara's position and could not if it wished. What it acquires is that nobody else can improve it. Baseline capture is therefore possible between parties neither of whom could ever interfere — which is why it thrives in competitive markets with free entry, where domination characteristically does not. The theses also have different objects: non-domination concerns a condition bad for the subject; mine concerns the offeror's *standing to justify*. Garnett is my nearest neighbour, and what I add is the diachronic question of where the lack of alternatives came from.

**Manipulation.** Noggle's survey has a section on “Manipulating Persons versus Manipulating Situations” (2026, §4.1) reporting Riker's *heresthetic*, “structuring the world so you can win” (Riker 1986: ix); Warwick and Kelman on “environmental” versus “psychic” manipulation; and Barnhill's category of manipulation that “changes the options available to the person or changes the situation she's in, *and thereby changes her attitudes*” (2014: 53). §4.2 canvasses manipulation without conscious intent, including by algorithms nobody designed to manipulate; §4.3 notes tactics that “work to increase the target's vulnerability to further manipulation”, which is explicitly sequential. So the category of situational, unintended, self-compounding influence exists, and a ratchet is not a gap in it.

Why, then, is Aurum not simply manipulating? Because of the clause I italicised: situational manipulation changes options *and thereby attitudes*, and Mara's attitudes are exactly what they were. Mills, drawing the same distinction, expressly reserves “manipulation” for interference with the “internal or subjective features” of a choice situation rather than the “external or objective” ones (1995: 97). And Raz states the remaining gap with unusual clarity: “manipulation, unlike coercion, does not interfere with a person's options. Instead it perverts the way that person reaches decisions, forms preferences, or adopts goals” (1988: 377). That dichotomy is exhaustive only if all interference with options is coercion. Aurum alters the value of Mara's options without coercing her, and leaves her deliberation immaculate. It falls between Raz's two chairs.

**Exploitation.** The historical accounts are the closest structural analogue. Hillel Steiner holds that “exploitation occurs when A gains more from an interaction, and B gains less, than they would have were it not for the existence of a prior injustice”, illustrated by an employer able to pay two dollars an hour “only because A (or someone else) has previously unjustly deprived B of alternative sources of labor” (Zwolinski, Ferguson and Wertheimer 2022, §2.2.2.2, reporting Steiner 1984). Roemer's and Sample's accounts are in the family; Goodin, Vrousalis and Zwolinski dissent, holding the source of vulnerability irrelevant or antecedent injustice neither necessary nor sufficient.

The ratchet is best located as a counterexample to the historical accounts. Steiner requires a prior injustice. Aurum commits none: its contributions were benefits, competitively priced, freely accepted, better than nothing on every estimate available at the time. Yet Mara's alternatives at step (4) are as poor as if she had been wronged, and their poverty is fully explained by Aurum's conduct. The case exhibits baseline degradation with no antecedent injustice anywhere in the chain.

The **nonworseness claim** must be stated in full: “Interaction between A and B cannot be worse than non-interaction when A has a right not to interact with B at all, and when the interaction is mutually advantageous, consensual, and free from negative externalities” (§3, crediting Wertheimer 1996, 2011; Zwolinski 2009; Powell and Zwolinski 2012). Two things follow. Most exploitation theorists are already sceptical of it (§3), so a result against it yields less than one might hope. But the first condition is one I want: by step (4) it is arguable that Aurum no longer has a clean right not to interact with Mara at all, precisely because of what steps (1)–(3) did. That is a second, independent route to my conclusion.

One concession. On Wertheimer's fair-market-value test — a fair price is what goods “would fetch in a hypothetical competitive market that excludes asymmetries of information and undue pressure”, one at which neither party “takes special unfair advantage of… special vulnerabilities in the other party's situation” (§2.2.2.1, quoting Wertheimer 1996: 232) — step (4) looks like straightforward exploitation. I agree. But this is not a rival; it is a demand for what I am supplying. The test is silent about *which* hypothetical competitive market: the one Mara faced in 2016, or the one she faces in 2024 with skills only Aurum can use. These give opposite verdicts and nothing in the account chooses. Baseline capture supplies the missing index, and a reason for it that is not distributive at all.

**Hold-up.** The mechanism is not new, and pretending otherwise would be a mistake. It is the hold-up problem of relational contracting. Klein, Crawford and Alchian locate it in “appropriable specialized quasi rents”: the quasi-rent value of an asset is “the excess of its value over its salvage value, that is, its value in its next best use”, and “after a specific investment is made and such quasi rents are created, the possibility of opportunistic behavior is very real” (1978: 298). Two of their observations help me. First, “an appropriable quasi rent can occur with no market closure or restrictions placed on rival assets” (299): lock-in needs no fence, no monopoly, no interference. Second, they treat the matter as one of efficiency — opportunism “is likely to result in a loss of efficiency and not just a wealth-distribution effect” (301) — with vertical integration or contracts as the remedies.

The differences mark my contribution. Their opportunism “by definition refers to unanticipated non-fulfillment of the contract” (300); Aurum fulfils everything it agrees. Their question is which arrangements minimise the losses; mine is what the party who benefits may say for itself. An efficiency loss is a cost. A defeated justification is something owed to a person.

## 4. The structure

Let B deal with A at stages *i* = 1, …, *n*. Write **r**ᵢ for B's *reservation value*, the value to her of her best alternative to accepting at that stage; **u**ᵢ for the value to her of accepting; **σ**ᵢ for the total gains from trade available, so B's share is uᵢ − rᵢ and A's is σᵢ − (uᵢ − rᵢ). A proposal is *acceptable* iff uᵢ ≥ rᵢ.

The standard picture treats reservation values as **exogenous**: B brings her alternatives to the table, the proposal is assessed against them, and consent given uᵢ ≥ rᵢ transfers permission. A ratchet is what happens when they are **endogenous** — when accepting at stage *i* lowers rᵢ₊₁. Relocation thins the set of employers Mara can reach; specialisation raises her Aurum-specific productivity relative to her general productivity; deferral puts an explicit price on exit. Each is a benefit conferred and accepted, and each lowers the value of *not dealing with Aurum next time*.

> **Failure of composition.** The property *consensual, mutually beneficial, and no worse for B than not transacting* is closed under sequential composition only if reservation values are exogenous. Where they are endogenous, uᵢ ≥ rᵢ can hold at every stage while uₙ < r₁.

A party who can influence the counterparty's alternatives can therefore satisfy the nonworseness condition at every step *while walking the counterparty downhill*. There is no step at which it is violated, and no step at which its satisfaction is evidence that the counterparty is being well treated. A corollary: since A's share is σᵢ − (uᵢ − rᵢ), a falling rᵢ lets A hold uᵢ down while every proposal stays acceptable — capture without a single unacceptable offer. I claim no monotonicity result; that would need assumptions about σᵢ I have not made.

Three neighbours should be set aside. Quinn's self-torturer faces a device whose “increments in current are so small that the self-torturer cannot tell the difference between adjacent settings”, is paid $10,000 per advance, and cannot go back (Andreou 2025, §1.3, reporting Quinn 1993): everything load-bearing there comes from imperceptibility and intransitivity, and no second party profits. The money pump (Davidson, McKinsey and Suppes 1955, as reported in §1.3) likewise runs on intransitivity; its victim is rationally defective, and Mara is not. A ratchet pumps a perfectly coherent agent. Parfit's harmless torturers (1984) distribute harm across many agents whose contributions are imperceptible; a ratchet has two parties and perceptible differences throughout.

## 5. Independence, not authorship

What exactly fails? Begin with what consent tells us. When B consents she certifies a comparison: that, as she sees it, accepting beats her best alternative. She does not certify that the terms are fair, that the transaction is good for her, or that A has treated her decently. Ordinarily this thin certification is powerful, because the alternative is *hers*, constituted by the world, her other relationships, her skills and savings and other people's offers. A proposal that beats that is likely worth having.

Now ask what must hold for the inference to work. Not that B's alternatives are good; they may be dreadful through nobody's doing and her consent still justify. What is required is that the comparison be *independent of the party invoking it* — that its coming out in A's favour not be explained by A's own conduct. This is an instance of a familiar epistemic norm: evidence loses force for a party in proportion as that party produced it.

> **Independence Principle.** Consent certifies that the consented-to option beat the consenter's best alternative. That certification supports A's justification only to the extent that the poverty of the alternative is not explained by A's conduct.

It is tempting to say instead that A *authored* the alternative, and that any offeror who can lower an alternative can make the certification come out true at will. That is false here. Aurum could not secure Mara's 2024 consent at will: it needed four acceptances, each of which she was right to make and any of which she could have declined. Distinguish three ways an outside option can fall: (a) A removes options B would otherwise have had; (b) B's own choices narrow her prospects, as when a generalist becomes a harpsichord specialist; (c) A offers options whose acceptance predictably narrows her future prospects, and B accepts. Nobody thinks (b) defeats B's later consent; everybody thinks (a) does. Aurum is (c), and (c) is not (a).

The evidential point, however, does not run on causal unilaterality. What defeats the inference from “she agreed” to “the terms are defensible” is that the comparison is *self-serving*, and it is self-serving when the party citing it is the party whose conduct explains why one side of it is so low. Mediation through B's own rational acceptances does not break that explanation; it is part of it, since A selected proposals whose acceptance was foreseeable precisely because they were good for her. Nor does mediation matter in case (b), for a different reason: there nobody invokes the comparison in his own favour, so there is no interest to discount for.

The electoral analogy now says what I want. That a candidate won a free and fair vote is powerful evidence of the electorate's will — unless the candidate shaped the ballot. No vote was invalid; every voter was competent, informed about who was standing, unpressured. The election is useless as a warrant *for him*, and the reason is conflict of interest, not causation. It does not matter whether he struck rivals off unilaterally or made each an attractive offer to stand down.

Because explanation comes in degrees, so does independence, and so does the principle's bite. That yields **Inverse Reliance** as a graded consequence rather than a stipulation.

Two clarifications. *Mara's consent is valid*: she really did permit what Aurum did, and Aurum commits no battery, trespass or breach. *Mara's consent does not justify*: validity and justificatory force come apart.

Justify what, exactly? The object is the **terms** Aurum obtains at step (4) — more exactly, Aurum's share of the surplus, the part the fall in Mara's reservation value made available. Making a proposal is not generally an act needing justification against a counterfactual baseline; taking a larger share because the other party's exit has become expensive is. “She agreed” is unavailable for that, not because the agreement was defective but because the comparison it certifies is one Aurum's own conduct explains.

What must Aurum show instead? Not that Mara is paid what her talents would command had she never dealt with Aurum: that test either prices her 2016 generalist skills, ignoring productivity Aurum financed, or prices present skills on a market that does not exist. The benchmark is the **division of the relationship-specific surplus**. Aurum must show that its share at step (4) is one it could have obtained while Mara's reservation value was still independent of the relationship — that the terms track the bargain available ex ante rather than the leverage available ex post. That is a question about a division, not about a phantom wage, and it is the shape severance rules, notice periods, portability requirements and non-compete bans already have.

## 6. Mill, and reliance running both ways

In Chapter V of *On Liberty*, Mill explains why a contract of self-enslavement should be void. His ground is evidential: “[h]is voluntary choice is evidence that what he so chooses is desirable, or at the least endurable, to him”. And the evidence is renewed by occupancy: the slave “is thenceforth in a position which has no longer the presumption in its favour, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it” (1859, pp. 194–95).

Both premises are more powerful than the use Mill makes of them. He is looking for a *threshold* — a quantity of freedom so large that alienating it defeats the point of protecting it — which is why he finds only the limit case. His premises deliver a *gradient*. If choice earns deference as evidence of the chooser's good, and if that evidence is sustained by the ability to leave, then the warrant degrades continuously as the ability to leave degrades. This reading is not novel, and I do not claim it as such: Olsaretti's “[a] choice is voluntary if and only if it is not made because there is no acceptable alternative to it” (2004: 139, quoted in Anderson 2023, §3.3) and Feinberg's treatment of variable voluntariness (1986: 233–244, cited in Eyal 2025, §5.3) occupy the same ground.

What is new is the direction of exploitation. Mill continues that these reasons “are evidently of far wider application; yet a limit is everywhere set to them by the necessities of life” (p. 195) — a single sentence, not a retreat; he means only that the wider application cannot void every constraining engagement. Two pages later he writes:

> “When a person, either by express promise or by conduct, has encouraged another to rely upon his continuing to act in a certain way—to build expectations and calculations, and stake any part of his plan of life upon that supposition, a new series of moral obligations arises on his part towards that person, which may possibly be overruled, but cannot be ignored.” (pp. 196–97)

That is a course of conduct, not an act, generating obligations because of the reliance it induces. Mill saw the structure. What he did not do is run it in the direction my case needs. His principle constrains the party who would *withdraw* from a relationship on which another has staked her plan of life; the ratchet asks what is owed by the party who *benefits* from the staking. They are complements, and the second follows from the same thought. If inducing reliance generates obligations because it shapes what the other can now do, then inducing reliance and then pricing against it generates obligations too. Mill's reliance principle, run forwards instead of backwards, is the Commitment Condition.

## 7. The positive view

> **Historicity of Consent.** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on the synchronic properties of the consent but on the causal history of B's option set, and specifically on the part A's conduct plays in explaining it.

This needs a discriminating criterion, since *every* relationship alters the parties' alternatives. Friendships, marriages, apprenticeships and citizenship all reduce the attractiveness of exit; that is partly what they are for. A conduct-based criterion — A's dealings lower B's reservation value *and* A uses the lowering to get better terms — fails twice over. It catches every internal labour market, and it catches the firm that cuts wages because it is failing rather than because it can; and “uses” smuggles in an intention the account elsewhere disowns.

The repair is to make the criterion **positional** rather than behavioural, on the model of fiduciary duty, where one who acquires discretionary power over another's interests is barred from self-dealing regardless of intent and regardless of the beneficiary's agreement.

> **Baseline-capture position.** A occupies it with respect to B when A's course of dealing with B predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A.
>
> **Duty of forbearance.** One who occupies that position may not invoke B's consent as a complete justification for terms that B's prior reservation value would not have permitted. Such terms require an independent showing.

Three consequences, each of which I accept. First, this is intention-free, so it reaches the cases I care about most: a platform whose network effects and data lock-in steadily reduce users' exit options, and a migration regime tying visa status to one sponsor. Nobody designs these. There can be **blameless baseline capture** that nonetheless defeats the consent defence.

Second, yes, every internal labour market occupies the position. I bite this bullet, because the conclusion is not that such arrangements are impermissible but that they are a standing site of justificatory burden — which is why we regulate them, and regulate them in the ways §9 describes. The failing firm occupies the position too, and the account gets it right: it also may not say “she agreed”; it must say “we were insolvent”, which is a justification benchmarked to something other than the consent. The spouse whose partner gave up a career, and who then insists on staying in the city he prefers, cannot rest on “you agreed” either; he must say the arrangement is fair given all each has done. That is what we actually think. A theory that predicts our practice here is doing better than one exempting benign relationships by fiat.

Third, dependence does not license reliance on the dependent party's agreement; it withdraws that licence.

> **Commitment Condition.** Where A's course of dealing predictably impoverishes B's alternatives, A may restore the justificatory force of B's later consent only by having bound itself in advance, in ways B can rely on, against using that impoverishment to obtain better terms.

This shows why the natural remedy — *disclose more* — cannot work. Suppose Aurum in 2016 had said: “this will make you cheaper to us in eight years.” It is not that Mara would have nothing to price; agents price others' future discretion constantly, in at-will discounts and risk premia. The point is that **pricing a risk is not authorising what eventuates**. A discount is money; consent is a permission; and at the first stage there is no determinate proposal to permit, only a range from which Aurum will later choose. Insurance against an outcome does not license it. So the duty generated by baseline capture is not a duty to inform but a duty to *bind* — to give up some of the latitude the benefit creates.

That yields a stronger thesis, which I state as the essay's conclusion:

> **Consent is justificatorily effective only over option sets that the party invoking it cannot subsequently reshape, or has bound itself not to reshape.**

The condition is unusual in a further way. Most conditions on valid consent govern the consenter's epistemic or volitional state; this one governs the *offeror's* normative situation. Certain parties may avail themselves of the consent defence only if they have first disabled themselves. Its single-agent cousin is the literature on plans and resoluteness — Bratman, Gauthier, McClennen, Odysseus at the mast (Andreou 2025, §2.4). What is new is that the commitment runs to another party's benefit and functions as a condition on the committer's standing to invoke consent at all.

## 8. Objections

**(a) “Mara was imprudent.”** Perhaps she should have foreseen the trajectory; grant it. The claim at issue is that *Aurum's* justification fails, and a defective performance by B does not manufacture a warrant for A. The concession is small anyway: declining step (1) was worse on every estimate available to her, and Aurum had committed to nothing.

**(b) “Which step is impermissible?”** The question presupposes a verdict of impermissibility; my conclusion is that a particular defence is unavailable for the step-(4) terms. Still, the underlying worry is real. One might hold that permissibility agglomerates, so that permissible parts make a permissible whole. I reject that: the property that defeats the consent defence — that the comparison Aurum invokes is one its own conduct explains — is relational and historical, and no single act instantiates it. Or one might locate the failure at step (4) alone; but then ask *why*, since every synchronic fact about step (4) is benign. The only available answer cites the history, which concedes Historicity. Either way the synchronic picture is false.

The right analogies here are evidential, not the familiar ones about rigged games, which smuggle in a hidden wrongful act the case excludes. Every measurement may be correctly made with a properly functioning instrument, and the series still be worthless as evidence for the person who calibrated the instrument. Every statement a witness makes may be true, and his testimony still worthless because he chose which events to witness. No part is faulty; nothing is concealed; the whole fails to support the conclusion of the party who assembled it.

**(c) “This is structural injustice, not a defeated justification.”** One might keep agglomeration, deny that a course of dealing has deontic status over and above its parts, and say only that the *outcome* is unjust. This is the most serious objection, and the concession of blameless capture invites it. My reply distinguishes blame from answerability. Losing the consent defence is not being blamed; it is owing a justification, and one can owe a justification without having done wrong. The structural reading leaves nobody answerable, whereas the case is built so that there is a determinate party who benefits, who can act, and to whom “why are these your terms?” can be addressed. “Ask the institution” would be a strange reply.

**(d) “You are covertly appealing to intentions.”** No: the criterion is positional, referring to what a course of dealing predictably does, not to what anyone meant. Intention may bear on blame; it does not bear on who must justify.

**(e) “This is paternalism.”** The opposite. Paternalism restricts what the weaker party may do; this restricts what the stronger party may *say in its own defence*. Telling Mara she may not sign and telling Aurum that her signature will not by itself exonerate it are different acts.

**(f) “She was compensated for each erosion.”** Suppose the bonus, training and equity included a premium for lock-in, so that Mara got the full expected value of the bargaining power she surrendered. The distributive complaint may vanish. Does the justificatory one? Not, as one might think, because payment cannot undo staging — that begs the question, since a fully priced 2016 bargain is itself struck against an independent baseline. So I concede more. Had Aurum in 2016 *bound itself* to the whole trajectory, terms included, Mara's consent then would have been given against a baseline Aurum's conduct did not explain, and it would justify. What Aurum did instead was buy an option and keep the discretion. Paying for a risk while retaining the power to resolve it in your own favour is not selling that power. Pressed properly, the compensation objection converges on the Commitment Condition rather than refuting it.

**(g) “Commitment has a perverse implication: a firm that binds and then extracts is vindicated, while one that binds nothing and behaves decently is not.”** Real, but not perverse, because the account governs the availability of a defence, not a ranking of characters. The firm that bound itself and extracts within the bound has a justification other than “she agreed”: the ex ante bargain, struck when the baseline was independent. The firm that bound nothing but behaves decently is not condemned; it simply cannot rest on consent, and must show its terms are reasonable — which, being reasonable, it easily can. Nobody is convicted here. Some parties are merely required to argue.

**(h) “This is Cohen's collective unfreedom.”** An instructive cousin. Ten people are locked in a room with one heavy key; whoever takes it can leave, but “whatever happens, at least nine people will remain in the room” (Cohen 1983, p. 9). Each is individually free to go, yet together they suffer “collective unfreedom”, each free “only on condition that the others do not exercise their similarly conditional freedom” (p. 11). That is a failure of aggregation across persons at a time; mine is across times for one person. Cohen also insists that freedom-attributions carry two temporal indices — one is forced *at t* to act *at t+n* (p. 8) — and uses them to argue that most proletarians are not forced, because escape routes stay open long enough. The ratchet is the complement: the exits stay open at every moment, and the agent still ends where nobody would have sent her, because the party she deals with has been buying up the value of her going. Cohen's jailer installs photoelectric devices. Aurum installs nothing. It only gives.

## 9. What follows

**For normative powers.** Powers are usually individuated by their conditions of successful exercise, and consent's have been theorised synchronically. The ratchet shows that a power can be successfully exercised and still fail to transmit justification, because its point — the evidential relation between a person's choices and her good — is defeated by the provenance of the conditions under which it was exercised. A normative power's justificatory yield can be laundered, and the test is independence: whether its exercise is independent of the party who stands to benefit. Promises, waivers, authorisations and delegations are candidates for analogous treatment. This conjecture is not virgin territory: bioethics already recognises “the good of independent decisionmaking” as something “no choice” situations can undermine even where they are not coercive (Millum and Garnett 2019, as reported in Eyal 2025, §5.3).

**For practice.** Where a party predictably erodes another's alternatives we should expect, and can defend, requirements that reconstruct an independent baseline: portable rather than firm-specific credentials, vesting on departure, notice and severance scaled to specific investment, bans on non-competes, data portability, visa status detached from a single employer, cooling-off rules in long-term consumer contracts. These are usually defended as welfare measures or as corrections for unequal bargaining power. The present argument gives them a different warrant: they are the conditions under which the stronger party's appeal to the weaker party's consent remains honest. They are not restrictions on freedom of contract but preconditions of its justificatory work. That they are also close relatives of the contractual solutions the hold-up literature recommends on efficiency grounds is no coincidence, and is some reason to think the diagnosis tracks something real.

**For moral theory.** We have good vocabulary for wrongs located in an act, and reasonable vocabulary for wrongs located in a character or a policy. We have little for justificatory failures located in a *sequence* whose every member is sound. Parfit taught us that harms can be assembled from imperceptible parts. The ratchet teaches something adjacent: that a party can lose the right to rely on another's agreement through a course of dealing composed entirely of perceptible, consensual, mutually beneficial acts — with no imperceptibility, no irrationality, no deception, and no antecedent injustice anywhere in the chain.

Three uncertainties remain. Measurement: the burden-shifted justification appeals to a counterfactual division of surplus, and such counterfactuals are contestable; I take this to be an implementation problem, since courts estimate comparable counterfactuals in wrongful-dismissal and reliance damages, but I have not shown it. Scope: I have given a positional sufficient condition for one defect, not a general theory of which histories matter. Priority: I could not read Arto Laitinen's “Normative Powers, Agency, and Time”, and although its published description suggests a different question, I cannot certify non-overlap.

The immediate lesson is blunt enough. If you want to know whether someone's agreement licenses what you are doing to them, do not only ask whether they agreed freely. Ask whether the alternatives they were comparing it against are ones your own conduct explains.
