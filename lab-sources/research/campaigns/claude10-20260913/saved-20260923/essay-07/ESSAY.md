# What Asking Does: Requests, Answerability, and the Deliberative Agenda

*Final manuscript (final-005), condensed from final-004/003/002/001; revised from `draft-001.md` in the light of `critique.md`. Earlier versions are preserved unchanged.*

## 1. The puzzle

A colleague asks: "Could you look at my draft this weekend?"

The request is cheap to refuse. "Sorry, I'm swamped" will do. You need not show that your weekend outweighs your colleague's need.

It is also expensive to ignore. Look through your colleague and walk on, and you have wronged them — even if, had you considered the request, you would have been entitled to refuse. The failure is not that you underweighted something. It is that you did not take the matter up.

So: whatever a request contributes is so slight that almost anything defeats it, yet so insistent that it cannot be passed over in silence. Considerations that almost anything defeats are normally the very ones we may ignore. Requests are not. My thesis:

**(T1)** Requesting adds no weight to the first-order balance bearing on the requested act. Need, friendship and the good of the profession would favour your reading the draft exactly as strongly had nobody asked.

**(T2)** Requesting is nonetheless a normative power on a second dimension: it puts an option on the addressee's *deliberative agenda* and makes her *answerable* for what becomes of it.

**(T3)** What it creates is neither a first-order reason nor quite a Razian second-order reason — Raz defined those as reasons to act, or refrain from acting, *for* a reason — but a requirement bearing on deliberative *uptake*.

## 2. Why weights are not enough

The literature asks whether asking *creates* a reason (Enoch, Lewis, Schaber) or merely *reveals* one (Weltman's epistemic account, on which "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking").[^1] Both sides ask what a request contributes to the first-order balance.

Suppose it contributes a reason of weight *w*. If *w* is substantial, cheap refusal is lost: I could not rightly decline merely to see a film. If *w* is an epsilon, something worse follows. Call a consideration *idle* in a context when the balance there is the same with or without it, and grant that one is not criticisable for failing to attend to what one reasonably takes to be idle — not a concession to frailty but a condition of deliberation, since every choice sits in a field of vanishing considerations. An epsilon is idle wherever anything weightier is present. So on a hard deadline I could leave the message unopened, knowing in advance that it cannot change what I ought to do. That is exactly what one may not do.

The better reply, and the one the literature makes, is not scalar. Lewis and Schaber explain discretion deontically: "A reason is non-obligatory if it would not be blameworthy for one not to act on it, and obligatory if it would be blameworthy not to act on it."[^2] A reason can be weighty and non-obligatory, so the inference from cheap refusal to tiny weight never starts. My earlier draft missed this.

But see what the retreat concedes. Requests are said to create reasons such that it is *never blameworthy not to act on them*, while it plainly *is* blameworthy not to attend to them. On any weight-based account the point of attending to a reason is to get the action right; inattention is criticisable because it risks acting wrongly. If non-compliance is never blameworthy, inattention cannot be criticisable for risking a wrong act — there is no wrong act to risk. The blame must have another ground, and finding it is this essay's project. Nor is "non-obligatory reason" a resting place: it is not a weight but a status defined by where blame falls, so whoever invokes it has already left the scale.

Independent evidence comes from what we protect: do-not-disturb settings, unsubscribe links, gatekeepers, office hours, and conventions of indirection that let a request be withdrawn before it lands. We say "don't ask me that" precisely to stop an agenda item being created. Nobody builds institutions against small increments to their reasons. We build them against having our attention conscripted and our silence made culpable.

## 3. The Agenda Thesis

> In requesting that S φ, A brings it about that:
> **(i) Uptake** — S must take up the question whether to φ, as "what A has asked of me";
> **(ii) Answerability** — if S does not φ, she must fail to φ *for* a reason she can offer A as the reason on which she acted;
> **(iii) Account** — S must actually render that account where A would otherwise be left without one.

The burden is one of production, not of persuasion. Before the request the question was not S's to answer; after it she must put something forward, but only a reason, not a *weightier* reason. Hence the profile: easy to discharge, impossible to ignore. This also disarms the worry that bigotry would discharge it. A burden of production is met by producing something of the right *type*; it certifies nothing about what is produced. Discharging it makes S non-culpable *for ignoring A*; whether she is culpable *for refusing* is settled by the first-order balance the request never touched.

Clause (iii) is derived, not added. My earlier draft made answerability merely counterfactual, so that S could silently consider, silently decline, and walk off — satisfying the condition while committing the very wrong at issue. But answerability is a *relation*: a standing liability to be called to account, discharged as circumstances allow. Where A cannot know whether S took the question up except by being told, it can be discharged only by telling. The account thus predicts the exceptions too: nothing need be said where compliance is manifest, where A said "no need to reply," or where A is unreachable. Relatedly, the operative condition on (ii) is *sincerity* — the reason offered must be the reason acted on. That rules out pretexts but not choosing by lot between equally good options; what it rules out is drift, never engaging and then discovering afterwards that some good reason was available.

Standing is a permission, not a success condition: the ill-standing request lands, which is why it is an imposition, and why "you have no business asking" is apt rather than idle. Uptake is defeasible — by incapacity and by more urgent claims on attention, not by the pointlessness of uptake or by small costs — and when defeated, answerability is *deferred* rather than extinguished: out of surgery I must still say, "Sorry, I was in theatre." Outweighed reasons leave no such residue. Where many requests compete, weight orders the queue but not its membership: the ninth request waits rather than vanishing. And agenda requirements never enter the balance: exclusionary reasons remove inputs, whereas a request leaves inputs alone and requires only that a question be run through deliberation and its output owned. Composition, not addition. It follows that repetition adds nothing — one cannot become more answerable by being asked twice — though asking again after circumstances change renews the claim, since it is no longer the same question.

T1 survives the hardest cases I could build. "Please don't tell anyone" seems to add weight, since telling afterwards is betrayal rather than mere indiscretion; but say "I'm not promising anything — I'm a terrible gossip," and the betrayal disappears. The gravity comes from my silence functioning as acceptance, not from her asking. So accepted requests create commitments, and commitments are weighty: refusal is cheap precisely because acceptance is what obligates. And this explains why tacit acceptance is possible at all — silence can count as acceptance only where silence is not neutral, and it is the request that makes saying nothing a move.

## 4. Address without a claim

Comparing being asked with overhearing does not isolate address, since overhearing varies common knowledge and higher-order information is still information. A cleaner test uses Raz's observation that "[i]t is possible ... to tell a person that, while I would like him to do something for me, I am not asking and am not going to ask him to do it."[^3] Compare (a) "Could you read my draft this weekend?" with (b) "I'd love you to read my draft — your comments are always so helpful — but I'm not asking, and I'm not going to." There is full information, full common knowledge, a genuine addressee. In both, S must say *something*, since conversational uptake is owed to any address — which is why politeness cannot be the difference. The difference is that in (a) S must *close* the question, and leaving it open indefinitely is itself a failure; in (b) she need not open it, and may silently resolve never to read the draft without wronging A at all.

Weltman's reply is ready-made. He grants that in requesting "I am to some extent responsible" for the addressee's compliance in a way I am not when she acts on a wish, calls this "a big difference," then brackets it as "not in the sense of the normativity we are interested in," comparing it to accidentally versus deliberately exposing one's cards at bridge, which leaves opponents "identical reasons" either way.[^4] But the bracket defines the topic rather than reporting a finding; and the analogy misdescribes the structure, since at bridge the culpability attaches to the revealer and nothing is required of his opponents, whereas the liability a request creates falls on the *addressee*. One may bracket the speaker's responsibility and still owe an account of a requirement falling on the hearer.

## 5. Where this belongs

My earlier draft claimed Raz's genus has an empty, unnamed positive species. Raz's definition, quoted by Piller, is: "A second-order reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason."[^5] The positive species is neither nameless nor empty — it is standardly called a *positive second-order reason*, attacked by Whiting and recently defended and classified by Sekine.[^6]

What survives is better. Clause (ii) *is* a positive second-order reason, since it constrains the reasons for which S may act. Clause (i) is *not*: considering is not acting for a reason, and a requirement to put a question on the agenda bears on deliberation, on what gets weighed. Raz's disjunction does not reach it. So I drop the label "inclusionary reason" I proposed, along with the symmetry it promised, and claim instead a category of *agenda reasons* that the standard taxonomy lacks. The gap is visible inside Raz's own motivating case, where, on Piller's exposition, the tired investor's refusal "is based on a reason not to consider the merits of the offer" — a matter of uptake, then reclassified as acting-for-a-reason. And requests are where the received picture finds nothing to say: Michael Moore notes that they are illuminated by the *absence* of exclusionary reasons, since "a mere request does not preclude one from weighing the pros and cons of doing the action requested."[^7] Exactly half the story. A request excludes nothing — and also does something second-order, on an axis the framework lacks.

Two debts are unpaid. Whiting argues second-order reasons are incoherent; my account is not hostage to this, since clause (i) is not a reason to act for a reason. And Stephen Darwall's work on second-personal address and accountability is the nearest relative of my positive thesis; I could not obtain it. Nor could I obtain Micha Gläser, reported by Weltman and Schaber to deny that requests create additional reasons while holding that they obligate the requestee. If those reports are right, we agree on the negative claim and differ on the positive one — testably, since an obligation to φ predicts blameworthy non-compliance, which the category of non-obligatory reasons denies. What is obligatory, on my account, is not φ-ing but taking the question up and standing behind the answer.

## 6. Conclusion

Agenda reasons are not peculiar to requests. Questions create obligations to answer that are cheap to discharge and, where the asker has standing, costly to ignore. Rights to be heard are powers to put a claim on an institution's agenda rather than powers to win, which is why a hearing one was bound to lose is nonetheless owed. Much protest aims not to shift a balance of reasons but to force a question onto an agenda; the complaint of the unheard is not "you weighed us too lightly" but "you did not even consider us."

Asking adds nothing to the scales. It puts a question on your agenda and makes you answerable for what becomes of it — a redirection rather than a deflation, since the power to conscript another's attention and make their silence culpable is among the most consequential we have over one another. Thomas Reid, who counted asking among the mind's *social* operations and complained that in the usual divisions "the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain,"[^8] was describing a gap in the philosophy of mind. The same gap runs through the theory of practical reason.
