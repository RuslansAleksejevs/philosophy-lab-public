# Making Goodness Cheap: Cost, Character, and the Ethics of Relief

## 1. Three cases

Maria's brother needs two years of rehabilitation that neither of them can afford. After a month of wretched arithmetic she decides to sell the flat she inherited from their mother — the only thing she owns, and the thing she had meant never to sell. The decision costs her a great deal, and she makes it. Before she can sign, a wealthy acquaintance who has guessed the situation quietly settles the clinic's bill. Her brother receives exactly the same care. Maria keeps the flat.

By the ordinary accounting this is a clean gain: one person is very much better off, nobody is worse off, and a house has not changed hands. Yet many people feel that something has gone out of the world along with the bill. The feeling is easy to dismiss as sentimentality about suffering, and it deserves to be dismissed if that is all it is. I shall argue that it is not.

Two further cases, less personal:

*The Honest Office.* A firm installs monitoring so complete that theft becomes impossible: every transaction logged, every till reconciled in real time, every anomaly flagged before it can be repeated. Losses fall to zero. Nobody steals. Has anybody become honest?

*The Frictionless Pledge.* A payroll giving scheme is redesigned so that a modest monthly donation is the default, and the deduction is small enough that nobody notices it. Total giving rises sharply. Do the givers give?

The three cases share a shape. A cost that stood between a person and a good option has been removed by somebody else; the good outcome has been secured; and something we cared about seems to have gone missing. My question is what exactly goes missing, whether its loss matters, and what follows for the people in the business of lowering costs: benefactors, employers, designers, legislators.

I defend three claims. First, cost enters our assessment of agents in three quite different roles — *evidential*, *constitutive* and *formative* — and most argument in this area, on both sides, equivocates between them. Second, the most attractive way of defusing the worry fails. That way is what I shall call *modal deflation*: the thought that what we care about is not that the agent paid but that she *would have*, so that relief destroys no value, only information. Modal deflation fails twice over. It cannot supply the *self*-knowledge that several of the relevant goods require; and for a class of choices I shall call *constitutive*, the counterfactual it leans on has no determinate truth value, so that relief does not conceal a fact about the agent but prevents one from coming into existence. Third, it follows that making goodness cheap carries a real and non-empirical cost — though not the cost that friends of hardship suppose. The conclusion is not that burdens should be left in place. It is that relief has a remainder, that the remainder belongs to somebody in particular, and that some ways of relieving are better than others.

## 2. Three roles for cost

Scanlon's lectures on the significance of choice are the natural place to begin, because they contain the distinction my argument needs and stop just short of drawing it. Scanlon separates three ways in which it can matter to a person that an outcome depend on her own response. Choice can have *instrumental* or *predictive* value: letting the diner order from the menu is usually the most reliable way of getting her food she likes. It can have *symbolic* value: where competent adults are expected to decide such things for themselves, losing the decision marks one as less than competent. And between these lies what Scanlon calls *demonstrative* value. His example: “On our anniversary, I want not only to have a present for my wife but also to have chosen that present myself” — not because this is the best method of producing a present she will like, but because the gift will mean something if he chooses it (Scanlon 1988: 179).

There is a condition on demonstrative value that Scanlon does not state, and that turns out to matter:

> **The discrimination condition.** A choice shows something about an agent only to the extent that some alternative was, for her, a live candidate — that is, only if there is a rival hypothesis about what she cares about on which she would have chosen otherwise.

This is a familiar point about evidence, wearing practical clothes. Evidence discriminates between hypotheses only where the hypotheses disagree about it. A choice that every candidate version of an agent would have made tells us nothing about which version she is. And the standard way for an alternative to be a live candidate is for the chosen option to cost something: to require giving up a good the agent had reason to want.

Hence the first role of cost.

**(R1) Evidential.** Cost is what makes a choice discriminating. It is the price that separates those who care from those who would like to have cared.

Kant's most famous passage is a study in this role, though it is not usually read that way. He introduces his examples by saying that the notion of duty contains that of a good will “although implying certain subjective restrictions and hindrances”, and then adds: “These, however, far from concealing it, or rendering it unrecognizable, rather bring it out by contrast and make it shine forth so much the brighter.” The vocabulary is optical and epistemic — concealing, unrecognizable, shining forth. The philanthropist whose mind is “clouded by sorrow of his own, extinguishing all sympathy with the lot of others”, and who nonetheless “tears himself out of this dead insensibility, and performs the action without any inclination to it, but simply from duty”, is a case in which cost isolates a motive that ordinary circumstances confound. Whether Kant also held that the absence of inclination *adds* to an action's worth is a long-running interpretive question that I set aside here. What matters is the structure of the example: the hindrance is what makes the worth *recognizable*.

**(R2) Constitutive.** Other goods are not merely evidenced by cost but partly defined by it. There is no courage where nothing is at stake; giving something up is not a symptom of sacrifice but its content; one cannot be generous with what one had no use for. Aristotle is explicit that this sets a limit to the eudaimonist thought that virtuous activity is pleasant: “Hence also courage involves pain, and is justly praised; for it is harder to face what is painful than to abstain from what is pleasant.” The brave man's losses are real losses, and they grow rather than shrink with his flourishing: “the more he is possessed of virtue in its entirety and the happier he is, the more he will be pained at the thought of death … But he is none the less brave, and perhaps all the more so, because he chooses noble deeds of war at that cost.” The concession is then drawn out in full: “It is not the case, then, with all the virtues that the exercise of them is pleasant, except in so far as it reaches its end.”

The difference between R1 and R2 is easy to miss and important to keep. Constitutive cost is a feature of the agent's *situation*, not of her psychology. Courage requires danger, not trembling; a soldier who faces the guns without inner struggle is not for that reason less brave. R1 concerns hindrances to the will; R2 concerns what is at stake in the world. Arguments that slide between them — from “her struggle reveals her” to “she must have had something to lose”, or back — are unsound, and the slide is common.

**(R3) Formative.** Bearing costs may build the dispositions that make bearing them easier: we become just, on the Aristotelian story, by doing just acts. This is the role most often invoked in defence of hardship and the least able to bear the weight. It is hostage to empirical psychology, which does not obviously support it in the strong form required; and even if true it would justify at most *exposure* to costly situations, not the actual payment of any particular cost.

The three roles are routinely run together, and the conflation does damage in both directions. The conservative complaint that ease corrupts is formative in form but is usually asserted with the confidence appropriate to a conceptual truth. The progressive reply — that this is nostalgia for suffering, and that a cost removed is simply a cost removed — treats every appearance of R2 as a disguised R1 or R3, and so never engages the possibility that some goods have prices built into them. The rest of this essay argues that the possibility is real, and that it survives the best attempt to argue it away.

## 3. Modal deflation

The best attempt runs as follows. Grant that cost is evidentially and even constitutively involved in our descriptions of what agents do. Still, nothing of *value* is lost when a third party removes it, because what we care about is a fact about the agent that is independent of whether the bill ever arrives. Maria's devotion to her brother is a fact about Maria. She had decided; she was set to sell; the cheque leaves the devotion untouched and merely spares its expression. Evidence has been lost, but evidence is not value. As for courage, what we prize under that name is a *disposition* to face danger well, and a person with the disposition has it whether or not the danger comes. Our praise tracks the modal fact, not the historical accident that tested it.

Call this **modal deflation**: the goods that appear to require cost really require only the truth of a counterfactual — that the agent *would* bear the cost were it required. Relief alters the actual world, not the counterfactual. So no value is destroyed; at most some information is lost, along with whatever formative opportunity the episode would have supplied, and the latter is an empirical matter about which we should not be dogmatic.

Modal deflation deserves its appeal. It explains why we do not think the lucky less virtuous than the tested. It blocks the ugly inference from “tests reveal virtue” to “tests are good”. It fits the plausible and widely shared view that character is a matter of dispositions rather than of episodes. And it dissolves my three cases at a stroke: the monitored employees are as honest as they ever were, the frictionless givers as generous, Maria as devoted.

I shall argue that it fails, and that the two ways it fails are the two halves of what is actually lost when goodness is made cheap.

## 4. The self-knowledge gap

Suppose the counterfactual is both determinate and true: Maria would have sold the flat. What has she lost?

She has lost her entitlement to believe it. And that entitlement is not idle.

Begin with what the entitlement is for. To promise, to take on a dependent, to enter a vocation, to tell somebody that they can count on you — each of these stakes another person's interests on your own future conduct. Doing so responsibly, rather than merely hopefully, requires some warranted confidence about what you will do when it becomes expensive. Practical self-trust is not a luxury of the introspective; it is a precondition of the commitments that structure an ordinary life.

Now notice how hard that warrant is to come by from the armchair. Predicting what one would do under serious pressure is predicting an interaction between oneself and a situation one has never met. Sincerity is no help: the person who says, and believes, that she would give up the flat is reporting a present attitude, not a future performance, and the gap between them is exactly what the situation would test. This is one modest lesson we can take from the social-psychological literature on character even if we reject its strongest conclusions. Whatever else the Milgram studies showed, they showed that people's confident predictions about their own conduct in unfamiliar high-pressure settings are unreliable in a systematic direction.

So actual payment has an epistemic role *for the agent herself* that no amount of counterfactual truth supplies. The good at stake is not “having paid”; it is being in a position to know who one is. And this good is destroyed, not merely hidden, when the occasion is pre-empted. A person who has never had to find out whether she would keep faith at cost stands to herself, in that respect, as a stranger. Her self-ascriptions are hypotheses. That is a limitation on what she can honestly say and honestly undertake — a limitation of agency, not merely of information.

There is an obvious objection, and answering it sharpens the claim. Some goods require *not* attending to oneself. The modest person does not tally her own merits; the friend who rescues you in order to discover what kind of person he is has, as Williams's phrase has it, one thought too many. Does my argument recommend a self-regarding relation to one's own conduct?

It does not, because the good I have identified is a by-product rather than an aim. Maria need not sell the flat in order to learn about herself; she learns about herself because she sold it. Like many by-products, this one is spoiled if pursued directly — the person who arranges trials for himself has arranged something other than a trial. But by-products can also be destroyed by removing their occasions, and that is what pre-emptive relief does. The asymmetry is important later: it is why the argument gives no licence to impose costs.

A second objection: is the lost self-knowledge not merely instrumentally valuable, so that we should weigh it against the enormous good of a treated brother and move on? Partly yes. But two things follow even so. The first is that the instrument has no ready substitute, since the situation that would have supplied it was rare and is now gone. The second, and more important, is that there is an interpersonal counterpart. To be trusted with reason is not the same as being trusted without one. A community in which everyone's reliance on everyone else happens to be true, but none of it is earned, is not epistemically impoverished alone; it lacks a particular kind of relationship. The deflationist owes an account of why that lack is nothing, and I do not think one is available.

Still, this first argument concedes the deflationist's central assumption: that there is a fact about what Maria would have done. The deeper trouble is that there usually is not.

## 5. The indeterminacy gap

Modal deflation requires the counterfactual “she would have paid” to have a determinate truth value. I shall argue that in the cases that matter it does not — and that this transforms the diagnosis of what relief destroys.

The cleanest statement of the underlying point comes from an unrelated dispute. Arguing that God could not have middle knowledge, Adams asks what could make true a conditional such as “if David stayed in Keilah, Saul would besiege the city”. The natural answer is the agent's psychology: Saul's intentions, the Keilahites' desires and character. Adams replies that this basis “is inadequate because it is not precisely necessitating”, since “[a] free agent may act out of character, or change his intentions, or fail to act on them.” What the psychology grounds is at most a probabilistic conditional — Saul *would probably* besiege the city — and that, Adams observes, is enough for David to act on but not enough for the theory he is attacking (Adams 1977: 110–11).

Adams's target was Molinism and his background assumption was libertarian freedom. I need neither. The point survives, in a weaker but sufficient form, under determinism. Whether Maria signs the contract depends on how the unrealized fortnight is filled in: on what her brother says on the Tuesday, on how the buyer behaves, on whether the clinic's deadline moves, on how much she has slept. Her antecedent psychology does not fix that filling. There are many candidate ways the world might have gone on from where she stood, they do not agree, and no further fact about Maria adjudicates between them. The counterfactual lacks a determinate truth value — not because we cannot discover it, but because there is nothing of the required precision to discover.

The situationist literature turns this from a possibility into a likelihood. Harman's claim is stark: ordinary moral thought commits “the fundamental attribution error”, and “there is no evidence that people have character traits (virtues, vices, etc.) in the relevant sense”, where the relevant sense is that of “broad based dispositions” that explain conduct across situations (Harman 1999: 315, 318). I need not accept the strong conclusion, which I think overshoots. I need only the weak one that almost everybody now accepts: that our traits are far less situation-invariant than folk psychology assumes, and that their grip is loosest in unfamiliar and extreme circumstances.

This yields a dilemma for the deflationist.

1. Either our character traits are robust and fine-grained enough to settle, for each unrealized high-cost situation, what the agent would have done, or they are not.
2. If they are, folk psychology about character is vindicated in precisely the strong form that the empirical literature has spent fifty years undermining.
3. If they are not, then “she would have paid” is, in the cases that matter, either merely probabilistic or simply indeterminate.
4. A probabilistic conditional cannot do the deflationist's work. “She would probably have sold the flat” does not tell us that her devotion had the magnitude at issue; it tells us that it might have.
5. So modal deflation fails just where it was needed.

And the failure is not marginal. The situations in which relief is most tempting are rare, extreme and unfamiliar — exactly those in which situational variance is greatest and behavioural track records thinnest.

Now the constructive claim. Call a choice **constitutive** for an agent when two things hold. First, before she makes it there is no determinate fact about which of two or more internally consistent continuations of her practical identity she is. Second, making it settles the matter — not by revealing an antecedent fact but by generating commitments, a history and a standing self-interpretation that constrain and licence her subsequent reasoning. A good test for the first condition is that her own honest and fully informed expectation is not authoritative: “I don't know what I will do” can be true, complete, and beyond remedy by further inquiry, and no expert knows better. The second condition is modest. It requires only that major choices have downstream normative consequences for the agent — that having sold the flat, Maria is thereafter someone who did that, which bears on what may be asked of her, what she may expect of herself, and what she may say.

I am not claiming that persons create themselves from nothing, nor that all self-ascription is retrospective invention. The claim is local and graded. Most of what we do is settled in advance by who we already are; some of it is not; and the proportion of the second kind rises with the stakes, because high stakes are where our coarse and probabilistic character structure runs out of instructions.

For constitutive choices, relief does not conceal a fact. It prevents one from coming into being. Maria's devotion to her brother, *at that magnitude*, was not antecedently there to be displayed; it would have been made. The benefactor did not merely deprive her of an occasion to exhibit something she already had, nor merely of evidence about it. He closed a question about who she is — and closed it, note, without an answer.

This is why the loss escapes Scanlon's taxonomy. Demonstration presupposes something to demonstrate. Scanlon's own formulation is suggestive here: he writes of “the features of oneself which one may desire to demonstrate or see realized in action” (1988: 179–80), and the second disjunct points where I am pointing. But it is not distinguished from the first, and nothing is built on it. The good at stake in constitutive choices is not demonstrative but **determinative**: the good of having a question about oneself settled by oneself, in the only way such questions can be settled.

The practical payoff is immediate. Because the question is about who she is, it was hers. A benefactor who answers it has not merely conferred a benefit; he has exercised an authority he did not have. This broadens the anti-paternalist story in a useful way. Scanlon locates the sting of paternalism in an implied verdict of incompetence: people “may not see [their choice] as mistaken and may attach demonstrative value to it”, and so “resent paternalistic legislation, which brands them as less than fully competent” (1988: 181). In the subsidy cases no verdict of incompetence is implied at all. The benefactor may think Maria's judgement impeccable; that is often precisely why he acts. Yet the complaint remains apt, because what has been taken over is not her competence but her authorship. The familiar taxonomy of coercion, deception, manipulation and paternalism has no slot for this, and it should.

The account also explains a phenomenon that is usually diagnosed as vanity. People who have led protected lives sometimes feel that their goodness is untried, and want, obscurely, to be tested. The feeling is not a desire for suffering and not a demand for applause. It is an accurate perception of indeterminacy: they notice that certain questions about them are open, that only they can close them, and that their circumstances have not yet allowed it.

## 6. What follows, and what does not

Three things do not follow.

**Hardship is not thereby good.** This needs saying, because the argument has exactly the shape that bad arguments in this area have. Two considerations block the inference. The first is internal: a cost arranged by a third party in order to elicit somebody's self-definition is not a constitutive choice at all. The arranger has already taken over the prior question — whether this person should face this test — and so has usurped the very authority the argument was protecting. To make someone's life an occasion for their own self-discovery is to use them, however elevated the purpose. The second is epistemic: we cannot tell in advance which burdens will be constitutive and which merely crushing. Costs destroy agency at least as often as they define it, and there is no reliable way of aiming at the good I have described. It can be lost, but it cannot be manufactured. The same applies to the debates about moral enhancement in which the phrase “the freedom to fall” has been urged against making wrongdoing impossible: my argument supports the thought that something real is at stake there, but it gives nobody a reason to arrange occasions for falling.

**Relief is not thereby wrong.** The goods I have identified are substitutable across occasions in a way that the burdens are not. A life contains no shortage of constitutive choices; if Maria is spared this one she will meet others, and she has decades in which to meet them. Her brother's untreated illness is not substitutable in any comparable sense: there is one course of rehabilitation and one window in which it works. So the ordinary asymmetry stands, and stands decisively. Relieve the burden.

**Being outweighed is not being absent.** This is where the argument earns its keep. Three implications follow for anybody in the business of lowering costs.

*Consult.* Where relief would pre-empt a constitutive choice and consultation is possible, the agent's view is not merely a courtesy. She has standing over the question, since the question is about her. What the anonymous benefactor got wrong was not helping but answering. Where consultation is impossible — emergencies, secrecy imposed by circumstance — relief may be entirely permissible and still leave a residue, which is why “you should have let me” is an intelligible complaint rather than a symptom of ingratitude, and why an apology can be owed by somebody who did the right thing.

*Lower the floor, not the ceiling.* This is the most useful principle the argument yields. Relief is objectionable in the present respect when it makes a good **unpayable-for**, not when it makes it **affordable**. Compare two ways of increasing charitable giving. An opt-out default that deducts a sum nobody notices raises totals and abolishes the act: there is no longer anything a donor does that a non-donor would not have done, so the discrimination condition fails completely, and the frictionless givers are, in the relevant sense, not giving. A low-friction interface that still requires a decision raises totals while leaving the act intact. Likewise, a public health system that removes the need for families to ruin themselves does not abolish costly care; it moves cost from the compulsory to the voluntary, which is precisely the right direction. Good relief removes the price of the minimum and leaves the price of more.

*Do not saturate an environment with enforcement.* Return to the Honest Office. Making wrongdoing impossible is not a neutral way of preventing it, and the losses are of both kinds I have described. The employees' honesty becomes not merely unknown but, for many of them, indeterminate: honesty is not a lookup table, they have never been asked, and there is now no fact about how they would have answered. That is the first loss. The second is practical. Mechanisms are local; dispositions are portable. A monitored office produces compliance within its perimeter and nothing beyond it, while the parts of life that cannot be monitored are most of life.

Here I must be honest about a tension in my own position, since the situationist premise that strengthened section 5 weakens this last point. If traits are not robust across situations, then what a person is like inside the perimeter predicts little about her conduct outside it, and the portability argument loses much of its force. Three replies, of decreasing confidence. First, the situationist critique targets *broad* trait attribution; it does not show that conduct in unmonitored settings is unrelated to anything an agent carries with her, only that what she carries is narrower and more context-bound than we assume. Second, whatever does govern conduct where nobody is watching, monitoring by its nature does not supply it, so a policy of eliminating opportunities for wrongdoing at least fails to produce the good it displaces. Third, and most importantly, the two halves of my argument do not actually conflict: situationism strengthens the indeterminacy claim as much as it weakens the portability claim, so the case against saturation survives on the first ground even if the second is conceded. What none of this licenses is leaving thefts possible in order to build character. The consideration is defeasible and weighs least where the harms prevented are grave; it weighs most in the vast middle range of pervasive, low-stakes surveillance, where we are trading a general and unpurchasable good for a small and local one.

## 7. Conclusion

Some goods require that somebody give something up. Where the cost plays an evidential role, removing it costs us information about a person. Where it plays a formative role, removing it costs an opportunity that may or may not be replaceable, on terms psychology has yet to settle. Where it plays a constitutive role — and this is the case that has gone unnoticed — removing it prevents a fact about a person from coming into existence, and leaves the question it would have answered permanently open.

The reassurance that would spare us this conclusion, that what matters is not what she paid but what she would have paid, depends on counterfactuals that our actual psychology does not underwrite. We are too coarsely made for that reassurance to be true of us. So making goodness cheap is not free, even when it is obviously right; and it is very often obviously right.

The correct response is not to keep goodness expensive. It is to notice whose loss this is, to ask her where asking is possible, to acknowledge the remainder where it is not, and to prefer the forms of relief that leave people something left to pay. That last principle deserves more attention than it gets, because it is the one that scales. Any project of arranging the world so that people cannot go wrong — by subsidy, by default, by surveillance, eventually by more direct means — should be able to say what it proposes to leave open, and to whom.
