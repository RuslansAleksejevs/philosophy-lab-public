# Off the Map: Unawareness, Obligation, and the Duties That Survive It

**Abstract.** The literature on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility assumes that
whatever an agent fails to be aware of is a proposition she could have entertained. Formal
epistemology recognises a second failure — *unawareness*, absence from the agent's space of
possibilities. I give a criterion of representability, argue that **Ought Implies
Representability** governs the deliberative 'ought', and show that option-grained obligation
therefore lapses at the frontier where novel harms are gravest. Requirements, however, are
coarser than options: coarse requirements are the *partial answers* that survive when a
presupposition of the deliberative question fails, which also disarms the duality objection.
Since only *de dicto* requirements bind the unaware, and their satisfaction cannot be verified
first-personally, frontier ethics must be institutional. A residue remains where nothing binds:
tragedy, not fault.

---

## 1. Two failures

Nadia is a hospital pharmacist.

**Sign.** A vial carries the label *do not warm above 25°C*. Nadia does not read it, warms the
solution, and a patient is harmed. She did not know warming was dangerous, but the question
*might this need to be kept cool?* was available to her, and she knew how to settle it.

**Tubing.** In 1975 she prepares neonatal infusions with the standard, licensed giving set. Its
material passed the toxicological screen of the day: test animals receive the highest dose the
assay will carry, no effect is observed, and a safety factor of one hundred fixes the human
limit.[^1] Years later it emerges that a plasticiser in the tubing harms neonates, but only at
low chronic exposure during a developmental window, so the high-dose screen was not a weak test
of the hazard but a test of something else. Nobody — not Nadia, the manufacturer, the regulator,
or the toxicologist who designed the screen — entertains the possibility that a substance
harmless at high acute dose could be harmful at low chronic dose. Their science has no place for
it: every calculation in the field assumes that effect is monotone in dose.

In *Sign* a precaution went untaken. In *Tubing* the precaution was taken; its design presupposed
a carving of the possibilities from which the actual hazard was absent. Nadia's reasoning is
impeccable given her repertoire. The repertoire is the problem.

The literature on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility calls both failures
"unawareness" and theorises the first. Its central puzzle is the tracing regress: if culpable
ignorant wrongdoing requires culpable ignorance, which requires a culpable benighting act, which
requires awareness, then "the only possible locus of original responsibility is an akratic act"
(Rosen 2004: 307, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). The responses disagree about almost everything
except the shape of the failure. *Tubing* has a different shape, and seeing it changes what we
should say about obligation, not merely about blame.

## 2. The Propositional Assumption

As Yates records, "recent theorists of the epistemic condition take true belief to be necessary
and sufficient for awareness, and they identify ignorance as the lack of true belief" (Yates
n.d., §1). Call the resulting frame the **Propositional Assumption**: the relevant epistemic
failures concern contents the agent could have entertained. It is a frame, not a doctrine; and
since ignorance is defined negatively, the unaware agent counts as ignorant. My complaint is not
that unawareness has no place but that it is *subsumed* rather than distinguished.

The subsumption shows across the map. Volitionists and weakened internalists specify the missing
occurrent or dispositional belief, and any benighting act, by contents already in the repertoire —
a dispositional belief is one "disposed to be occurrent under certain conditions" (Yates n.d.,
§1). Vice theorists appeal to failures to answer challenges the agent met; a challenge one cannot
formulate is not one dismissed. Some quality-of-will theorists keep a *de re* awareness
requirement (e.g. Talbert 2013: 242; Harman 2011: 465, cited in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.3); even on
Angela Smith's view (2005, 2017), on which failures of factual awareness "sometimes reveal
inadequate moral concern" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.3), the concern is revealed toward something the
agent's mind has hold of. Capacitarians count agents as meeting the condition if they "should and
could be aware of [the relevant considerations] given the available evidence" (Rudy-Hiller 2022,
§3.4, summarising Sher 2009: 87) — but evidence is evidence only relative to hypotheses one can
raise, and the low-dose studies of the 1990s were not evidence available to Nadia in 1975.

Levy's awareness-of-alternatives condition — that agents be blamed only "if they believed that
alternatives were available to them, and understood the significance of these alternatives" (Levy
2011: 111, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §1.1) — is a belief condition on alternatives already in
view. It says nothing about those not in view at all.

## 3. Unawareness, formally

In Fagin and Halpern's logic of general awareness, awareness is a syntactic operator independent
of knowledge: "implicit knowledge is the standard (S5) notion of knowledge; explicit knowledge
amounts to implicit knowledge and awareness" (Halpern and Rêgo 2006: 2). *A<sub>i</sub>φ* holds
iff φ is in the agent's awareness set, and *X<sub>i</sub>φ ⇔ K<sub>i</sub>φ ∧ A<sub>i</sub>φ*
(2006: §2). Unawareness is a "lack of conception rather than a lack of information" (Schipper
2014: 1, quoted in Roussos forthcoming, §1): not a position in doxastic space but absence from it.

Two features matter, and they pull in opposite directions.

> **Opacity.** No agent explicitly knows, of any particular content, that she is unaware of it.
>
> **Transparency.** An agent may know that there is something she is unaware of, without a witness.

Halpern and Rêgo state Opacity for models in which awareness is "generated by primitive
propositions" (2006: 2). That class will not serve: in it, they note, "the formula
A<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x) is valid, which does not seem to be reasonable in many
applications" (2006: 7), so *de dicto* awareness of one's own unawareness could never fail. I
state Opacity instead for the class they recommend, where awareness is only *weakly* generated;
it survives there.[^2]

Transparency is their positive result: *K<sub>i</sub>(∃x(A<sub>j</sub>x ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>x))* "is
consistent and … can be true at state *s* even though there might be no formula ψ … such that
*K<sub>i</sub>((A<sub>j</sub>ψ ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ))*" (2006: §3), and the language contains
*X<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x)*, that agent *i* "knows that there exists a formula of which he
is not aware" (2006: 2). Their example: a physician referring a patient "knows that an oncologist
is aware of things that could help the patient's treatment of which he is not aware", but not
"which specific thing he is unaware of … (if he knew which one it was, he would not be unaware of
it!)" (2006: 2).

A caution. Quantification ranges over the infinitely many quantifier-free sentences of the
language (2006: §3), so *K<sub>i</sub>∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x* holds automatically for any agent whose
awareness set is everywhere a proper subset of them. The formalism gives a *consistency* result —
no logical barrier to knowing that one's repertoire is incomplete without knowing where — not the
premise an agent needs, that she is missing something *relevant here*. That premise must come
from induction on her field's history (§6). The formalism licenses the shape of the duty, not its
occasion.

Opacity and Transparency are compatible. An agent can know that her repertoire is incomplete while
being unable to say where: she knows not that there is a sign she has not read, but only that
there are kinds of sign.

## 4. What "representable" means

> **Representability.** φ is *representable* for *S* at *t* iff *shall I φ?* is a question *S*
> can pose and treat as answerable — iff she can say, from her present repertoire, what would
> settle it.
>
> **The Revision Criterion.** A gap in *S*'s options is a failure of representability rather than
> of attention iff closing it requires *awareness revision* — a new proposition, a new
> distinction, or the correction of a logical relation *S* treats as fixed — rather than a
> redistribution of confidence over propositions she already recognises.

The vocabulary is Roussos's. Awareness revision, "the formation of a new subjective awareness
state, or space of subjective possibilities", differs from belief extension, which "involves no
evidence about what is the case", and from belief revision, which does (Roussos forthcoming, §1).
Awareness grows by *expansion* (the space was not exhaustive), *clarification* (its members were
not mutually exclusive) or *refinement* (a finer partition is introduced) (§2).

The criterion excludes a **thin** reading, on which φ is representable if the agent has its
concepts and could combine them. Nadia has TUBING, CONTAMINATE, DOSE and EFFECT; on the thin
reading *Tubing* is ordinary ignorance. But possessing a question's constituents is not
possessing the question, since concepts are held under commitments about how they combine. *Could
a dose too low to register matter more than one high enough to register?* is not a recombination
of DOSE and EFFECT but the denial of a relation under which Nadia's field holds them. This is
Roussos's clarification. Naledi could combine RAIN and SUN before her sun shower, but for her
"RAIN and SUN were genuinely exclusive and exhaustive options before", and clarification changes
one's understanding of "what is necessarily false of the world" (§2).

It also excludes a **thick** reading, on which φ is representable only if the question would
actually arise. That collapses into salience and excuses *Sign*, where *is this heat-sensitive?*
did not arise either. The test is answerability, not occurrence. In *Sign* Nadia knows what would
settle her question — read the label — so her failure is inattention. In *Tubing* the only
settling procedure available, the screen, has the contested assumption built into its design.

Three consequences. Representability is relative to a repertoire, so my verdicts are conditional
on repertoire ascriptions, which I state. The formal models do not supply the criterion: they fix
a language, and my phenomenon is the language's growth. And the kinds of growth differ
normatively. Refinement gaps usually leave a coarser representable requirement — if the missing
option is *use glass tubing*, *find out whether the tubing matters* remains, and §7 binds the
agent to it. Expansion and clarification gaps often leave nothing coarser. *Tubing* is built as a
clarification case because its coarse requirement was discharged.

## 5. Ought implies representability

> **Ought Implies Representability (OIR).** If *S* deliberatively ought to φ, then φ is
> representable for *S* when she deliberates.

The ought is the *deliberative* one, which answers the agent's question and grounds
accountability, not the objective ought that ranks acts by their upshots and is addressed to no
one.[^3] OIR constrains requirement, not evaluation: the glass line would have been better.

A requirement can be put to the agent, complied with *as such*, and its violation met with *why
did you not?* An unrepresentable requirement fails all three: putting it to her would be making
her aware of it; her compliance would be accidental; and *why did you not use a glass-lined
line?* has, in 1975, no answer that is about her.

This is the guidance argument for perspectivism about the deliberative ought, and I claim no
credit for its form. Lord's thesis, in Vollmer's statement, is that "if you deliberatively ought
to φ, then it must be possible that you perform this required action in a way that is creditable
to you" (Vollmer 2026, §2, on Lord 2015, 2017b). What I add is a new way for its antecedent to
fail. That literature's cases are ignorance of fact and unknown inability — its organising case is
a doctor "unable to give drug A" who does not know it (Vollmer 2026, §2, after Way and Whiting
2017) — and where Vollmer considers plans one "cannot even grasp", he holds that "it is usually
transparent to the subject" that she cannot intend them (§3). Opacity says that for unawareness
this is exactly what is not so.

**OIR is not *ought implies can*.** Nadia *can* use a glass-lined line; the glassware is in the
cupboard. What she lacks is the ability to pose the act, not to perform it. One could read "can"
as *can do as the upshot of deliberation*, which would cover the case; but that is not the reading
at work in the alternate-possibilities debate, and its consequences have not been drawn.

**OIR is weaker than Kiesewetter's principle, which is an advantage.** On Kiesewetter's view, in
Vollmer's statement, "a subject only deliberatively ought to φ if they are in a position to
justifiably believe that they ought to φ" (Vollmer 2026, §3, on Kiesewetter 2016). That entails
OIR. But it is an access condition on the *normative* fact, and it threatens to make moral
ignorance exculpating. OIR requires access to the *option*, not the *reason*, so someone who
thinks moral ignorance never excuses can hold it. A weaker principle that suffices for what
follows is not idle for being entailed.

**OIR differs in content from Levy's rationality condition, but not in extension here.** Levy
requires that conformity be "something they can do rationally (and not merely by chance or
accident)", in the sense of "what I can decide to do as the result of engaging in reasoning"
(Levy 2009: 735, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.2). Read as stated, that covers Nadia. What
remains is that the condition can fail in two ways and Levy develops one: his Mr Potter has the
option in view and cannot see the reason, while Nadia sees the reason — *do not poison infants* —
and lacks the option.

## 6. The frontier problem

Stanford's New Induction holds that "we have … repeatedly occupied an epistemic position in which
we could conceive of only one or a few theories that were well confirmed by the available
evidence, while subsequent inquiry would routinely (if not invariably) reveal further, radically
distinct alternatives as well confirmed by the previously available evidence as those we were
inclined to accept on the strength of that evidence" (Stanford 2006: 19, quoted in Stanford 2023,
§3.3). I need neither his conclusion nor his strength: not that unconceived options were *equally
supported*, only that some would have been *better*. The move from unconceived alternatives to
obligation has been made for scientists' *epistemic* obligations (Sample 2015, known to me only
from its abstract); mine concerns practical obligation.

The practical premise has an argument. Technique grows cumulatively, by conceptual as well as
material innovation, and its growth is unpredictable from inside, since a predictable option
would already be in the space. So the options a field recognises are always a proper subset of
those it will recognise. What I assume without proof is the distributional claim that omissions
matter most where technique is newest, where the least of the space has been explored.

Hence: (1) unrepresentable acts are not deliberatively required (OIR); (2) no agent can identify
a specific gap in her repertoire (Opacity); (3) gaps are normal, and densest where technique is
newest. So obligation *at the grain of options* lapses at the frontier, invisibly, and in
proportion to the novelty and scale of possible harm.

Is this a staged crisis, since no one thinks obligation is as fine-grained as options? No: the
standard account makes deliberative verdicts option-shaped. On the two-stage picture, deliberation
first settles "which options are open" and then "which of these options is the best" (Hedden 2012,
2015, reported in Vollmer 2026, §5); the deliberative question is "Which of my options ought I to
do?", whose complete answer "should include a specific course of action" (Vollmer 2026, §4). The
problem is internal to that account, and it threatens not blame, as the tracing regress does, but
requirement itself.

Nor is repertoire luck a rival to constitutive luck, as I first supposed. Practising in 1975
rather than 1995 is circumstantial luck, "luck in the circumstances in which one finds oneself"
(Nelkin 2025, §1). But it is a distinctive species. The standard reply to circumstantial luck asks
what an agent would have done elsewhere, holding her dispositions fixed. Here that is ill-posed:
deliberative dispositions are dispositions to respond to *represented* options, so the repertoire
cannot vary while they stay fixed. I offer this as a structural observation, not a proof.

## 7. The grain of obligation

Options are fine-grained act-types indexed to technique. Requirements need not be.

> **Coarsening.** If *S* is unaware of option *o*, *S* may still be bound by a requirement *R*
> such that (a) *R* is representable for *S*; (b) *o* would have been a means of complying with
> *R*; and (c) *S* can comply with *R* knowingly — some strategy representable to *S* is such that
> executing it constitutes compliance. Then unawareness of *o* does not unbind: *S* is required
> to comply with *R*.

Without (c) the principle is invalid. *Do not expose patients to avoidable toxic risk* satisfies
(a) and (b) in every case, so nothing would ever be residual. And if representability attached to
the *wording*, coarsening would always succeed, since *do not wrong anyone* is representable by
everyone; (c) makes it attach to the requirement. It also keeps §5 consistent: a coarse
requirement whose only means are unrepresentable fails OIR's rationale just as the fine one does.

**(i) Outcome constraints do not survive.** No strategy representable to Nadia constitutes
avoiding a hazard she cannot pick out. Such constraints fail (c), and I move them to the
evaluative side: they locate the objective wrong and justify recall, compensation and regret, but
say nothing about what anyone deliberatively ought to have done. So coarsening does not by itself
restore accountability anywhere; that is the price of validity.

**(ii) *De dicto* procedural requirements survive.** *Knowing that you are missing something,
proceed in ways that tolerate being wrong*: stage the introduction, prefer reversible
interventions, instrument outcomes, monitor for effects no one has hypothesised, do not scale
until the small case has run long enough. Transparency shows that such contents face no formal
barrier; each names a strategy whose execution is compliance, satisfying (c).

This is not the precautionary principle, which in its legal sense concerns how to act "when it is
scientifically reasonable to suspect a risk … but the evidence is not strong enough to show
conclusively that the risk exists" (Hansson 2022, §6.2). Nor is it Guerrero's principle, in
Rudy-Hiller's gloss one "that urges restraint in the face of uncertainty regarding the potential
harms that our actions might bring about" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.1, on Guerrero 2007: 79). Both are
keyed to a *represented* hazard. Family (ii) works a step earlier, where there is no hazard
hypothesis at all.

**(iii) Awareness-transferring requirements survive.** *Route this decision through someone whose
repertoire is not yours* — as the physician does in referring to the oncologist. Consultation,
external review and adversarial testing act on knowledge of one's unawareness without converting
it into knowledge of what one is unaware of. The family is a species of the duties of inquiry that
the literature distinguishes from norms of awareness, which "impose obligations to undertake
information-gathering actions" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.4, citing Rosen 2004, 2008; H. Smith 2014;
Wieland 2017a). Because (ii) and (iii) demand actions rather than states of mind, they escape the
worry that "becoming aware is not something we do but something that happens to us" (Sher 2009:
112, quoted ibid.), which leads Clarke to deny that awareness norms generate obligations (Clarke
2014: 167, reported ibid.).

### Coarse requirements as partial answers

Vollmer takes the deliberative question to be *Which of my options, A, B or C, ought I to do?* A
presupposition of a question is "entailed by every complete answer"; if one fails, "there is no
complete answer", though such questions "might still have partial answers" (2026, §4). His
**ability presupposition** is that "the best among the supposed options in the set one is
referring to is, in fact, an open option" (§5). Unawareness needs a mirror-image presupposition,
which is mine:

> **The Completeness Presupposition.** The deliberative question presupposes that the best *open*
> option is among those referred to.

His failure is a listed item that is not an option; mine is an option that is not listed. Three
consequences follow.

First, in *Tubing* Nadia's question has no complete answer: at the grain of options there is
nothing she deliberatively ought to do. OIR falls out of the semantics of questions.

Second, the duality objection dissolves. Against "nothing ought" verdicts, Blumberg and Fritz
argue, as Vollmer puts it, that since "ought or requirement and may or permission are duals, a
denial of the former seems to lead to an endorsement of the latter", yielding a permission "just
as inaccessible" as the obligation (Vollmer 2026, §3, on Blumberg and Fritz 2025). That tells
against a bare denial of obligation, not against presupposition failure: the *may*-question
carries the same presupposition, so — as Vollmer argues for his own case — no option is one the
agent "deliberatively may choose" (§5). Nadia is not permitted to use the plastic set; the
question a permission would answer has misfired.

Third, coarsening *is* the survival of partial answers: when a presupposition fails, "some
normative guidance is still to be had" (§4). *Do not proceed in a way that depends on your list
being complete* is a partial answer; so is *consult someone whose list is longer*. Families (ii)
and (iii) are not a rescue bolted onto a failed theory but what the failed question leaves
standing.

So *you should have thought of that*, taken literally, violates OIR. Taken as it is meant — *you
should not have made everything depend on your having thought of everything* — it is a charge
under (ii) or (iii), and it can be fair.

## 8. Pricing caution

A duty never priced is a mood. Expected value cannot price caution about an unrepresented harm, so
the price must depend on variables available without representing it. Two are: **scale of
exposure** (Nadia knows how many neonates are on infusions) and **irreversibility** (she knows
whether a mistake can be undone). Family (ii) requires a margin proportionate to both, defeasible
against the representable costs of delay.

Safety engineering already prices caution this way. Safety factors are multiples of a measured
quantity — human limits of "one hundredth of the highest dose … that gave no observable negative
effect in experimental animals" — and inherent safety is preferred because "as long as the hazard
still exists, it can be realized by some unanticipated triggering event" (Hansson 2022, §4). These
principles, Hansson notes, "aim at protecting us not only against risks that can be assigned
meaningful probability estimates, but also against dangers that cannot be probabilized", and "it
remains … for philosophers of technology to investigate the principles underlying safety
engineering" (§4). Family (ii) says what they answer to. *Tubing* shows a multiplier on the wrong
quantity failing, which is why family (ii) is plural and why family (iii) exists.

## 9. Objections

**"Unawareness is just deep ignorance."** I once replied that ignorance yields to evidence and
unawareness does not. That is false. Roussos guesses that "in most real cases, agents undergo
changes of awareness because they gain some evidence about the new possibilities", as when a
forecast giving snow a 30% chance makes Naledi both aware of snow and informed about it (Roussos
forthcoming, §2). The distinction is instead the Revision Criterion's: evidence that redistributes
confidence over a fixed space versus evidence that prompts revision of the space. Real episodes
combine both, which explains the intermediate cases without making the difference one of degree.

**"Quality of will handles it: Nadia's repertoire reflects her cares."** Sometimes a gap is the
shadow of a defective will. The limiting test:

> **The Shared-Gap Test.** If a gap in *technical* repertoire is shared by everyone within *S*'s
> deferential reach, whatever their evaluative orientation, it gives no *differential*
> explanation of *S*'s conduct in terms of *S*'s will.

This version concedes three things. Its reference class is fixed by family (iii): the agents *S*
could have consulted. If a reachable polymer chemist had the concept, Nadia's failure falls under
(iii) rather than excuse. It claims only the absence of a differential explanation, not that the
gap expresses no one's will. And it does not apply where a gap's uniformity is explained by whom a
community counts — harms to people absent from or unrepresented in a profession. There a shared
gap is shared incuriosity, and a shared vice is still each member's. That concession yields a
further requirement of type (iii): compose deliberative bodies so their repertoires are not
correlated. Engineers demand the same of barriers, since "if two or more barriers are sensitive to
the same type of impact, then one and the same destructive force can get rid of all of them in one
swoop" (Hansson 2022, §4).

**"OIR excuses the slaveholder who could not conceive of abolition."** The slaveholder is not
unaware of the *options*: *do not beat this person* and *let these people go* are representable to
her. What she may lack is the moral concept requiring them, and that is ignorance. There is a
representable requirement — do not inflict this suffering — with which she can comply knowingly,
so (c) is met. Whether moral ignorance excuses is a question on which OIR, unlike Kiesewetter's
principle, takes no stand. The thesis extends no excuse in the interpersonal case; its domain is
gaps in the repertoire of *means*, not in the perception of *claims*.[^4]

**"Opacity undercuts the *de dicto* duties too: Nadia might not know she is in a domain of unknown
effects."** Yes. That is the residue.

## 10. The residue, and why frontier ethics is institutional

Roussos distinguishes "conscious unawareness", in which an agent is "aware than [sic] there is
something they are unaware of", from "the presumably more common state of unconscious unawareness"
(Roussos forthcoming, §1 n. 2). Where an agent is unconsciously unaware at the level of the
*domain* — unaware not just of the item, as Opacity guarantees, but of being where items go
missing — families (ii) and (iii) lapse too.

> **The Residue.** Unawareness fully unbinds where no representable requirement, not even a *de
> dicto* one, was available: the objective ought is violated, no deliberative ought is, and no one
> is accountable.

That is the right verdict for first-of-a-kind harms: something terrible happened and it was
nobody's fault. Refusing it produces hindsight scapegoating, which manufactures a fault to match
the harm, or blanket exculpation, which infers from *no one conceived of it* to *nothing was
required*. Both assume that the grain of obligation must match the grain of harm.

**Nadia's verdict.** On the stipulated repertoires, Nadia is in the residue and the regulator is
not. She used a licensed device by the approved protocol, within a system that had discharged
*find out whether the tubing matters*, and nothing marked neonatal infusion as a domain of live
unknowns. The regulator knows that the material is novel, that the exposed population is large and
physiologically atypical, and that the screen is a screen for acute effects. That is conscious
unawareness of the domain, which triggers family (ii): staged approval, post-market surveillance,
monitoring for unhypothesised effects. If these were in the regulator's repertoire and went
unused, the regulator is accountable and Nadia is not. Unawareness moves requirement upward, not
away.

Three arguments support the institutional conclusion.

*External verification.* Compliance with a *de dicto* precautionary duty cannot be verified
first-personally: to check that I was cautious enough about what I cannot conceive, I would need to
survey it, which Opacity forbids. I can verify that I followed a procedure, not that it was
adequate to my blind spots; someone with a different repertoire can. Verification is externalised
by the duty's structure, not by human weakness. (I withdraw the unsupported claim that a duty with
no salient object loses every contest with a live one; *call the oncologist* has a salient object.)

*The meta-level regress.* Institutional designs are inventions; once, rotating reviewers with
different repertoires was unconceived. So OIR applies one level up, and at a true frontier the
compensating institutions may themselves be missing. I accept this but deny that it confines the
conclusion to easy cases. Technical options are domain-specific; institutional forms — staging,
blinding, independent review, monitoring — are domain-general and transfer once invented. The
practical New Induction is weak for them, so the meta-level repertoire is nearly saturated even
where object-level repertoires are not. The regress bites in 1750, not now.

*The kind of duty.* The duty to build such structures is itself *de dicto* in content — *use
arrangements that do not depend on anyone's having thought of everything* — and substantive only in
its costs. I withdraw my earlier claim that it is first-order "rather than a matter of
administration". The accurate claim is that since only *de dicto* duties bind the unaware, and
only outsiders can verify them, designing the verifying structures is where frontier requirements
acquire determinate content.

The account predicts that duties to consider specific items appear where domains are routinised
enough to list the items, and are absent at the frontier. I have not checked this. It would be
falsified by a mature domain whose codified duties remain purely procedural, or by a novel one
whose advance consider-this duties proved well chosen.

The epistemic-condition literature thus has a blind spot of the kind it studies: it theorises
failures of belief while subsuming failures of repertoire. Once these are admitted, morality speaks
at the frontier only in the *de dicto* voice, and so through institutions or not at all.

**Scope.** I leave open whether the deliberative ought is prior to the objective one, whether
accountability requires desert, how domains are individuated, and what an agent owes once her
awareness grows. The last matters, and the source I most wanted on it could not be retrieved.

A<sub>i</sub>¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ*; by T, *K<sub>i</sub>¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ ⇒ ¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ*. So
*X<sub>i</sub>¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ* is unsatisfiable. Opacity is a claim about models, the formal
correlate of the observation that recognising a specific gap already fills it. I read Halpern and
Rêgo directly, but not the models they characterise (Fagin and Halpern 1988; Modica and
Rustichini; Dekel, Lipman and Rustichini; Heifetz, Meier and Schipper).

[^3]: I use only a two-way contrast between objective and deliberative oughts, rely on no author's
version of the familiar tripartitions, and did not consult that debate's primary texts.

[^4]: Failures of collective interpretive resources — lacking the concept under which a harm can be
named — are structurally close to unawareness and connect with work on hermeneutical injustice
that I did not consult. They would press hardest on the collective-vice concession of §9.
