# Critique: What an Ending Can Explain

## Reconstruction and verdict

The essay argues that later-dependent descriptions can legitimately select an earlier occurrence without explaining its production. Its diagnostic fixes a local target and alleged producing conditions, changes a later continuation, and asks whether the explanation loses its ground merely because retrospective significance changes. A companion substitution check distinguishes reference to an occurrence from explanatory use of the retrospective feature. The account permits modern concepts, unintended mechanisms, future-directed intentions, evidence from later traces, and explanations of extended processes.

This is coherent and unusually readable. Revise rather than set aside. The best contribution is a disciplined method for exposing target shifts inside retrospective explanations, not the discovery that later consequences fail to cause earlier actions. The present draft sometimes inflates that modest method into a diagnosis of illicit necessity, and has not yet shown that the diagnostic resolves a hard case better than familiar distinctions do.

## Main argumentative vulnerabilities

1. **The test risks presupposing its verdict.** Holding fixed producing conditions and stipulating an independent later fire already makes the earlier occurrence invariant. The author carefully makes the test conditional, which saves validity but limits informativeness. A rival can deny independence, dispute what counts as producing conditions, or explain an extended process. Say explicitly that this is an internal consistency challenge to a specified explanation, not an independent test of causal relevance. Add a case in which initially plausible rival readings are distinguished by a concrete piece of evidence. For instance, identical references to administrative “needs” can mean evidenced 1820 incentives or only 1850 usefulness; surviving instructions to officials adjudicate the difference. Explain exactly what the continuation exercise adds to simply asking for that evidence.

2. **Passing is not endorsement, and failure need not refute the historical sentence.** A wholly false explanation by astrological influence survives the warehouse fire. Conversely, “the council adopted the decree that founded surveillance because tax disputes demanded uniform records” loses its original referential expression when the fire occurs while its explanation of adoption remains perfectly sound. The manuscript anticipates the second point through substitution but should explicitly distinguish sentence truth, successful reference, explanatory ground, and our warrant. State that surviving the test removes one specific objection only. Avoid phrasing that suggests explanatory validity follows from invariance.

3. **The opening promises to remove necessity that the argument never establishes.** Unsupported purpose and historical inevitability are separate errors. An author could falsely say surveillance needs explain adoption while accepting that adoption was contingent; another could correctly explain adoption by severe fiscal incentives yet wrongly claim its inevitability. Delete the claim that the diagnostic removes “illicit necessity,” or give a separate argument about modal strengthening. The present test identifies an unsupported explanatory transition, not all retrospective determinism.

4. **The most important structural counterexample should be developed.** Imagine standardization is repeatedly attempted across districts, and only records useful for surveillance are retained. Surveillance function might explain the existence of the record system encountered in 1850 despite explaining no initial local decree. The draft already accepts selection and feedback; use that acceptance to identify why a historian's compressed statement may be legitimate for an explanandum individuated as a surviving institution. The strongest opponent is not someone committing crude backward causation, but someone who says the whole historical question was always why this institution exists. Require clarification, not automatic accusation of error. A local target can be fixed for assessment without granting that the earlier component is the privileged historical object.

5. **Explanatory asymmetry needs a stated scope.** The draft rightly permits constitutive explanation and later phases of extended processes. Consequently the key premise is not a general ban on later explanatory facts. Define “production” more plainly as the causal coming about of the specified occurrence, and restrict the rule to unsupported transfer into that kind of explanation. Calling the problem an “explanatory circle” is also potentially misleading: the central issue is lack of a supported explanatory connection, which need not form a literal circle.

## Originality and literature

Danto is closer than the first source paragraph reveals. Beyond the project/success distinction, his pp. 167–168 discuss selection by relevance and the role of narrative sentences in justifying inclusion. The warehouse-fire maneuver also resembles his hurricane interrupting the result of rose planting on pp. 164–165. These do not prove that the proposed explanatory-use test is already present, but they require the essay to locate its increment carefully: coordinating target specification, continuation changes, and substitutions to challenge an inference within one account. Cite the relevance discussion directly rather than presenting selection as largely new terrain. Primary text: https://paperzz.com/doc/8013133/narrative-sentences---university-of-oregon .

A focused search found Nick Tosh's 2003 article “Anachronism and retrospective explanation: in defence of a present-centred history of science,” 34(3):647–659, DOI 10.1016/S0039-3681(03)00052-9. Its publisher abstract defends retrospective selection of activities ancestral to modern science, using judgments unavailable to actors. That is a close lead for the selection/actor-availability combination. Direct opening failed, so I cannot assert that Tosh anticipates the diagnostic or its explanatory restrictions. Investigate a legally accessible full text if feasible; otherwise explicitly retain this originality uncertainty. URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0039368103000529 . Do not cite the abstract as having established the whole paper's argument.

The functional explanation paragraphs articulate familiar requirements of selection or feedback. They are useful applications rather than demonstrated original contributions. The essay can remain focused without a broad survey, but should not let the absence of surveyed precedents inflate novelty.

## Significance and readability

The opening makes the issue intelligible immediately; the hypothetical decree earns its space. The prose generally distinguishes intentions, causal conditions, evidence and significance without needless jargon. Keep this. “Production” is less natural than “why the decree was adopted,” so explain the term once. “Inferential dependence,” “temporal structures,” and “explanatory authority” need no expansion beyond their concrete applications.

There is some repetition across the final two sections and the initial three-question contrast. Use any recovered space for one difficult institution-survival case and the diagnostic's explicit limits. A verified historiographical example would strengthen significance, but an honestly hypothetical difficult case is preferable to an unverified quotation. The current essay offers a clear methodological contribution with modest established novelty; it does not yet demonstrate a major new theory of historical explanation.

## Revision priorities

First, narrow the thesis to a defeasible, target-relative challenge and expressly deny that passing validates an explanation. Second, separate unsupported purpose from inevitability. Third, strengthen the legitimate compressed institutional explanation as an opponent and show the exact burden your test imposes. Fourth, acknowledge Danto's selection discussion and investigate or flag Tosh. Preserve the existing clarity and careful evidence/ground distinction.
