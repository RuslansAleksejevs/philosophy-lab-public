# What Foreknowledge Can Take Away

A reader learns a novel’s ending before opening it. She subsequently enjoys the novel more than she would have enjoyed an uninformed reading: knowing the destination lets her appreciate the construction. Yet she regrets learning it early. This need not be an error about how much pleasure she received. She may have lost a valuable way of arriving at her understanding, even while gaining a better experience overall.

The distinction matters beyond spoiler etiquette. If aesthetic value resides partly in what appreciators do, the order in which information reaches them can determine which valuable activities remain possible. An artwork can remain intact while one of its aesthetic opportunities disappears for a particular person. Conversely, the disappearance of that opportunity need not diminish the value of the person’s subsequent encounter on balance.

I defend a limited claim: some works offer an aesthetically valuable opportunity to revise an actually held interpretation through attention to their unfolding form. Foreknowledge can eliminate that opportunity. Its loss is distinct both from having information imposed without consent and from enjoying the work less. Recognizing it supports a defeasible reason to sequence explanations after initial engagement, but no general prohibition on advance knowledge.

## 1. Two questions about spoilers

Neil Levy distinguishes the effect of foreknowledge from our objection to having it thrust upon us. He proposes that much spoiler aversion concerns epistemic autonomy. He also expressly recognizes twist and puzzle stories as cases where foreknowledge can remove something enjoyable. These qualifications matter: merely announcing that spoilers interfere with discovery would repeat ground already covered. The question here is whether an aesthetically valuable opportunity can be lost independently of both an autonomy violation and a net hedonic loss. This is a question about grounds for regret, rather than a general explanation of the intensity of spoiler aversion. (Levy, section 3 and notes 5–7.)

Consider three distinct judgments. “You had no right to tell me” concerns another person’s conduct. “I would have enjoyed this more without knowing” compares pleasures. “I no longer have the chance to arrive at this understanding in that way” concerns an opportunity. These judgments often coincide, but none simply entails either of the others. A reader can request a plot summary, rationally prefer the informed experience, and still acknowledge something forgone. Another reader can resent an unsolicited summary of a work she has no interest in experiencing. The latter resentment does not by itself establish an aesthetic loss.

To separate the questions, start with an accidental disclosure involving no objectionable agent. A reader opens an electronic book; a software fault momentarily displays the final page. Set aside inconvenience and suppose no one was negligent. Something may still have been lost. Now suppose she intentionally opened that page because, after considering the options, she reasonably wanted a guided reading. The same opportunity may disappear, though she has no complaint against anyone and no reason to reverse her decision. The value of a foregone option does not depend on the choice being wrong.

But this establishes little until we explain what the option is and why it is aesthetically valuable. Almost every event excludes unrepeatable alternatives. Irreversibility is not a source of value by itself.

## 2. An interpretation changed from within

Imagine a short film constructed as follows. In its first half, repeated shots frame a woman from a distance while she watches another person leave a building. Their duration, placement, and restricted viewpoint make surveillance a reasonable interpretation. Small details also support a less threatening interpretation, though they are initially easy to assimilate to the first. A later sequence reveals that she is rehearsing how to approach an estranged family member. Earlier shots return with altered context. What looked cold becomes hesitant; a pause that seemed calculating becomes an inability to move.

Assume that this is accomplished convincingly, through the relation between framing, gesture, rhythm, and the distribution of information. Nothing in the argument depends on the merit of every film with this premise. A clumsy version would fail to supply the value at issue.

Compare two attentive viewers. The first provisionally sees the sequence as surveillance, notices details that trouble that interpretation, and eventually reorganizes her understanding. The second knows the situation beforehand. She notices all the same formal details and understands why they could sustain an initial misreading. She can admire the construction, respond to the character, and reconstruct the uninformed viewer’s route. Her experience need be neither shallow nor inferior overall.

Nevertheless, the first viewer performs an activity the second does not. She allows the work’s developing evidence to alter a live interpretation of her own. The second tracks an alteration that the work could produce; the first undergoes and partly conducts it. The difference is not merely that one person feels surprise. An abrupt unrelated revelation could surprise without providing an intelligible revision. Nor is the distinction between intelligence and passivity: the informed viewer may do more sophisticated interpretive work.

Call the relevant activity *work-guided revision*. It has three conditions. First, the appreciator provisionally endorses an interpretation that is reasonably supported by what has so far been presented. Second, further attention to the work gives her grounds to modify that interpretation. Third, the transition itself contributes to her appreciation of how the work organizes significance, expression, or form. These conditions distinguish an aesthetic encounter from simply learning that an earlier guess was false.

The third condition is evaluative, but it is not an empty restatement that the activity is valuable if it is valuable. It identifies what must make the activity worth having. In the film, the work places the viewer’s own tendency to construe stillness as menace in a relation to stillness as vulnerability. The viewer experiences the expressive force of the same arrangement changing as her understanding changes. This is one way the film makes its formal choices appreciable. The viewer does not merely receive a proposition about ambiguity; she participates in the organization of that ambiguity.

One can deny that this participation adds value. But a substantial reason favors recognizing it: success in such a construction is itself an intelligible object of aesthetic praise. We distinguish a revelation that transforms earlier details from one that arbitrarily replaces them. That distinction concerns how the work recruits and reorganizes attention, not simply whether its conclusion is surprising or its story true. A successful exercise of the capacity that grounds this praise can accordingly be worth experiencing. Work-guided revision is a realization of that capacity.

This does not establish that every realized capacity of a good work is valuable. Some capacities are incidental or harmful. Here the connection is direct: the appreciator’s transition is the event through which a specifically organized expressive relation becomes salient. The proposed value lies in attentive participation in that relation.

## 3. Activity, achievement, and understanding

Nguyen’s engagement account provides an important precedent. It treats the process of seeking correct aesthetic judgment, rather than merely possessing the judgment, as central to appreciation; his discussion includes interpreting, rethinking, and directing perceptual attention. The present proposal accepts the local possibility that such activity matters without making it the foundation of all aesthetic value. It adds a temporal question: which valuable activities cease to be available once the appreciator knows enough? (Nguyen, abstract and manuscript section “What is the value in direct autonomy?”)

The proposal is narrower than a general demand to think for oneself. The uninformed viewer need not invent an interpretation without assistance. Someone may suggest watching the woman’s posture, discuss alternative explanations, or help the viewer notice the later shift. Such help can sustain the activity. What matters is whether an interpretation remains live and answerable to the work, not whether every ingredient of it originates privately.

Nor does the value require winning a puzzle. Our viewer may never predict the revelation. The film’s achievement can depend precisely on her reasonable failure to anticipate it. She can participate attentively without earning credit for solving anything difficult. Her value need not be pride in competence. A simple, beautifully controlled reorientation may supply it as well as an intricate mystery.

Still, the objection that this is generic intellectual activity has force. One can revise a mistaken interpretation of a bus timetable. Merely discovering that information was misleading does not turn reading it into an aesthetic episode. The answer is not to insist on an absolute boundary between aesthetic and epistemic goods. Aesthetic appreciation often uses ordinary capacities. What makes their exercise aesthetically relevant here is its object and organization: framing and temporal arrangement make an expressive change available through the viewer’s shifting construal. The value of the activity depends on these features working together. Replacing the film with a list of correct plot facts preserves information but removes the basis of the particular appreciation.

We should therefore distinguish three things: reaching an accurate interpretation; acquiring an understanding of the work’s construction; and realizing a valuable way in which that construction can engage someone. The informed viewer may have the first two as fully as anyone. The argument does not downgrade her understanding or challenge her standing as a critic. It claims only that she cannot realize one of the available activities concerning the fact she already knows.

This last qualification is crucial. Foreknowledge is rarely exhaustive. Knowing a plot conclusion may leave the manner of revelation, a character’s motivation, or a visual relation unsettled. Many opportunities for work-guided revision survive. The loss attaches to a particular interpretive transition, not indiscriminately to the whole work or every future reading.

## 4. Why reenactment is not replacement

An informed viewer may imagine being uncertain. She can attend to evidence in presentation order, withhold commentary, and recall how the earlier scenes once appeared. Why is that not enough?

It can be enough for several purposes: enjoying suspense, reconstructing the narrative’s effects, or understanding its design. The claim requires no theory according to which foreknowledge abolishes suspense. But imagining that an interpretation is unsettled does not normally make one’s own interpretation unsettled. Rehearsing a route to a known solution and determining where a route leads are distinguishable activities, even when their phenomenology overlaps.

Someone might respond that the phenomenological overlap is all that matters aesthetically. This is the clearest point of disagreement. If only the felt character of an encounter matters, then a perfect experiential duplication will preserve its entire aesthetic value. The present account instead allows aesthetic value to belong partly to an actual relation between attentive activity and the work. This is motivated by our concern with whether an apparent insight actually responds to features of an artwork, rather than merely feeling insightful. The film viewer’s revision has such a relation: her interpretation changes because of what she discovers in the work.

That motivation supports, but does not conclusively prove, a nonexperiential component of aesthetic value. An experientialist could insist that the important relation merely causes or warrants a valuable experience. Yet she would then need to explain why a compelling but wholly unresponsive simulation of insight is aesthetically interchangeable with responsive appreciation when their pleasures match. Denying interchangeability is not necessarily a moral objection to deception or an epistemic preference for truth. It can concern whether the encounter realizes the work’s way of organizing attention.

Memory complicates the claim of irreversibility. A reader may forget an ending and encounter it again under genuine uncertainty. Then the opportunity can return. “Nonrenewable” would be too strong if it meant impossible under any circumstances. The practical claim is that disclosure normally removes an opportunity that replay alone cannot restore. One cannot ordinarily decide to unknow what one has just learned. This modest asymmetry is sufficient for a reason to consider sequencing.

## 5. Prediction and the fate of an opportunity

A distinction between losing and realizing an opportunity explains why self-generated foreknowledge is not uniform. If the viewer infers the family relationship by attending to the early film, she may already have exercised the relevant agency. Later confirmation completes a route that she started herself. Reaching the interpretation earlier than the director expected need not destroy the value of reaching it through the work.

But suppose the answer occurs to her as a confident, lucky thought before she encounters any relevant feature. She takes it as settled and never subjects it to revision. Her belief may foreclose an opportunity despite being self-generated. The account predicts that provenance by itself does not settle aesthetic loss. Independent guessing can close a valuable path, while collaborative viewing can realize it.

There is also no requirement that everyone regret successful anticipation. A viewer can reasonably prefer seeing how the filmmaker implements a structure she has inferred. Nor must a lucky guess make the story aesthetically defective. Predictability is a relation between a work and an audience with particular expectations. An opportunity can disappear for a viewer without a defect arising in the object.

These distinctions limit the autonomy explanation rather than refute it. A person may resent imposed knowledge even when it removes no valuable interpretive activity. Conversely, a valuable activity may become unavailable without any imposition. We should not infer from the absence of a pleasure decrement that only a social convention could ground regret; neither should we infer from the presence of regret that an artistic opportunity has been destroyed.

## 6. The modest priority of an uncompleted route

What follows for criticism and presentation? Where both informed and uninformed engagement are attractive, an uninformed encounter can normally be followed by informed reconsideration. The reverse sequence cannot normally recover the initial live question. This gives an initial encounter a kind of option-preserving priority.

The qualification “where both are attractive” prevents a false dominance argument. Time and attention are scarce. An unsupported viewer may abandon a difficult work, become confused in an unrewarding way, or simply prefer the richer first encounter that explanation enables. Starting without help can also influence later appreciation adversely. It is false that uninformed-then-informed experience always contains everything obtainable through informed experience alone.

The defensible recommendation is conditional. When an appreciator has reason to value both routes, can realistically take both, and loses little by delaying decisive information, preserving the live interpretive question has an aesthetic justification. A critic can therefore give orientation before giving resolution: direct attention to relevant features, then offer a settled interpretation when the reader chooses it. This is not an instruction to maximize ignorance. Good orientation makes a valuable question tractable; withholding needed context can make it unintelligible.

The same distinction guards against paternalism. Preserving an option is not a mandate to impose it. A reader who knowingly chooses advance explanation may sacrifice a real good for a greater one. Respecting that decision is compatible with describing the sacrifice accurately. Calling every disclosure “spoiling” obscures both the worth of guided engagement and the specificity of the opportunity forgone.

Aesthetic value is thus not exhausted by the work considered alone, by the truth of the viewer’s final judgment, or by how pleasant the encounter is. Some of it can reside in a successful temporal relation between a work and an appreciator’s changing understanding. Once that relation becomes unavailable, a later encounter can still be excellent, perhaps better overall. A lost way of appreciating need not make what remains a lesser work—or a lesser appreciation.
