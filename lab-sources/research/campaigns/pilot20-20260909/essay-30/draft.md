# What a Simpler Animal Model Does Not Deny

An animal solves a problem. One investigator explains its success by attributing a cognitive capacity: remembering an outcome, anticipating a consequence, or representing a relation. Another constructs a model that produces the same success through learned associations. What has the second investigator shown? Perhaps that the experiment does not establish the proposed capacity. Perhaps that the animal did not exercise it. Perhaps that the animal lacks it. These conclusions are different, and the distance between them does not disappear when the alternative model becomes impressively accurate.

I defend a constraint on negative inferences about animal cognition: the scope of a denial must be supported by the scope of the model’s exclusions. A model that can reproduce behavior without a capacity does not thereby describe a mechanism that excludes its exercise; a mechanism that bypasses a capacity in one setting does not thereby exclude its possession. Moving between these claims requires additional evidence. I develop this constraint through the distinction between realizing a capacity and bypassing it, then show why even an excellent bypass explanation can leave a capacity attribution untouched. The positive proposal is to assess absent capacities against independently constrained opportunities for their exercise. This permits serious negative evidence while blocking both premature denial and an indefinitely retreating appeal to hidden competence.

## Three claims hidden in one objection

Consider a proposed capacity C and an experimental dataset E. The following objections have different targets:

1. **Explanatory non-necessity:** E can be adequately explained without attributing C.
2. **Non-exercise:** C did not guide the behavior responsible for E.
3. **Non-possession:** the animal did not possess C during the relevant period.

The first concerns an evidential relation. The second concerns the production of particular behavior. The third concerns the animal’s repertoire. These are not three quantities on a single scale. In particular, the existence of a capacity does not guarantee its exercise, and the availability of an explanation does not establish that explanation’s truth.

An associative model may establish the first claim if its mechanisms are sufficiently specified, its learning history is plausible, and it actually accounts for the relevant observations. A verbal suggestion that the animal could have learned something is not enough. But even a fully worked countermodel establishes only that a particular inference from success to capacity possession is not compulsory. It may undercut the experiment’s claim to demonstrate C without providing a reason to believe that C is absent.

This distinction does not depend on denying inference to the best explanation. Suppose one model predicts the observations much better than its rivals and is supported by independent evidence. We may then have good reason to believe its account of how the animal acted. The question remains what that account says about C. It may say that C was realized through a mechanism whose description looks different from the original hypothesis. Alternatively, it may say that another mechanism controlled the response. Neither conclusion is automatically a verdict on the animal’s entire repertoire.

Two existing criticisms help locate this problem. Buckner distinguishes senses of association that concern the construction of models from senses intended to exclude cognitive processes; their conflation conceals the possibility that associative mechanisms implement cognition (2011, pp. 321–322). Dacey separates statistical hypotheses from substantive models and rejects granting a model default status merely because a statistical null has survived. He also explicitly observes that possessing a capacity does not determine how likely an animal is to use it in a particular setting (2023, §§3–5). These are substantive precedents, not conclusions first discovered here.

The further task is to connect them. Even after we stop privileging associative models and accept that cognition can have associative implementations, a well-supported local model may still be allowed to carry an unsupported global denial. That error survives an otherwise sensible model comparison. We need to examine the exclusions of the winning model, not merely improve the competition that selects it.

## Realization and bypass

I use a modest example rather than trying to define cognition as a whole. Let C be the capacity to alter a learned action selectively in light of a change in the value of its expected outcome, without relearning the action’s consequences through renewed reward. This does not stipulate consciousness, a concept of causation, or human practical deliberation. It identifies a limited flexibility for which a representational explanation is worth investigating.

Dickinson discusses a familiar experimental way to probe this flexibility. Rats learn an action associated with one food; that food is subsequently devalued away from the action. Testing the action without providing the food helps distinguish sensitivity to the outcome’s changed value from direct reinforcement of a new response. Dickinson also argues that appropriately organized associative processes can implement aspects of rational cognition (2012, §3 and conclusion). The empirical paradigm therefore resists treating “associative” as a verdict by itself.

Imagine two mechanisms capable of producing the same action while the food remains attractive. The first stores a response tendency strengthened during training. The second stores an action–outcome relation and combines it with the outcome’s current value. The second mechanism may itself be entirely associative. If its organization supports C, redescribing it as a network of associations does not explain C away. It explains how C works.

The first mechanism presents a different possibility. A fixed response tendency can bypass the outcome-sensitive operation while still producing the rewarded action. Once the food is devalued outside the action setting, the two mechanisms may diverge. This is an architectural contrast, not a contrast between impressive and unimpressive behavior or between two vocabularies.

The claim that a mechanism realizes C requires justification. Merely assigning a unit the label “outcome” does not give it representational content. One must establish that the unit’s information and causal role support the flexibility at issue. Conversely, establishing that a system lacks explicit sentence-like symbols does not show it lacks that flexibility. For this essay, the important requirement is that the criterion for C be stated independently of which model wins. Otherwise every successful associative model can be classified as non-cognitive by definition, or as cognitive simply because it succeeds.

This requirement leaves room for disagreement about representation. Someone may accept the modest capacity while denying that it deserves a representational interpretation. The present argument does not settle that dispute. It identifies what follows conditionally once investigators agree on a target capacity and a criterion for its realization. Such agreement can be local; it need not await a theory of every animal mind. A dimensional approach to causal cognition, such as Starzak and Gray’s (2021), offers another reason to replace sweeping verdicts with more specific targets.

## A model can win without settling possession

Now compare three hypothetical animals. Animal H has only the response-tendency mechanism relevant to this task. Animal R has the outcome-sensitive mechanism and uses it. Animal HR has both, but in a familiar training context a control process routes behavior through H. Outside that context, under some independently specifiable condition, R can guide its actions. The letters name explanatory possibilities, not established anatomical divisions.

While the food is attractive, all three animals may act alike. Following devaluation, suppose H and HR continue the trained response, whereas R reduces it. The result supports a habitual account over an account on which outcome sensitivity controls responding in that context. It does not discriminate H from HR. Both predict the same failure to respond to devaluation, but only H lacks C.

This matters even when the habitual model fits extremely well. Give H and HR the same response mechanism, noise distribution, and learning parameters wherever the context keeps the second mechanism out of control. They then assign exactly the same probabilities to every possible sequence of observations obtainable under those conditions. Repeating those observations can sharpen the contrast with R. It cannot separate H from HR. Relative to this pair, the likelihood ratio remains one.

The claim is conditional and elementary, but its consequence is easy to miss: an experiment can become increasingly decisive about the mechanism controlling performance without becoming more decisive about possession of the bypassed capacity. More data can strengthen a conclusion while leaving its scope unchanged. The route to progress is not always a larger sample or a more accurate model of the original task. Sometimes the unresolved question requires changing the conditions under which control is allocated.

A critic may reply that HR is needlessly complex. If H explains everything, why posit the extra capacity? There are two answers. First, there may indeed be no reason to posit it. Suspending attribution is an appropriate result. The objection is to treating lack of reason to add R as though the observations had independently shown its absence. Second, model simplicity may supply a defensible prior preference or an explanatory consideration, but then that preference must bear the argumentative weight. The fit of H to these observations cannot do it again. Moreover, relevant evidence might concern other behavior, neural organization, or developmental history. The smallest model of one experimental task need not be the most economical account of the animal.

HR is not offered as a universal rescue hypothesis. If its only description is “the capacity exists but always hides,” it has little scientific value. It functions first as a counterexample to an invalid inference. A possibility can show that a conclusion fails to follow without being sufficiently supported to deserve acceptance. Turning that possibility into a serious empirical rival requires a specification of when the capacity should matter.

Nor is the difference merely fanciful. Bouton reviews findings from his laboratory in which contextual changes and other manipulations can restore goal-directed control after habitual responding. He interprets habit learning as potentially inhibiting rather than erasing goal-direction (2021, abstract and introduction). This does not establish HR as the correct account of any arbitrary animal experiment. It establishes that the distinction between current control and retained capacity cannot safely be dismissed as a philosopher’s contrivance.

## Opportunities make denial possible

How can the absence of a capacity be investigated without either demanding exhaustive tests or protecting it from every failure? The answer is to make the opportunities for its exercise part of the substantive hypothesis.

A scientifically useful attribution of C should specify a range of circumstances in which C is expected to make an observable difference. Call this range D. The specification includes the animal’s relevant learning history, perceptual access, motivation, available actions, and time for responding. It also includes expectations about when another process might prevent expression. A hypothesis that an animal can flexibly update an action after an outcome changes should say more than that it might do so somewhere, sometime.

D is not defined as whatever circumstances happen to produce success. It must be constrained by evidence beyond the disputed performance or by explicit commitments made before that performance is known. For example, independent tests might establish that the animal discriminates the foods, detects the change in value, remembers the action’s consequence, and can execute both relevant responses. A previously supported account of contextual control might identify a setting in which habitual responding should weaken. None of these observations alone guarantees expression of C. Together they can justify expecting a difference if the particular capacity hypothesis is true.

Consider returning to H and HR. Specify HR more sharply: after a particular familiar contextual cue is removed, the outcome-sensitive mechanism should influence choice immediately, without additional action–reward training. Now design a comparison in which the cue changes but perceptual access, motor requirements, and the relevant outcome information are retained. H and the sharpened HR can make different predictions. If the animal retains insensitive responding under conditions in which HR predicts a reliable change, the result counts against HR. If sensitivity appears, the explanation must address why; it is not automatically proof of the stipulated internal architecture.

The qualification about immediate change matters. Extensive new training could create the capacity whose earlier presence is being investigated. A successful later test might then establish acquisition rather than reveal retention. The opportunity must be suitable not only for displaying C but for preserving the temporal claim. This is another respect in which the scope of the conclusion needs defending.

Negative evidence becomes stronger when several independently motivated opportunities fail and the main masking explanations receive contrary evidence. It need not become certain. The rational conclusion can be that the animal probably lacks C within a specified domain at the specified time. Generalizing to a population, a species, or all possible contexts introduces additional claims about variation and transfer. Those claims require their own support.

This approach places a burden on the capacity advocate as well as the skeptic. After a failed prediction, a revised masking explanation is permissible, but it changes the hypothesis. It should earn credibility by predicting something beyond the failure that prompted it: perhaps a specific recovery condition, a pattern of interference, or a dissociation from another task. A succession of explanations that only say why yesterday’s test was unfair does not cumulatively establish possession. Equally, a skeptic cannot count repeated trials within the very same alleged mask as independent demonstrations that no capacity exists. The dispute should turn on the credibility and range of the expression conditions.

There is no general prohibition here on absence-of-evidence reasoning. Brown and Key defend its possible legitimacy in debates about animal pain and warn against using multiple realizability as an automatic escape from negative evidence (2021, §§1–2). The present argument accepts that methodological point without endorsing a particular claim about pain. Evidence that a necessary enabling organization is absent could also support a capacity denial. The crucial work would lie in defending its necessity at the relevant level and establishing its absence, not in pointing out that some simpler model reproduces one behavior.

## Is this only familiar experimental caution?

The distinction between competence and performance is familiar. So are demands for controls and warnings about underdetermination. Why add another statement of them?

The value lies in identifying two separable places where an apparently deflationary explanation can fail to deny what it appears to deny. The first concerns constitution: the winning mechanism may realize the target capacity. The second concerns allocation: it may bypass a capacity that remains available. Correcting the first does not correct the second. Conversely, acknowledging latent competence does not establish whether an associative mechanism realizes or bypasses it. These are different explanatory questions and recommend different next investigations.

If the problem is constitution, investigators need to examine the organization, information, and causal roles inside the model. If the problem is allocation, they need evidence about where and when the relevant organization gains control. A task can be excellent for distinguishing two controlling processes yet poor for investigating whether both are present. Confusing those purposes can make a productive experiment appear to have settled a broader philosophical question.

The proposal also explains why a result may remain informative after its strongest advertised interpretation is withdrawn. Establishing habitual control is an achievement even if it does not establish a general absence of outcome-sensitive cognition. A restriction of scope preserves that discovery. It may reveal something more interesting than a boundary between species with and without a faculty: a boundary between circumstances under which one animal can use what it has learned.

One final objection is that defining possession through opportunities reduces a mental capacity to behavior. That is not the proposal. D specifies evidential commitments of an attribution, not a complete metaphysical analysis of C. Mechanistic, developmental, and neural evidence can help establish both possession and non-possession. The opportunity requirement matters when a claim is being tested through performance. It asks what difference possession should make under the conditions investigators can justify, while leaving open which internal organization explains that difference.

An unconstrained claim about a capacity that could never affect any accessible observation may remain logically possible. It cannot demand the same evidential standing as a claim that risks failure. Respecting that limit is compatible with refusing to interpret every successful simpler model as an inventory of everything the animal lacks.

## Conclusion

A simpler model can undermine a claimed demonstration, identify the process controlling a response, or support the absence of a capacity. Which achievement it secures depends on what its evidence excludes. The associative character of the model does not answer that question. Nor does excellent fit to a restricted task.

The central discipline is to keep constitution, current control, and possession apart. Establish whether the model realizes the capacity or bypasses it; if it bypasses it, investigate independently constrained opportunities under which possession should matter. Failure there can support a negative conclusion. Success elsewhere can expose a limit of expression rather than the emergence of a new faculty. This discipline neither presumes animal sophistication nor rewards skepticism by default. It makes each side specify the claim its evidence is actually capable of deciding.
