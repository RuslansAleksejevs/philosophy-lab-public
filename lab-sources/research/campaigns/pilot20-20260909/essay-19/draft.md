# What a Checked Proof Has Not Yet Told Us

A proof assistant reports success. Its kernel is sound, its assumptions are acceptable, and its proof object really establishes the formula displayed. A mathematician nevertheless asks whether the announced theorem has been proved. This need not express distrust of computers. It may express uncertainty about which mathematical claim the formula represents. Another successful execution of the same checker can answer a question about reproducibility while leaving this question untouched.

The familiar lesson is that formal verification requires faithful formalization. The less familiar question is what follows for the assessment of evidence. I argue that successful checks have a limit set by the distinctions they can register. When an identified, consequential misinterpretation would survive a check just as readily as the intended interpretation, that check cannot, by itself, answer the challenge posed by the misinterpretation. Mathematical communities should therefore assess assurance by the relevant errors that evidence excludes, rather than by the number or technical sophistication of successful checks. A carefully chosen contrast can contribute more to one interpretive question than another complete proof replay.

This is a conditional epistemic norm, not a demand that all mathematics acquire a new ritual. It neither makes examples into proofs nor requires every user of a theorem to understand its proof. Its point is to explain when a particular request for more evidence is rational, what kind of evidence would answer it, and why impressive evidence of another kind may not.

## Three claims behind one announcement

Consider the announcement, “This development proves that a sequence converges uniformly.” It can compress at least three claims. First, a formal formula follows from the development's assumptions by accepted rules. Second, the formula and its definitions express uniform convergence in the intended sense. Third, the result therefore licenses the ordinary uses of uniform convergence, such as selecting a single approximation threshold that works throughout the specified domain. The first claim concerns derivation. The second connects representations. The third exports the result into mathematical reasoning.

The distinctions do not make the three claims unrelated. Reading a definition may establish the second claim straightforwardly; the first and second can then support the third. Nor is the first claim merely a fact about meaningless marks. Mathematicians can understand the formal formula perfectly well. The issue is the additional identification expressed when a result is announced under a familiar mathematical description.

Adams explicitly distinguishes an incorrect formal statement, including errors in its supporting definitions, from unsound inference. His proposed proof audit combines replay with review of the final statement and its dependencies. The need to check correspondence is therefore no new discovery here (Adams 2016, §3.1.2 and §4.3). Avigad likewise treats the recognition of logical and mathematical structure as part of understanding assertions, rather than treating a successful derivation as a complete account of understanding (Avigad 2010, §3.1).

My concern begins after these distinctions have been accepted. Suppose a team responds to an interpretive challenge with more and better evidence of derivability. When should that response improve confidence in the announced theorem? The answer depends on whether the new evidence bears on the contested connection, not simply on whether it is strong evidence somewhere in the argument.

## A theorem that passes under the wrong name

Let a sequence of real-valued functions f_n on a domain D have limit f. Pointwise convergence requires that, for every x in D and every positive error tolerance ε, there be a threshold N such that every n at least N satisfies |f_n(x) − f(x)| < ε. The threshold may depend on x. Uniform convergence requires that, for every positive ε, there be a single N that works for every x in D and every n at least N. The difference lies in the position of the quantifier over x relative to the choice of N.

Imagine a development whose predicate called “uniform convergence” accidentally uses the pointwise quantifier order. This is not a supposition that the kernel secretly ignores quantifiers. The kernel respects exactly the predicate it receives. A long, valid proof about the misnamed predicate can therefore be checked without detecting the error.

Take f_n(x) = x^n on D = [0,1), with candidate limit zero. For each fixed x in this interval, x^n tends to zero. Thus the sequence satisfies the erroneous predicate. But the convergence is not uniform. Set ε = 1/2. Whatever threshold N is proposed, choose a positive integer n at least N, and put x = (3/4)^(1/n). This x belongs to [0,1), while x^n = 3/4. No common threshold controls every point. The formal result under the mistaken predicate is true; the announcement of uniform convergence is false.

Now narrow the domain to [0,1/2]. Here 0 ≤ x^n ≤ (1/2)^n, so a threshold making (1/2)^n less than ε works for all x. Both the correct and mistaken predicates accept the narrowed case. Many checks made only on this smaller domain would therefore be compatible with either predicate. They would show that these instances behave as expected, but they would not test the missing uniformity.

The larger domain supplies a discriminating contrast. The investigator asks whether the formal predicate accepts the sequence on [0,1). If it does, while the intended concept requires the common threshold just shown impossible, the purported identification fails. A definition review can reveal the same failure more directly by inspecting the quantifiers. The contrast is valuable because it gives the formal difference a visible mathematical consequence. It explains what the quantifier order permits and prevents.

Nothing in this example is a report of an actual defective library or a completed machine experiment. It is a deliberately small mathematical model of a possible error. Its simplicity lets us grant complete confidence in proof checking while holding the representational question open. Once the formula is inspected competently, of course, there are no two equally correct readings of its quantifier order. The uncertainty belongs to people assessing the relation between the formula and the announcement, not to the formal semantics itself.

## Why repeated success can leave a question unchanged

The example supports an argument with a restricted conclusion. An investigator is uncertain between two explanations of an announcement: the development uses the intended predicate, or it uses a nearby mistaken predicate. The investigator has already accepted that whichever formula was submitted has a valid proof. Another operation that merely checks that same derivability supplies no new discrimination between the two explanations. The possible mistake is outside the operation's remit.

This does not entail that replaying a proof never increases confidence in the announced theorem. A replay may remove a different obstacle: perhaps the original success report was inaccurate, or the saved files differ from those used previously. Removing that obstacle can increase overall confidence. But it does not thereby discharge the outstanding question about the predicate. One can remove uncertainty about delivery while leaving uncertainty about the delivered object intact.

Nor does the argument rest on treating all successful checks as probabilistically independent, or on assigning numerical probabilities to mathematical truths. The operative distinction is between reasons addressing different possibilities of error. An inference of the form “the proof has now passed five checks, so the concern about quantifier order has been answered” requires a further premise explaining how those checks bear on quantifier order. Without it, the inference changes the question while keeping the word “verified.”

Sometimes the further premise is available. A second checker may expose the fully expanded statement, and its operator may compare that statement with the intended definition. Or the history of a team's work may make successful checking indirectly informative about its care in translation. Such evidence should be counted for what it actually includes. It is not the naked fact of derivability that settles correspondence. In the first case, there is an additional act of interpretation; in the second, a defeasible inference about a reliable process.

The resulting norm is this: when a concrete and consequential rival interpretation remains a live possibility, a claim that it has been excluded must rest on evidence capable of distinguishing it from the intended interpretation. Merely repeating evidence that leaves that distinction untouched does not discharge the claim. The norm is intentionally local. It does not demand protection against every logically possible mistranslation. It demands an answer to a specified alternative that threatens an advertised mathematical use.

The practical suggestion of testing definitions also has precedents. The public math-commons assurance conventions distinguish verification from validation and propose checks of known values and nondegeneracy (math-commons, README, “The assurance model” and “Documents”). Such conventions illustrate existing practice proposals; they do not establish that any particular test suffices. The philosophical point developed here is the condition under which a challenge remains unanswered even after additional authentic verification. Test quantity does not settle that condition.

## Why contrasts are evidence about mathematics

A contrast is useful only if its expected outcome has a warrant that does not inherit the disputed error. If both the theorem and its test are generated by expanding the same mistaken predicate, their agreement is no surprise. This is a dependence problem even when different people, programs, or proof assistants perform the work. Separation of workers may help, but the relevant separation is between the reasons fixing the intended outcome and the assumption under challenge.

In the convergence example, the intended outcome is anchored by the demand for one threshold and the explicit construction defeating every proposed threshold. That reasoning is open to criticism independently of the name attached to the formal predicate. It can also be formalized. Nothing requires the discriminating evidence to remain informal or to be produced by a human. What matters is that the bridge from the intended question to the test has not simply been assumed in constructing the answer.

Positive contrasts have a role too. The narrowed interval demonstrates that the test is not rejecting convergence indiscriminately. More generally, a useful challenge specifies both what should remain possible and what should become impossible when a mathematically relevant condition changes. Nevertheless, no universal prescription requires paired tests. Sometimes a single definition inspection, an equivalence theorem to an accepted formulation, or a counterexample to a proposed identification is decisive enough for the question at hand.

There is also an asymmetry between success and failure. Finding a case where the formal predicate and the intended condition diverge defeats their claimed equivalence. Agreement on finitely many cases usually cannot establish equivalence over an infinite domain. Successful contrast checks therefore support correspondence defeasibly; they do not replace a general account of the definitions. Their epistemic role is often to remove a particular reason for doubt, rather than to provide an exhaustive semantic foundation.

This distinction prevents a misleading response to powerful proof automation. One cannot repair an uncertain specification by demanding an arbitrarily large collection of certified consequences drawn from it. All may faithfully reflect the same mistaken premise about meaning. The needed gain is a relevant constraint on the interpretation, not further elaboration within an interpretation already chosen.

## Three objections

First, is this merely good software engineering? A mistake in a predicate resembles a mistake in a program specification, and the proposed contrasts resemble tests. The resemblance is welcome. But the argument concerns when a mathematical claim is warranted, not only how to reduce development costs. In the example, a fully correct artifact is insufficient for the announced mathematical conclusion because the evidence supports another proposition. Efficiently producing the artifact and being entitled to use it are different questions. Engineering methods can address an epistemic requirement without exhausting its philosophical explanation.

The philosophical consequence is modest but substantive: strength of derivational assurance and adequacy of interpretive assurance need not increase together. Thus a hierarchy of mathematical warrants ordered solely by proof-checking rigor misses a dimension of error. A short ordinary argument about the threshold may answer the outstanding question better than a technically stronger audit of derivation. This is not because informal reasoning enjoys a general superiority. It is because the former reason addresses the contested proposition.

Second, does the norm overburden testimony? A mathematician routinely uses theorems without checking their definitions from first principles. Must everyone devise a contrast before knowing a formalized result? No. Competent testimony that the statement has been reviewed can support the relevant connection. Familiar definitions in a well-understood setting can make a suggested mistranslation too remote to deserve further work. The duty arises when an alternative is salient, credible, and consequential for the use being claimed. Even then, a user may reasonably rely on another person's answer. The account identifies what the evidential chain must address; it does not insist that each person reconstruct the chain privately.

Third, does formalizing mathematics eliminate the representational gap by making the formal statement the theorem itself? It can eliminate one intended task. A community may decide that its object of study is exactly the explicitly given formal predicate, whatever its suggestive name. Then a correct proof establishes a result about that object, and no prior informal target needs to be recovered. But this does not license importing the consequences of another concept through the name. A new predicate with pointwise quantifiers may be perfectly legitimate; calling its satisfaction “uniform convergence” does not yield a common threshold. The need for correspondence reappears at any boundary where the result is advertised or used in that stronger sense.

These responses mark a limit to the thesis. It is not an account of all mathematical understanding. A person might know that a formal statement faithfully represents a theorem without understanding why the theorem holds, why it matters, or how to generalize it. Conversely, someone might understand the central proof idea while misreading a formal encoding. The relevant competence here is narrower: recognizing enough of the claim's structure to justify the proposed passage from one representation to another.

## The remaining question after success

Formal proof changes the distribution of mathematical uncertainty. It can make enormous chains of inference dependable without making every part of the surrounding announcement equally secure. Recognizing this achievement requires saying exactly which uncertainty has been removed.

An interpretive challenge should therefore be formulated as a mathematical difference with consequences. Does the threshold depend on the point? Does the result cover the advertised domain? Would the proposed representation admit a case the announced concept excludes? The answer may come from direct reading, a checked equivalence, a contrast, or justified testimony about such work. The method can vary. What cannot vary is the need for the answer to bear on the difference being challenged.

A checked proof deserves its authority. That authority does not spread automatically across every identification surrounding it. When a remaining doubt concerns which theorem was checked, another success matters only insofar as it helps answer that remaining question.
