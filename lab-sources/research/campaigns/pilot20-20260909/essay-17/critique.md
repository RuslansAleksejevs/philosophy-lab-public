# Critique of essay 17: When Blame Makes Answering Impossible

## Verdict and strongest reconstruction

Revise, preserving the central project. This is a clear and disciplined essay with a plausible narrow result: the fact that a culpable wrongdoer does not perform a demanded response is not sufficient evidence of a new culpable refusal when the response conditions block performance. A blamer must assess the subsequent alleged wrong under its own circumstances, including circumstances produced by accountability itself. The disjunction—provide a reasonable opportunity or forgo the unsupported additional charge—usefully avoids imposing rehabilitation or continued contact on victims.

Its best prospective contribution is the analysis of escalation: accountability can alter the evidential significance of the silence it subsequently condemns. The administrative example makes this intelligible. But the draft sometimes shifts from an evidential constraint to categorical conclusions about blameworthiness which the argument does not establish. Its most ambitious aggregate-demand conclusion also needs closer literature engagement and a more careful individuation of wrongs.

## 1. Separate missing evidence from absence of culpable refusal

The careful thesis says nonperformance “does not by itself establish” refusal. The committee example supports that thesis. Several later formulations go further: someone must provide opportunity or “abandon that additional charge”; the private-letter example says blocked submission cannot be a culpable failure; the conclusion refers to blocked absence not authenticating a charge. These can be read as excluding an otherwise established actual refusal whenever successful compliance was impossible.

Consider a member who receives a demand, decides out of contempt to refuse it, writes and sends “I will not cooperate,” and deliberately makes no attempt to answer. Unknown to everyone relevant, the portal is broken. There is an actual refusal expressed in actual conduct, not merely a prediction about what the person would do in a different world. The portal failure explains why successful submission was impossible but need not explain why the person decided not to answer or made no attempt. It is implausible that the discovery of the outage erases all grounds for the additional charge of refusal. The draft permits blame for an attitude, but has not shown that refusal itself must be reclassified as attitude rather than conduct. Refusing a demand and failing to complete its requested performance are different things.

A subtler version uses a contemporaneous private decision, not a sent message: the member deliberately discards a completed account because they reject accountability; the closed portal would independently have prevented receipt. The essay's claim to remain neutral about alternative possibilities now needs defending against preemption, rather than relying on a case where a hypothetical willingness to refuse is all that exists.

Precise repair: distinguish (a) nonreceipt/noncompletion, (b) failing to make reasonable efforts, (c) actual refusal or a culpable policy, and (d) objectionable attitudes. State the constraint as an undercutting of the inference from (a) to (b)/(c), with independent actual-conduct evidence still available. Replace the unqualified disjunction with “provide an adequate opportunity or forgo inferring culpable refusal from its absence,” unless the author intends to defend the stronger exclusion. This preserves the substantial result and the tracing discussion.

## 2. Aggregate impossibility does not settle how many wrongs occur

Section 4 correctly rejects treating jointly incompatible claims as if each existed alone. However, “that is different from a hundred independently culpable refusals” overstates what the arithmetic establishes. Suppose the agent can reasonably answer fifty claimants, refuses all one hundred out of equal contempt, and explicitly rejects every request. Each claimant can point to a feasible response they would have received under some reasonable schedule. The draft may be right that one should assess the agent's policy, but one policy can wrong many people. Policy-level explanation does not entail a single wrong or a single proper object of blame.

The strongest counterexample is discriminatory selection: the agent answers fifty privileged claimants and ignores fifty equally situated marginalized claimants. Total capacity is fully used. Nonetheless, the neglected claimants can each have a complaint about the unfair selection procedure even though the agent could not have answered everyone. Capacity limits do not distribute burdens fairly by themselves. Conversely, a fair lottery may leave fifty uncompensated claims without fifty culpable omissions.

Precise repair: say that overload defeats the inference from each unmet demand to each culpable refusal; it does not preclude multiple directed wrongs grounded in independently established contempt or unfair allocation. Define a reasonable response policy minimally through comparative urgency, equal standing, non-discrimination, and available common responses. These need not become a complete theory. Contrast fair selection with discriminatory selection to show what the policy test adds beyond arithmetic. Avoid counting blame as if the count of wrongful omissions follows from hours available.

## 3. Recognition conditions risk confusing answering with answering adequately

Section 3 rightly distinguishes forgiveness and acceptance from opportunity. But “an apology can count as an apology” and “a defeated explanation remains different from a refusal to explain” need a threshold. An offender can produce formulaic words as a deliberate substitute for answering: “I am sorry you are upset,” followed by a fabricated diversion. The victim may accurately describe this as refusing to explain despite an utterance that resembles an explanation. The alternative to counting every attempt as sincere is not necessarily closing the channel.

Specify the demanded response at the appropriate level: an account addressing the conduct, a sincere apology, or an admission supported by evidence may be what is owed. A token performance can be evidence of actual evasiveness. The relevant requirement should be that some reasonably attainable performance could satisfy the demand, with genuine disagreement about whether this token meets it. The current section can retain its point while dropping any suggestion that verbal form automatically precludes refusal.

## Significance and originality

The narrow inference rule is credible but close to familiar principles about evidence, omission, and opportunity. It is not yet clear how much of the essay is a new philosophical result rather than a valuable application. The strongest answer is to emphasize reflexivity: the assessor can create a circumstance which undercuts the assessor's subsequent inference. Distinguish inability generated by the accountability process from ordinary independent inability, without claiming a new moral exemption.

A focused independent search found a particularly relevant precedent: Robin Zheng, “Moral Criticism and Structural Injustice,” Mind 130(518) (2021):503–535, §5.1, https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/130/518/503/6129389 . Zheng considers individually justified claims whose aggregate exceeds a respondent's capacity, citing Kolers (2016), p.148. Her surrounding discussion distinguishes assessment from formative criticism. This means the aggregate-demand premise itself should not carry the originality claim. The author should read this section directly and explain the narrower additional claim about blame-generated response demands and escalation. I did not independently retrieve Kolers, and recommend citing Zheng directly unless the book is read. This search neither establishes priority of the exact thesis nor exhausts relevant literature.

## Readability and revision priorities

The opening identifies the question and stakes quickly. The committee example earns its place; there is no decorative scene-setting. “Feasible policy of response” is the main underexplained phrase and should receive the concrete fair/unfair allocation contrast above. “Authenticate” and “accusatory accuracy” add little over “support” and “accurate accusation.” Section 6 repeats several safeguards already established; some of that space could support the hard counterexamples.

Priorities: first, distinguish nonperformance from actual refusal and qualify the disjunction. Second, repair the aggregate claim and include fair versus discriminatory selection. Third, engage Zheng as a close precedent and specify the remaining contribution. Fourth, qualify the token-answer discussion. These changes can sharpen the result without restoring the initial, much broader agency-preservation thesis. This is one fresh critic's assessment, not a certification of truth, originality, independence, or eligibility.
