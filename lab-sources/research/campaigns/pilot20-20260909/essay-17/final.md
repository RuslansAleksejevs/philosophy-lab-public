# When Blame Makes Answering Impossible

A person who has done wrong can also wrong others by refusing to answer for it. Concealing what happened, dismissing a justified complaint, and declining a feasible act of repair may give victims further grounds for blame. Accountability can therefore accumulate: an original wrong becomes the occasion of a second wrong in the response to criticism. But that accumulation has a condition which blaming itself can defeat. Sometimes the manner in which an answer is demanded removes the opportunity to give one. If the resulting silence is then treated as another offense, the practice manufactures an appearance of additional culpability.

I defend a constraint on this escalation. When a response to blame is unavailable to its recipient, nonperformance does not by itself establish a culpable refusal. Someone who controls the conditions of response must either provide an adequate opportunity or forgo inferring culpable refusal from the missing performance. Independent evidence of an actual refusal may remain. This constraint leaves the original wrongdoing intact and requires neither forgiveness nor continued contact. Its special importance lies in the reflexivity of accountability: the person assessing a response can create the conditions that undermine its significance as evidence. Individually defensible demands can have the same effect when their accumulation makes compliance impossible.

## 1. The second charge

Consider a committee investigating a member who knowingly falsified a report. The evidence establishes the falsification; responsibility for it is not in doubt. The committee demands a written account by Friday. In one version, it gives the member the relevant documents and a functioning means of submitting an answer. The member is capable of answering but chooses to ignore the demand. In a second version, the committee locks the member out of the documents needed to identify the altered entries and closes the only accepted submission channel. The member tries unsuccessfully to submit an account. On Friday the committee adds a finding: the member has refused to cooperate.

The objection to the second finding is straightforward. Failure to supply the account does not establish refusal when the committee made supplying it impossible. This remains true even if the committee had excellent reasons for restricting access. It might have needed to prevent further falsification. Justified protection can make a particular form of cooperation unavailable without making the failure to perform it blameworthy. The committee must distinguish its reason for restricting access from its reason for interpreting the member's subsequent conduct.

This distinction becomes less obvious outside formal procedures. Someone may demand an immediate explanation while continuously interrupting it, insist on an acknowledgment while declaring every attempt insincere in advance, or require separate detailed responses to more accusations than the recipient can address. Emotional pressure can also impair answering, although the fact that a confrontation is painful does not establish that it is disabling. The administrative case isolates the structure without relying on uncertain psychological generalizations. An opportunity can be removed by the environment even while the person retains the general capacities of a responsible adult.

Three judgments must be kept separate. First, the person was responsible for the original wrong. Second, a demand for some response is legitimate. Third, the failure to provide this response on these terms is a fresh culpable wrong. Neither the first judgment nor its conjunction with the second entails the third. A legitimate demand can be implemented in a way that blocks its own satisfaction.

The relevant response must also be specified. An accused person might be unable to provide a detailed explanation but able to acknowledge receipt. Inability to make restitution immediately need not prevent proposing a schedule. Conversely, the ability to say something is not necessarily the ability to do what has been demanded. A committee requiring a documented account cannot establish noncooperation by observing that its subject could have uttered a sentence. The content and timing of the additional accusation must match the content and timing of an available response.

## 2. A limit within accountability

The proposal belongs near two established approaches to blame. McKenna treats directed blame through an analogy with a conversation, in which wrongful conduct, moral address, and an account occupy successive positions. He recognizes burdens to social relations, personal freedom, and emotional stability; he also explicitly treats desert as supplying defeasible rather than conclusive reasons to blame (2019, pp. 257, 264–267). My claim therefore does not challenge a theory according to which desert licenses every response. It identifies a particular error in moving from one position in the exchange to the next.

McGeer's account of blame emphasizes institutions that support its constructive features, and her account of scaffolding agency presents reactive practices as sustaining responsible capacities (2013, introduction; 2019, abstract). The general thought that blame should take agency seriously is accordingly not new. The narrower issue here is the warrant for an additional accusation when the blaming process has altered the conditions under which a response could occur.

The argument rests on four claims. Noncompletion, failure to make a reasonable effort, actual refusal, and an objectionable attitude are different objects of assessment. An inference from the first to the others must be sensitive to circumstances that make completion unavailable irrespective of effort or willingness. The effects of the blaming process belong among those circumstances. And responsibility for the original wrong supplies no automatic answer to this new question of attribution. Together these claims prohibit treating noncompletion as sufficient grounds for escalation when accountability itself blocks completion.

Why accept the second claim? If every attempt to submit an account is blocked, nonreceipt occurs whether the person tries to comply or declines to do so. It cannot discriminate between these possibilities. Other conduct might reveal defiance, but the bare failure cannot. This is an undercutting point: it removes the alleged support that one observation gives a conclusion, without establishing that the conclusion is false. In the absence of such a check, a procedure can produce what looks like corroboration of its accusations even when the accused does everything reasonably required.

Suppose, however, that the member sends an explicit contemptuous refusal before discovering that the submission portal is broken. The outage does not erase that refusal. Or suppose the member deliberately destroys a completed account to prevent the committee receiving it, although the broken portal would independently have prevented receipt. There is actual conduct manifesting rejection of accountability. The committee can assess that conduct without pretending that nonreceipt establishes it. By contrast, a letter expressing only what the member would do in a hypothetical confrontation provides evidence of an attitude, not necessarily of a refusal that actually occurred. An accurate charge identifies which of these things has been established.

The proposal is therefore neutral about whether responsibility always requires alternative possibilities. It does not deny culpability whenever an independent obstacle would have prevented success. It denies that the absence of success supplies the missing evidence of culpable agency. This makes the result narrower than a general opportunity condition on blameworthiness: its immediate subject is the justification of an accusation based on the response to blame.

## 3. What providing an opportunity requires

An adequate response opportunity has three elements. The recipient needs a sufficiently intelligible specification of what is being requested, a feasible means and period for responding, and conditions under which some reasonably attainable performance could satisfy the demand. An opportunity to apologize does not include a guarantee of forgiveness, belief, or restored trust. Nor does it make the words “I apologize” sufficient. A legitimate demand may require a sincere acknowledgment of specified conduct, rather than a formula selected to end the conversation.

A victim may reasonably find a purported apology evasive and treat it as evidence of refusal. A fabricated diversion can be a way of refusing to explain, even though words have been uttered. The distinction is between a deficient attempt to answer and an evasion disguised as answering; verbal form alone settles neither. Conversely, a candid explanation may fail to justify the wrong without being a refusal to explain. The recognition condition requires openness to an attainable adequate answer, not acceptance of every offered answer. A critic who dismisses a particular response on substantive grounds differs from one whose criteria exclude all possible responses in advance.

The obligation I propose has a disjunctive form: make an adequate opportunity available or do not infer culpable refusal from the missing performance. This does not license the inference whenever opportunity exists; ignorance, misunderstanding, and other explanations may still matter. A victim who needs to end contact may choose the second option. They can retain justified anger, describe the original wrong, warn others, and maintain protective boundaries. They need not make themselves available for an encounter they cannot bear merely to preserve the wrongdoer's agency.

Nor must every blamer personally organize a process. Often an answer through a mediator or an existing channel is enough. Where the person pressing the charge has no control over the blockage, the practical implication may simply be restraint in making that charge. What requires justification is the inference that the wrongdoer's missing response manifests disregard. A victim who declines to help does not thereby acquire a duty to resume contact, but neither does closing contact provide evidence that the wrongdoer refused it.

This qualification matters because blame can already burden those wronged. Brunning and Milam distinguish letting go from forgiving, and emphasize both the risks of abandoning blame and the unequal burdens that social position places on those who blame (2023, §§6.1–6.2). An agency-preserving account should not quietly turn these burdens into an obligation to provide indefinite moral care. Its demand here is instead one of accusatory accuracy. People may cease a conversation without either forgiving or describing the foreclosed answer as a new refusal.

## 4. When no individual closes the channel

The aggregate limitation has an important precedent. Zheng explicitly considers individually justified claims whose total exceeds a respondent's capacity, attributing the point to Kolers. She places it within a distinction between criticism that assesses agency and criticism that seeks to improve it (2021, §5.1). My claim is not to discover the limitation. It concerns what happens when accountability generates that limitation and then treats unmet demands as fresh evidence of disregard.

Consider a collective case. Imagine one hundred people each entitled to ask a wrongdoer for a detailed account of a common incident. Each asks for twenty minutes of preparation and insists on receiving an individual answer within the next day. Each demand, considered in isolation, might be reasonable. Their total requires more than thirty-three hours before allowing for any other activity. Even a perfectly willing respondent cannot comply with all of them.

This does not imply that each claimant has acted wrongly. A claimant may not know about the others. Nor does it follow that no answer is owed. It follows that each unmet demand cannot be treated as evidence of culpable refusal merely because that demand was individually reasonable. The conditions of satisfying them are joint conditions. A set of permissions to ask is not automatically a set of jointly performable obligations. The arithmetic establishes overload, not how many wrongful refusals occurred.

The example presents a more difficult problem when the wrongdoer could have answered any particular claimant. Suppose the person can reasonably answer fifty, but explicitly rejects all one hundred requests out of contempt. There may be many directed wrongs here, despite the impossibility of universal compliance. A culpable policy can wrong many people; identifying one policy does not establish that only one wrong occurred. The aggregate constraint leaves those charges available when grounded in the rejection itself, rather than inferred from a list of missing answers.

Suppose instead that the person answers fifty requests and leaves fifty unanswered. In one version, the claims are equally urgent and a fair lottery determines whose detailed questions are answered, with a common account available to everyone. In another, the person deliberately answers only privileged claimants and excludes equally situated marginalized claimants. Capacity is fully used in both cases. Yet in the second, each excluded claimant can complain about discrimination. The defect lies in the selection, not in a failure to exceed the total capacity. The contrast shows why “I could not answer everyone” is not a sufficient defense and why “you did not answer me” is not a sufficient accusation.

A reasonable response policy must take comparative urgency, equal standing, and available common responses into account; it cannot allocate attention through irrelevant status distinctions. Sometimes a lottery is fair, sometimes priority to more urgent claims is required. No complete allocation theory follows here. The requirement is to justify the relevant selection in the actual field of claims. The evidence for a new wrong can then identify unfair allocation, deliberate dismissal, or avoidable failure of effort rather than treating every unmet demand as self-explanatory.

This is the contribution of the constraint beyond the simple blocked-channel case. Accountability is not merely directed at an independently fixed agent with independently fixed opportunities. It creates demands whose interaction can alter what responsible responding consists in. Assessing the response therefore requires a second assessment of opportunity, effort, and allocation after those demands have been made. The fact that the original conduct met the conditions for responsibility does not exempt the accountability process from this assessment.

A suitable correction need not silence the hundred claimants. They can all state their condemnation. What requires coordination is the further claim that each is owed a separate response within incompatible terms, and the interpretation of resulting omissions. A shared account, representative questions, or a revised period can preserve the content of accountability without treating finite responsiveness as proof of limitless defiance. These are examples of ways to satisfy the constraint, not universally required procedures.

## 5. Distress, manipulation, and inherited fault

A wrongdoer may attempt to exploit this view: “Your anger makes me unable to answer, so you must stop being angry.” That inference fails. A report of inability is evidence to assess, not an authority to dictate another person's emotions. The claim might be false. Even when true, it does not cancel responsibility for the original wrong. And the victim can decline further engagement rather than supplying the conditions for another conversation.

The difficult cases involve partial capacities and costly effort. Ordinary accountability is often unpleasant; embarrassment and reluctance do not equal inability. A person can be expected to incur some costs in answering for harm they caused. The proposed constraint cannot determine those costs without a substantive account of what answering is reasonably owed in the case. Its cleanest applications involve clear blockage, incompatible instructions, or demonstrably excessive combined demands. Psychological applications require further evidence. This limitation is preferable to importing an unsupported presumption that all harsh blame incapacitates or that all distressed silence expresses contempt.

An equally serious objection concerns responsibility for becoming unable to answer. The wrongdoer may destroy records precisely to prevent an investigation or deliberately render themselves unreachable. In such a case the failure to answer can be attributable to an earlier culpable act. But the inference must be supplied. One must establish the relevant responsibility for creating the inability, not merely repeat that the person committed the original offense.

The distinction is especially important where the original wrong predictably attracts blame. It may be foreseeable that falsifying a report will lead to an exhausting investigation. That does not make every inability arising during the investigation the wrongdoer's culpable creation. Otherwise any procedural obstruction could be traced back to the offense simply because no investigation would have occurred without it. A person who starts a conflict does not thereby become the morally responsible author of everything other agents subsequently do in it.

There can nonetheless be genuine inherited fault. If the falsifier destroys the only records while anticipating a justified demand for an account, then a later inability may be explained by a culpable attempt to avoid accountability. The committee may blame that attempt and its foreseeable consequences. The proposal excludes automatic inheritance, not tracing supported by an appropriate connection between an earlier choice and the present failure.

Finally, some blamers do not seek an answer at all. They aim to protest, affirm a victim's worth, or end a relationship. My constraint does not make their blame conceptually defective. It concerns the moment at which nonresponse is invoked as additional wrongdoing. An expression of condemnation need not offer a reply channel. But a person who deliberately closes every channel must not then appeal to the absence of a reply as though a channel remained open. An independently communicated refusal remains assessable on its own evidence.

## 6. What this establishes

The strongest version of an agency-preserving ideal would prohibit all blame that diminishes a person's capacity for responsible action. That goes beyond the argument defended here. Some protective measures are justified despite such effects; some victims need to withdraw despite foreseeable costs to offenders. A duty to maintain everyone's agency at all times would demand too much and would give too much control to those who threaten to become unresponsive.

The narrower result concerns warranted escalation. A person's past responsibility cannot supply missing grounds for attributing a subsequent failure to disregard. When accountability changes the conditions of answering, those conditions must enter the interpretation of nonresponse. Joint overload calls for assessment of effort and allocation, while leaving room for multiple wrongs established by actual contempt or discrimination. A blocked answer's absence cannot by itself support an additional accusation.

This is an application and development of familiar requirements of accurate attribution, not a new exemption from responsibility. Its contribution is to identify a recurring feedback error: accountability can make willing and unwilling respondents look alike, then treat the appearance it created as independent support for escalating its demands. Recognizing that error places an obligation on the assessor to inspect the process as well as the person. The appropriate correction is evidential restraint, renewed opportunity, or a more accurately specified charge; which correction is available depends on the case.

These conclusions do not dissolve blame into a technique for producing better behavior. They apply even when the prospect of moral improvement is poor. Nor do they require a victim to relinquish justified resentment. They require the practice of accountability to distinguish the responsibility it discovers from the nonperformance it produces. Without that distinction, demanding an answer can become a way of ensuring that there will always be something further to blame.
