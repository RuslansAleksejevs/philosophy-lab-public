# Keeping the Debt: The Limits of Historical Reasons

Can someone stop having a moral reason by deliberately ceasing to care? The question is not whether she can stop feeling its force. People become indifferent, cultivate callousness, and sometimes welcome the loss of a scruple. The question is whether changing the psychology through which a requirement once moved her can remove its present authority.

A tempting argument against desire-based accounts is that an agent could otherwise escape morality by altering her desires. But that argument moves too quickly. An impermissible act can successfully change what someone subsequently ought to do. Destroying a borrowed instrument can make returning that instrument impossible, even while making compensation obligatory. Moreover, the person might retain blame for engineering her indifference even if she no longer has the original reason. Neither the badness of the change nor the survival of blame establishes the persistence of a reason.

I develop a test that initially seems to favor a historical solution: an agent acquires a reason through a concern, undertakes an obligation, and subsequently loses the concern while the obligation remains. But the solution faces a problem. A second agent could assume an equally valid obligation without ever having the relevant concern. If the continuing claim supplies both agents with a reason, prior concern is unnecessary. If only the formerly concerned agent has a reason, history creates an asymmetry that the continuing claim cannot explain. If neither has a reason, history has not preserved practical authority.

The result is a limited negative argument. Motivational history can distinguish culpability and explain how an obligation was undertaken, but neither function by itself establishes a distinctive mechanism for retaining its practical reason. The promising distinction between conditions of entry and exit does not settle whether motivation is an enduring condition of having the reason. It relocates the familiar dispute about moral authority unless an independent argument explains why an earlier concern should confer authority now.

## 1. The view under pressure

Bernard Williams’s opening distinction concerns two interpretations of claims that an agent has a reason to act. On the internal interpretation, an appropriate connection with the agent’s motives is required; an external interpretation lacks that condition (Williams 1981, opening passage). The relevant contrast should not be caricatured as one between doing whatever one currently feels like and obeying morality. A motivation-based theory may consider information, deliberation, commitments, and dispositions far beyond a passing appetite.

My target is the following restricted thesis, which I call present-base dependence:

> An agent has a practical reason to perform an action only if an appropriate route of sound deliberation from her present motivational set can support motivation toward that action.

This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient condition and not a theory of how strong reasons are. It permits unnoticed reasons and motivational conflict. A presently unmotivated debtor can still have a reason to repay if sound deliberation from her existing concerns could move her to do so. To test the thesis we therefore need more than distraction, weakness of will, or a momentary absence of feeling.

There is already a substantial problem here. Kent Hurtig considers whether an agent temporarily deprived of motivation retains reasons associated with his ordinary concerns. His examples include externally induced indifference to an opportunity; he also examines appeals to earlier motivations (Hurtig 2006, author manuscript, §V). The possibility of deleting an inconvenient concern is also not new. Peter Suber explicitly considers a self-modifying agent removing a second-order desire that produces guilt or hesitation (Suber 2002, §5).

An especially close precedent is Searle’s example of ordering and drinking a beer, then finding no desire that favors paying. He argues that undertaking the commitment establishes a desire-independent reason and treats that connection as internal to the notion of commitment (Searle 2001, point 8). The possibility that voluntary entry creates a reason independent of subsequent desire is therefore not new. The question here is what can be established without identifying commitment with practical reason at the outset. I keep an outstanding obligation, its bearer’s reason, and the history of her motives distinct, then examine the limits of connecting them.

## 2. An obligation with an open exit

Mara accepts temporary custody of Leila’s emergency fund. Leila will request its return on Friday. Mara understands this arrangement, sincerely cares about Leila and about being trustworthy, and recognizes a reason to return the money. Assume that there is no defect in the arrangement: no coercion, deception, unjust purpose, or conflicting emergency. The money remains available, and returning it requires only a simple transfer.

On Thursday Mara undertakes a precise psychological intervention. It eliminates every element of her motivational set from which sound deliberation could support returning this money: the relevant affection, concern for trustworthiness, aversion to wrongdoing, and any instrumental concern that would independently favor return. She retains factual memory, ordinary practical competence, and the ability to make the transfer. She can pursue other projects coherently. She does not mistake the money for her own. There is no threatened sanction, reputational advantage, or future reward that would supply a replacement route. On Friday Leila requests her money.

This is an idealized case. Its purpose is to isolate a proposed dependence, not to predict a medical technology or describe ordinary depression. It stipulates selective motivational alteration while preserving agency and the available action. If such alteration is impossible in principle because every agent necessarily has a route to honoring an entrusted claim, that is a substantive escape from the case. It requires an account of that route, rather than a reminder that actual people usually retain overlapping concerns.

Compare a second case. Before Friday, Leila freely and competently gives Mara the money, releasing her from the obligation to return it. There are no creditors or other affected parties. Mara then undergoes the same intervention. Her resulting motivational profile can be the same in both cases. Her information about release differs, but by hypothesis neither information state activates a relevant concern. The practical opportunity is also the same: she can transfer the money back.

In the release case, the special reason to return the money as something owed has ended. Other reasons to help Leila might remain; they are outside the comparison. In the alteration-only case, the special obligation remains; whether its associated practical reason remains is the contested issue. The money is still held on the original terms, the return is still possible, and Leila has done nothing to relinquish her claim. What has changed is Mara’s responsiveness to it.

The comparison establishes a difference in what is owed, but an internalist can accept that difference completely. In the release case there is no debt. In the alteration-only case there is a debt without a present internal reason to pay. Both cases lack a practical reason of the targeted kind, but they do not have the same normative status. Leila’s release affects her claim; Mara’s intervention affects a proposed motivational condition on reasons. No confusion between psychological power and normative power is necessary to this account.

Three events must therefore be distinguished. A claim can be extinguished by an authorized release. A reason can disappear because one of its enabling conditions disappears. Or responsiveness to an independently grounded reason can disappear while the reason remains. A person who loses interest in a picnic may lose a reason to attend without exercising any authority to cancel reasons. The fund example is decisive against present-base dependence only if motivation belongs to the third category rather than the second. The persistence of Leila’s claim does not alone establish that classification.

## 3. The bridge from obligation to reason

A defender of present-base dependence will ask whether this simply assumes that morality always supplies reasons. We should separate the steps.

First, the claim that the obligation persists has a relational justification. The arrangement enabled Mara to take custody without acquiring ownership. Treating her subsequent withdrawal of concern as terminating the obligation would make the continuation of Leila’s claim conditional on a decision wholly reserved to Mara. Yet this was not one of the terms on which custody was accepted. The purported exit would change the arrangement unilaterally while leaving Mara in possession of its benefit. There is therefore a direct explanation of why release matters and psychological alteration does not: release changes an authoritative term of the relationship; alteration changes neither the undertaking nor Leila’s control over the money.

This explanation is normative. It relies on the familiar judgment that an agent cannot cancel another person’s legitimate claim merely by changing her own attitude toward it. But it does not rely on the universal thesis that every moral consideration supplies a reason to every possible agent. Its application is to the undertaking: Mara cannot terminate it merely by eliminating her route of responsiveness while keeping its benefit and her ability to perform. The internalist countermodel grants precisely this much.

Second, a present obligation supplies a present practical reason only if we accept an additional bridge. Call this local moral rationalism: where an agent remains able to discharge a legitimate obligation of this kind, the fact that performance is still owed counts in favor of performing it. This bridge is modest in scope but indispensable. Nothing in the meaning of “moral obligation” alone forces every metaethical theory to accept it.

Ordinary creditor demands give some support to the bridge. Leila’s request on Friday concerns what Mara can now settle. Returning the money would discharge the outstanding claim, whereas explaining Thursday’s intervention would not. The request purports to offer Mara a consideration for deciding what to do: this is money she owes. Its practical point is not exhausted by classifying her conduct or recommending that others recover the fund. A theory that recognizes such requests as justified offers of considerations for the debtor’s deliberation has a reason to accept local moral rationalism.

But this is an argument from the best interpretation of a practice, not a deduction from the existence of the obligation. The internalist can preserve an applicable moral standard for Mara, her knowledge of it, and an appropriate moral assessment of her refusal. The theory can distinguish justified moral demands from reasons available to their addressee in its technical sense. It can explain the ordinary practice through the fact that most debtors retain some motivational route toward repayment. The exceptional debtor shows the limits of that explanation, not a contradiction within it.

I favor the rationalist interpretation because it explains why Leila can offer the very same consideration as a basis for repayment without first investigating Mara’s concerns. It treats the claim as making a practical difference to its bearer, rather than making that difference conditional on a further fact about her psychology. Nevertheless, this preference is defeasible. It does not establish that any justified moral demand necessarily supplies a practical reason, and the historical fact that Mara once cared adds no immediate support to the bridge.

The distinction also explains why merely preserving blame is insufficient to establish a present reason. Suppose Mara’s intervention was culpable on Thursday. A tracing account can attribute Friday’s refusal to that earlier culpable choice. But it answers a backward-looking question: why may Mara be blamed? It does not answer the present question: does she have any reason now to return the money? Mara could acknowledge that her earlier self acted badly and still maintain, under present-base dependence, that no current reason favors repayment. Leila’s outstanding claim challenges that second assertion independently of the first.

Indeed, the persistence argument does not ultimately require culpable self-alteration. If a third party produces exactly the same motivational result, Mara may be less blameworthy or blameless. Yet while she remains the same competent custodian, the money does not become hers. This variant helps identify the ground of persistence. It is the unextinguished relationship, not a penalty imposed for choosing the intervention. Culpability brought the problem into view; it does not solve it.

## 4. The limited trilemma

We can now state the conflict precisely. Grant that selective alteration leaves no relevant deliberative route from Mara’s present motivational set. Three claims cannot all survive:

1. Present-base dependence is true.
2. The legitimate, still-performable obligation remains after alteration alone.
3. Local moral rationalism is true of that obligation.

The second and third claims yield a present practical reason to return the money. The first, together with the stipulated motivational result, excludes that reason. Nothing contradictory follows merely from motivation changing over time. The contradiction arises only when a specific account of reasons meets a specific account of persistence and a specific bridge between obligation and reason.

The first response is to deny persistence. Perhaps the obligation was itself conditional on Mara’s continuing concern. But why should the terms of custody include that condition? Mara’s original concern may explain why she undertook the arrangement or why the undertaking initially supplied her with a reason. Neither fact shows that Leila acquired only a claim revocable by motivational withdrawal. This blocks an attempt to make the obligation itself continuously conditional on concern. It does not block the internalist’s different claim that obligation and motivation are jointly necessary for a practical reason at every time. That is an application of present-base dependence, not an illicit inference from entry to exit.

A second response preserves the obligation while rejecting local moral rationalism. Mara still morally owes the money but has no reason to return it. This answer should not be dismissed as incomprehensible. It gives morality a critical standpoint that need not supply reasons to every person it criticizes. Its cost is also exact: even someone who voluntarily entered a legitimate arrangement with a recognized practical reason can retain its obligation while losing every present reason to comply. The moral standard still applies to her; what disappears is its status as a consideration counting in favor of her present action in the sense under dispute.

A third response denies complete deletion. There may be universal concerns or constraints of rational agency that secure a route from any competent motivational set to repayment. But formal consistency alone will not identify Leila as the person to whom money must be returned. A route must contain enough substantive material to distinguish returning entrusted funds from pursuing Mara’s unrelated projects. If that material is essential to agency, the theory owes an argument for its necessity. If it consists in a concern that normal people happen to retain, it leaves the stipulated case unanswered.

Finally, a theorist can reject present-base dependence while retaining a historical connection to motivation. Mara’s earlier concerns helped establish a reason that remains until the underlying relationship is appropriately terminated. This hypothesis deserves examination, but it must do more than rename the survival of the obligation. The formal incompatibility above identifies the choices. It does not yet vindicate this fourth response, which rejects the first premise rather than the deletion assumption or either normative premise.

## 5. The custodian who never cared

Introduce Nara. She accepts custody on exactly the same valid terms, but solely to obtain access to the fund. She understands what she is undertaking and intentionally undertakes it; she has no concern from which sound deliberation could support return. Her ulterior motive does not invalidate Leila’s claim. On Friday, Nara and the altered Mara have the same relevant present motivational limitations, knowledge, general competence, and ability to transfer the money. Each has an equally valid outstanding obligation. Their difference is that Mara once possessed a relevant concern and Nara did not.

For this comparison, the relevant reason is the consideration that the money is owed, rather than every conceivable additional reason these agents may possess. Mara might have special reasons of self-repair, and her earlier decisions might affect responsibility. Those possibilities do not yet explain why the creditor’s claim counts in favor of return for one custodian but not the other.

A historical repair now faces three possibilities. If the claim supplies both custodians with a present reason, a prior motivational connection is unnecessary for this kind of reason. The repair may be an excellent account of obligation, but it has not preserved motivation as an essential historical condition. If the claim supplies neither with a present reason, motivational history has failed to retain the reason. If it supplies a reason only for Mara, the theory must justify why identical legitimate claims and current capacities differ in practical authority because their bearers had different earlier concerns.

The third answer is not formally inconsistent. Reasons often depend on history: promises, harms, and benefits can make present differences. The objection is that the relevant histories of undertaking, harm to the creditor, and entrusted benefit have been held fixed. A previous concern must now be shown to have a further normative effect beyond explaining why Mara initially saw a reason to comply. Merely pointing to the earlier reason restates the proposed retention rule. It does not defend it.

One possible defense appeals to self-government: a person’s deliberate adoption of a policy can bind her future deliberation. But accepting custody and adopting a policy are different events. Mara’s concern does not necessarily amount to a policy to preserve that concern, and Nara too deliberately undertakes the same return condition. If all deliberate undertakings have the relevant binding force, Nara again has the reason. If only undertakings endorsed by an antecedent concern do, the historical theorist must explain the exclusion. An account of policy authority might supply that explanation, but the persistence of Leila’s claim cannot substitute for it.

A second defense appeals to the wrongfulness of deleting the concern. Perhaps an agent cannot rid herself of a reason by wrongfully destroying its basis. This is too broad: wrongdoing can eliminate an option or change the facts that made an action appropriate. More specifically, tracing culpability to an earlier choice does not provide the missing present reason. And if a third party erases Mara’s concern without her consent, the claim still persists. A repair restricted to wrongful self-alteration would therefore protect the reason in one case while leaving the other unexplained.

The comparison also rules out an indiscriminate appeal to past desires. Mara’s abandoned interest in flute playing need not continue to supply a reason to practice. To distinguish the fund from the flute, we can point to Leila’s legitimate claim. But that distinction equally favors Nara’s obligation. The very relationship that makes the historical repair selective threatens to make earlier motivation unnecessary.

The conclusion is deliberately limited. Motivational history may ground additional reasons under a defended theory of self-government, personal identity, or commitment. I have not shown that no such theory is possible. I have shown why the surviving interpersonal claim does not itself provide the missing defense. Relative to that claim, the historical repair either extends practical authority beyond prior concern, fails to preserve it, or introduces an asymmetry requiring another normative argument. The apparent middle position between current motivational dependence and desire-independent obligation is not secured by reason retention alone.

## 6. Could Mara act for the reason?

There remains an explanatory challenge. A theory of reasons may require that normative reasons could figure in an explanation of acting for them. It is not enough to show that Mara can perform the same bodily movements for a reward. Acting because she owes the money is different from acting because someone pays her to transfer it. An internalist can reasonably insist on this distinction without defining moral responsiveness into all agency.

Present-base dependence specifies a particularly demanding connection: sound deliberation from the agent’s current motivational resources must be able to support the relevant motivation. A surviving reason after complete deletion would violate that condition. The fact that the earlier Mara could act for the claim does not establish that the present Mara can do so. Thus the historical repair inherits the motivational explanation problem rather than automatically solving it.

One weaker condition permits non-deliberative restoration of a concern, followed by deliberation in which the claim explains repayment. On this view the reason can remain while its accessibility is impaired. The restoration need not be the reason for payment; it may enable the agent to respond to the claim. But this weaker possibility is also available to Nara if she can acquire the concern through education or another psychological change. The explanatory requirement then supplies no historical asymmetry. Restricting the admissible change to restoration of a formerly possessed concern, excluding first acquisition, requires justification beyond the mere possibility of acting for a reason.

The negative argument here does not require choosing between these possibility conditions. It requires making their consequences explicit. If the current deliberative condition is retained, the proposed historical reason fails it. If a broader capacity after psychological change is enough, prior possession of the concern may cease to matter. The rationalist position I favor accepts the latter kind of possibility in these cases, while recognizing that this relaxes the internalist’s constraint rather than satisfying it on its own terms. Neither account is established simply by demonstrating that the transfer remains physically possible.

This does not authorize forced restoration of concern. Suber separates respect for self-modification from the protection of unconsenting others (2002, §§4–6). An agent might have authority over her mental life without thereby acquiring ownership of entrusted money. Whether another person may forcibly alter her psychology requires an additional argument, whichever account of her reasons is correct.

## 7. What the retention test establishes

The retention question is useful precisely because its initial attraction can mislead. The altered custodian still owes the money; the creditor has not released her; and earlier culpability does not discharge the debt. None of these propositions by itself establishes a present practical reason. Searle’s stronger commitment account supplies that connection directly. An internalist can deny it while consistently preserving the obligation. A historical solution must explain why the connection survives for an agent who once cared and whether it also holds for an otherwise comparable agent who never did.

The result is not a new refutation of all motivational theories. It is a constraint on a proposed repair: the history of responsiveness cannot do the work of a theory of normative authority merely because the obligation extends through time. Comparing Mara with Nara separates the continuing claim from the earlier concern; comparing both with a released custodian separates that claim from a current obligation-free opportunity to pay. Together the comparisons reveal exactly where a further argument is needed. We can explain who still owes without yet explaining why what is owed supplies its bearer with a practical reason. To bridge that gap, the past fact that she once cared is not enough.
