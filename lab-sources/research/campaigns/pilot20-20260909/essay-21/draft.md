# Recovery Without Vindication: Changing Desires and the Comparison That Matters

A person can cease wanting what she has lost and build a life she sincerely enjoys. That development can be good for her. It does not follow that the loss did her no harm, that she has been fully compensated, or that bringing about the loss together with the recovery was a good way to help her. These are different claims, but discussions of adaptive desires easily move between them.

The difficulty is especially pressing for desire theories of well-being. If welfare depends on getting what one wants, why not improve a person's life by making her want what remains available? One response distrusts the new desires. Another accepts them and seems to vindicate the deprivation. Both responses move too quickly. We can give new desires their full prudential importance while refusing to let the comparison that establishes the value of recovery settle the value of the package that made recovery necessary.

I defend a constraint on that comparison. When the goods acquired through recovery could also have been provided without the loss, a prospective assessment must consider that alternative. Call this the **portable recovery constraint**. Its force is conditional: it applies only where the relevant enrichment really is transferable. It does not show that all adaptive preferences are defective, or even that adaptation cannot fully compensate a loss. It shows why accepting their value need not remove a wholly prudential objection to policies that create losses and then change what people want.

## Three questions about a changed life

Consider a community choir whose rehearsal venue becomes unavailable for a year. Mira loves singing there. During the first month of closure she misses valued rehearsals and experiences substantial disappointment. She then joins a ceramics group, develops a strong interest in pottery, and eventually stops wanting to sing. Her enjoyment and fulfilment in the ceramics group are entirely genuine. There is no deception, coercion, or external judgment that singing is a superior activity.

Suppose the organization that controlled the venue could instead have kept it available and introduced her to ceramics. In that alternative she would have enjoyed the first month of singing and then voluntarily moved to pottery. Her later life, including her interests, would have been just as good. These stipulations identify a possible case; they are not empirical generalizations about how people respond to deprivation.

There are three questions. First, does joining ceramics benefit Mira after the closure? Compare the life with closure and ceramics against the life with closure but without ceramics. The answer may plainly be yes. Second, does her recovery compensate her for the closure? Compare her recovered life with the life she would have had if the closure had not occurred. That answer depends on what would actually have happened otherwise. Third, should the organization choose closure followed by ceramics as a way of promoting her welfare? If keeping the venue open while introducing ceramics is feasible at the same relevant cost, that is an additional comparison. On the stated facts, the loss is avoidable without sacrificing anything gained through recovery.

The third question is not answered by either of the first two. Indeed, suppose that without the organization's intervention Mira would never discover ceramics. The closure-and-ceramics package could leave her better off than the actual no-intervention history. It could therefore compensate the loss in an ordinary counterfactual sense. It would still be worse for her than an available intervention that offered ceramics without first closing the choir. A package can compensate a loss while including an unnecessary loss.

This matters because rejecting deprivation does not require rejecting recovery. The prudential reason against closure in this case comes from the better feasible alternative, not from declaring Mira's later flourishing illusory. Her statement that her life now goes well is perfectly compatible with that reason. Even her statement that the new life is better than the old one need not settle whether this was the best available route to it.

## What desire theory must and need not say

The background problem is familiar. Heathwood discusses changing tastes and considers concurrent desire satisfaction as a way to avoid imposing things people once wanted but no longer want (2006, §1.1, pp. 541–542). The relevant lesson is that an earlier desire need not govern what should now be provided. It would be strange to insist that Mira return to singing solely because she used to love it. Recognizing this does not require treating her missed first month as worthless.

A different familiar problem concerns the acquisition of desires. Dietz distinguishes proactive desire satisfactionism, on which acquiring new satisfied desires can benefit someone, from reactive approaches that resist this implication. His discussion also presses the importance of satisfying desires once acquired, and of a bystander's ensuring satisfaction of desires someone else will create (2023, §§2–3). Those issues reveal the cost of treating the origin of a desire as sufficient to exclude it from welfare. The present proposal takes no such exclusionary route. It allows ceramics to become a basic good for Mira and asks which complete histories should be compared.

Nothing in the argument requires the claim that all desire satisfaction is intrinsically good. A hedonist, an objective-list theorist, or a pluralist can apply the same comparison. But its application within a desire theory is especially revealing: subjectivism about what makes lives good does not entail indifference about how a person reaches a life with a new set of desires. Differences in missed satisfaction, frustration, and available routes can all matter on the theory's own terms.

The key distinction is between respecting old desires forever and counting what happened while they mattered. Suppose Mira genuinely enjoyed each of the first month's rehearsals in the no-closure history. The value of those episodes need not depend on her continuing to want singing years later. Similarly, a month of genuine frustration does not become a month of fulfilment when she changes her interests. Giving her current desires authority over present provision does not by itself give them authority to rewrite every earlier episode.

That claim needs a qualification. Some desires concern extended achievements or later events. Their satisfaction may not be settled when the desire ceases. Bruckner's abstract explicitly defends the possibility that later fulfilment of past desires affects past welfare (2013). My argument does not presuppose that every prudential fact about the past is settled immediately. The choir case instead specifies completed episodes: wanted rehearsals that did or did not occur, and frustration that was or was not experienced. No later fulfilment can turn a missed rehearsal into an attended one. A theory may still claim that later attitudes transform its value, but that is an additional holistic thesis, not a consequence of allowing tastes to change.

## The portable recovery constraint

The argument can be stated without numbers. Take two feasible interventions. In one, a person undergoes a loss and then receives an enrichment. In the other, she receives the same relevant enrichment without the loss. Suppose three conditions hold.

First, the better early period in the second history matters prudentially. There is genuinely more fulfilment or less frustration there, according to the theory under consideration. Second, the subsequent lives are equally good for the person: the enrichment has not been weakened, delayed, or made less authentic by removing the loss. Third, there is no further whole-life feature that reverses the advantage of the better early period. Under these conditions, an ordinary temporal dominance principle ranks the second history higher: an otherwise equally good life is better when one of its periods is better.

This is not a proof of a complete theory of welfare. The substantive work lies in identifying when the conditions hold and preventing a shift of comparison from hiding their relevance. The conclusion is nevertheless stronger than the claim that adaptation is sometimes incomplete. Even complete compensation relative to the history that would otherwise occur does not establish that the harmful route is prudentially justified. Its justification requires attention to alternatives for providing the purported compensation.

An illustrative numerical representation helps keep the questions apart. Let ordinary continuation have a welfare value of 10, unsupported closure a value of 4, closure followed by enrichment a value of 12, and equally feasible enrichment without closure a value of 15. These are hypothetical rankings expressed numerically, not measurements of actual welfare. Recovery contributes 8 relative to unsupported closure. The package exceeds ordinary continuation by 2. Yet choosing the package rather than enrichment without closure costs Mira 3. It is entirely consistent to affirm all three claims.

In particular, the 8-unit remedial improvement cannot simply be subtracted from a loss measured against some other alternative and then cited as proof that no better intervention was available. Each difference has its own comparison. Changes of desire do not abolish that discipline.

The constraint should not be converted into a rule that the best conceivable life is always the compensation baseline. There are two distinct assessments. Retrospective compensation may properly ask whether Mira is at least as well off as she would have been absent a particular action. Prospective prudence asks which feasible action best serves her. An enrichment that would not otherwise have occurred can count toward the first assessment even when it could have been separately provided. The availability of separate provision matters to the second assessment. Calling the resulting package objectionable need not entail denying that it compensated her in the first sense.

This separation blocks an attractive but mistaken argument: since introducing ceramics would have been beneficial anyway, it cannot compensate the closure. That is too strong. An independently valuable benefit can compensate a harm. What does not follow is that causing the harm was necessary, advantageous, or the best available choice.

## Why portability cannot be assumed

The obvious objection is that the two histories are unrealistically easy to match. Perhaps losing the choir is exactly what enables Mira to care deeply about ceramics. Had singing remained available, she would have declined the invitation. Perhaps the process of overcoming disappointment is itself meaningful to her. Perhaps time spent singing would crowd out the social relationships that make ceramics valuable. Then the enrichment is not portable in the required sense.

This objection marks the boundary of the argument. Practical accessibility is stronger than logical possibility. An organization cannot assume it can install the same later fulfilment by offering the same class. Nor can an evaluator invent a psychologically impossible combination of every benefit and no cost. The no-loss alternative must include a credible route by which this person develops the relevant interests, with its time, resources, and risks counted.

But the objection also yields a useful burden on a proposed justification. If a loss is defended because it enabled adaptation, we should ask what exactly made the loss necessary. Did it supply an indispensable experience, remove a genuine obstacle, or merely happen to precede access to a previously neglected opportunity? These are different causal structures. In the last structure, the loss receives credit for an enrichment that could have been supplied directly. A story about personal growth does not settle which structure obtains.

This does not assign a universal presumption that loss is unnecessary. It identifies evidence needed when its alleged indispensability is doing justificatory work. If ceramics was already attractive to Mira and a compatible schedule was available, the portable alternative is plausible. If her new interests depend on a deep transformation inseparable from the loss, the comparison must be revised. The value of adaptation is compatible with both structures and so cannot discriminate between them by itself.

Nor is this a requirement that Mira herself wish she had taken the alternative route. A person's retrospective endorsement is important evidence. It may also contribute to welfare if she wants her life to have a certain history. If she has a powerful, satisfied desire specifically to have overcome the closure, the two later lives are no longer equal in the stipulated respects. That is a reason to revisit the case, not to suppress the endorsement. But simply preferring her current activity to her former one does not establish a preference for having been deprived of the former activity before making the change.

## Two further objections

One might object that temporal dominance neglects the shape of a life. A life of overcoming adversity may be better than an otherwise richer life lacking that development. The argument does not assume unrestricted addition across time. Its third condition explicitly excludes a compensating whole-life difference. Where the closure contributes meaning, coherence, or achievement, those are candidate benefits to be assessed. What would be question-begging is to infer such a benefit merely from the fact that the person recovered. Recovery must not serve simultaneously as the evidence that later life goes well and as unexplained proof that its painful route improved the whole.

The example can also be simplified to weaken this objection. Suppose the closure causes a short interval of bored disappointment that Mira never regards as transformative. The ceramics invitation had already been arranged, and she would have accepted it on the same date whether the choir remained open or not. In both histories she later evaluates her development in the same way. A theorist can deny that the lost rehearsals matter even here. But such a denial now needs a substantive account of why an otherwise unredeemed improvement in a lived period has no value. It cannot rest merely on the familiar fact that people move on.

A second objection is that the result is just opportunity-cost reasoning. It is. The contribution is not the discovery of opportunity cost, or a new intrinsic good. It is the identification of a misplaced theoretical demand in the adaptive-desire debate. To preserve an objection to deprivation, a desire theorist need not first discover a criterion that disqualifies the desires acquired afterward. An objection can survive while those desires count fully, because the favorable remedial comparison and the unfavorable prospective comparison answer different questions.

This shifts where disagreement must be located. If the no-loss enrichment alternative is rejected, the dispute may concern feasibility. If it is feasible but not better, the dispute may concern the temporal or holistic structure of welfare. If it is better but the loss is still permissible, the dispute may concern costs to others or nonprudential reasons. None of these disputes is settled by calling the new desires adaptive. The label by itself provides too little information.

## What recovery establishes

The account leaves many outcomes open. Adaptation can improve a damaged life without fully compensating its loss. It can fully compensate the loss while leaving a better prospective intervention available. Where its central goods really require the loss, adaptation can contribute to a successful prudential defense of the entire package. And other reasons may justify a package that is not best for the affected person. The argument supplies no general verdict on legal compensation, distributive entitlements, or the all-things-considered permissibility of interventions.

Its central conclusion is narrower and usable. A person's new satisfactions should not be discounted merely because they arose after deprivation. Equally, those satisfactions should not be credited to deprivation without comparing feasible ways of providing them. To ask whether a changed desire benefits someone is to begin the assessment, not to complete it. The relevant prudential question is often not whether she has recovered, but whether she could have acquired what makes the recovered life good without first losing something else.
