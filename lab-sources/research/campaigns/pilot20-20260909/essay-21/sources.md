# Sources and contribution boundary

Primary sources actually consulted and precise coverage are recorded in sources/retrieval-notes.md. Dietz (2023), sections 1–3 and 4.1–4.6 (partial), is the main argumentative interlocutor; Heathwood (2006), opening and section 1.1, supplies the changing-desire background. Bruckner (2013) was accessed only as an author abstract and is treated explicitly as such.

The provisional proposal has narrowed. It does not deny that new desires or adaptation can improve welfare. It distinguishes (i) improvement relative to unassisted loss, (ii) compensation relative to the actual no-loss history, and (iii) prudential justification of a loss-plus-recovery package against a feasible no-loss-plus-enrichment alternative. The last comparison must allow transferable new goods on both branches. This supports a conditional dominance argument without treating old desires as permanently authoritative or dismissing new desires as inauthentic. It does not establish that adaptation never compensates, that comparative harm fixes legal entitlements, or that every recovery process is portable.

Novelty remains uncertain: the contribution is a proposed structured use of counterfactuals in a familiar debate. Nearby work already distinguishes manufacturing satisfaction from satisfying desires. Broader compensation literature was located but not read; its significance is an explicit limitation.

Source scope: web-visible primary portions, bibliographic metadata and compliant paraphrase notes retained. No full-text reading of unavailable material, empirical verification, or exhaustive novelty survey claimed.
