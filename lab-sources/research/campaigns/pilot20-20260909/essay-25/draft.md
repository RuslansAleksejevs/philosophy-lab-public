# Who May Change What Counts as Good Work?

A tool can carry out our instructions perfectly while weakening our authority over what we are doing. This possibility is easiest to miss when the tool genuinely improves performance. Fewer errors, lower costs and easier operation seem to settle the question in its favor. Yet these improvements are measured against some specification of success. Sometimes making an artifact is also how we discover that the specification was inadequate. A technology that improves execution while closing this discovery process may transfer authority without transferring the visible appearance of authorship.

My claim concerns this transfer, rather than automation or lost manual skill generally. When a person retains authority to determine what counts as successful work, the arrangement should preserve a feasible route through which experience can lead that person to revise the specification. A facility for correcting outputs against an accepted standard does not by itself provide such a route. Nor does a right to appeal if appeals require the user already to possess an articulated alternative. What may be missing is the opportunity to develop the grounds of disagreement.

This is a restricted claim. Many tools properly execute settled tasks; no one needs to rediscover what a clean plate is whenever using a dishwasher. Some authority can also be delegated. The issue arises when an arrangement purports to leave someone in charge of a purpose while making the operative account of that purpose practically immune to that person's inquiry.

## Two kinds of correction

Consider a hypothetical workshop restoring a table. Its owner asks for a replacement joint that will hold firmly but permit later disassembly. An automated tool produces joints according to a menu of tested specifications. It is highly reliable, and the craftsperson can reject any output that fails the selected specification. This is substantial control. It is not a sham simply because machines perform the cutting.

Now compare two versions of the workshop. In the first, the craftsperson can use scrap material to vary a joint, feel how it behaves, attempt disassembly and revise the plan. The machine can perform every cut in these trials. In the second, the system permits trials only if the craftsperson first states a recognized defect in its proposed specification. It supplies comprehensive assurances that the standard joints satisfy the available categories. Both systems expose execution errors equally well. Neither manipulates the craftsperson or conceals its rules.

Suppose the craftsperson initially cannot say whether the desired joint should prioritize resistance to repeated movement, ease of release, or preservation of fragile surrounding wood. These are intelligible concerns, but their relevant relationship is unsettled. Handling a trial joint may reveal that the initial idea of “easy disassembly” is mistaken: an apparently convenient release motion threatens the surrounding material. Discovering the problem and discovering a better description of the task are parts of the same practical inquiry.

The second workshop has not necessarily produced a worse joint. Its menu may happen to contain the best solution. The difference is that it makes the craftsperson's authority conditional on already having formulated what the inquiry would help formulate. A request for exploratory variation is rejected because it cannot yet name the defect whose discovery would justify the variation. The person remains able to choose among approved descriptions, but cannot effectively take responsibility for whether those descriptions exhaust the relevant possibilities.

Call the first activity correction of execution and the second correction of specification. Execution correction asks whether the artifact satisfies a given account of success. Specification correction asks whether that account captures what the activity should achieve. The distinction concerns the role of a standard in a particular inquiry, not two permanently separate layers. A specification can become a settled instruction later; an apparent execution error can expose a defect in the specification now.

This distinction also avoids identifying specification correction with unlimited preference satisfaction. Fragile wood may make the original aspiration impossible. A maker can learn that a purpose must be modified or abandoned. Authority over a project includes being answerable to such resistance; it is not the power to declare every favored outcome successful.

## Why a chance to inquire matters

The argument for preserving specification correction has four steps.

First, authority over a purpose includes a limited jurisdiction to decide what considerations bear on its realization. A craftsperson hired only to cut a supplied pattern lacks authority to redesign the entire table. A craftsperson engaged to determine how to restore it has a different role. Calling both people “users” obscures this difference.

Second, exercising this jurisdiction may require discovering the relevant considerations through action. Not every reason arrives as a proposition available before making begins. This need not involve mysterious inexpressible knowledge. The reason can become perfectly expressible after a trial: “This release motion tears the old wood.” What was unavailable beforehand was the warranted identification of that reason as pertinent to this project.

Third, if access to the necessary exploratory activity depends on presenting the reason it would reveal, formal permission to revise the purpose is ineffective in those cases. This is not merely a shortage of convenience. The arrangement permits the end while systematically withholding a necessary means of exercising the relevant authority. The same circularity would arise if a researcher could obtain a diagnostic observation only after specifying what the observation would discover.

Fourth, an arrangement cannot preserve a person's practical authority merely by recognizing decisions the person would make after an inquiry the arrangement blocks. If the project is still presented as theirs to direct, preserving that authority supplies a reason to provide a route for inquiry, or else openly narrow their role. The route need not guarantee success. It must give the person a reasonable opportunity to acquire and act on reasons concerning the specification.

The conclusion is a norm of consistency between an assigned role and the means to exercise it. It does not establish an unrestricted right to equipment, endless experimentation or control over other people's projects. It identifies a defect in an arrangement that attributes a jurisdiction while defeating its exercise. Other considerations can justify the restriction. They do not make the authority fully preserved.

Skilled making is instructive here because the artifact is simultaneously a product and an instrument of inquiry. Sennett describes ways of working with resistance that include recasting a problem, adjusting one's approach and finding a tractable point of entry. His treatment supports the modest point that skill can change how a problem is understood, rather than merely improve execution within its initial terms (2008, chapter 8, pp. 219–222). The philosophical claim does not depend on privileging handwork. A simulation, a machine-cut prototype or a conversation with another maker can perform the relevant epistemic work.

## What existing accounts already establish

The need to support effective human intervention is well established. Bainbridge showed how automation can remove the ordinary practice through which operators maintain abilities required for exceptional conditions. A nominal ability to take over can therefore coexist with an inability to do so effectively (1983, sections 1.1 and 2). This explains one way a correction route deteriorates over time. It also cautions against merely adding an override switch.

Meaningful human control is likewise not reducible to continuous manual operation. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven distinguish responsiveness to relevant human moral reasons from tracing system behavior to appropriately situated human agents. They explicitly allow that the reasons tracked need not always be the immediate operator's, and discuss responsibilities distributed upstream (2018, “Tracking” and “Tracing”). My argument therefore cannot claim as new either distributed control or the insufficiency of immediate causal intervention.

Contestability research is closer still. Alfrink and colleagues include intervention throughout a system's life, scrutiny of its broader justification, effective authority to change decisions and accessible collective review (2023, sections 2 and 6.1). Veluwenkamp and Buijsman's operator contestability directly connects accountability with access to relevant reasons, cognitive capacity and authority to act on challenges (2025, abstract and sections 1–2). These are substantial precedents for the initial intuition that users need more than apparent choice.

The narrower proposal here concerns a failure that can survive mechanisms aimed at correcting already identifiable mistakes. A system may respond fairly to every articulated challenge, expose its existing rules and competently enforce the chosen standards, yet block the activities through which users could discover that a different challenge should be made. The workshop's problem is not that its review board rejects the craftsperson's evidence. It is that the prerequisite for obtaining evidence is possession of that evidence in advance.

This is compatible with an expansive understanding of contestability. Indeed, a sufficiently rich account should include it. The contribution is a discrimination within that broader ideal: responsiveness to presented reasons is different from preserving feasible opportunities to develop relevant reasons. Their difference supplies a test for arrangements that appear highly responsive because every admissible complaint receives careful treatment. We must also ask how a person can come to have a complaint that does not already fit the available categories.

## Delegation, risk and the value of ease

An immediate objection is that the craftsperson can simply delegate specification to the system's designers. We delegate such matters constantly and often wisely. The objection succeeds against any requirement that every user personally investigate every standard. It does not defeat the distinction between delegated execution and delegated authority.

Suppose the craftsperson knowingly commissions a standard joint and accepts the supplier's judgment about its suitability. That may be an excellent arrangement. The resulting loss of personal specification control is part of the service, not a concealed defect. By contrast, suppose the workshop continues to designate the craftsperson as the person who determines what restoration requires while allowing only choices within standards fixed elsewhere. The promise and the effective allocation of authority now diverge. Transparency about menu constraints helps, but does not alone repair the divergence if the role still demands judgment beyond them.

Delegation can also preserve a route for inquiry. The craftsperson might bring an unsettled concern to a specialist who can arrange trials, examine the existing wood and help refine the requirement. The specialist need not obey every request. What matters is that the concern can initiate an inquiry without already being a completed technical objection. This is materially different from a support channel that responds only with proof that the standard specification was followed.

A second objection concerns safety and cost. Exploratory variation is sometimes dangerous; reliable standardized components may be preferable. Nothing in the argument requires experimental joints to be used in occupied structures or speculative mechanisms to bypass safety interlocks. The relevant opportunity could be a small test, simulation, supervised prototype or review before deployment. If no safe and proportionate route exists, specification authority may properly rest elsewhere. The point is then to locate that authority honestly rather than preserve the operator's label while withdrawing its substance.

Nor must the entire cost of inquiry be borne by the provider. A request can reasonably be refused because it consumes disproportionate resources. Still, “you have not yet proved a defect” is a poor general rule for admitting exploratory work when defect discovery is precisely its function. A system can ration inquiry by cost, risk and plausibility without treating the current specification as the sole test of whether inquiry is warranted.

A third objection presses the value of ease. Most users do not want to become craftspeople. Removing requirements for skill often extends agency to people excluded by the demands of manual work. This is a decisive reason against equating autonomy with difficulty. The relevant question is whether the simplification removes labor or removes a route for the user's purpose to become better articulated. A system can simplify exploratory work as well as routine execution. In the first workshop, the machine may make variation cheap enough for a novice to discover possibilities previously accessible only to an expert. Such automation strengthens the kind of authority defended here.

There is also a difference between keeping an opportunity available and requiring its constant use. A maker need not prototype every joint to retain authority. Reliable delegation and ordinary trust are compatible with a standing route for reopening the specification when uncertainty arises. Conversely, a dormant button provides little if years of disuse, missing materials or prohibitive delays make it unusable. Feasibility concerns the actual arrangement over time, including training and access to others, rather than the interface alone.

## Is this only a matter of better outcomes?

Perhaps all that matters is whether inquiry improves the artifact. If the closed workshop reliably produces better joints, why object? This objection identifies a real limit: practical authority is not the only value, and it can conflict with product quality. A person may reasonably choose reliable results over personal direction. But the values remain distinct.

Imagine that both workshops produce the same joint on this occasion. In the open workshop the craftsperson could have discovered and acted on a conflict between release motion and preservation; in the closed one the relevant choice belongs to the menu's designers. Equal results do not make these allocations identical. The difference resembles the difference between accepting another person's judgment and having no effective role in deciding whether to accept it. The accepted proposal can be correct in both cases.

This does not imply that the closed workshop makes the person wholly unfree. They still choose materials, manage schedules and decide whether to undertake some projects. Authority is specific to questions and scopes. The claim is that success on those dimensions cannot compensate conceptually for absence of authority on another. An account that calls the user “in control” without specifying control over what will conceal the transfer.

The same point explains why blame is not the sole concern. The maker might work privately, bear no professional liability and produce nothing harmful. Even then the workshop can convert an activity of determining how something should be made into selection among predetermined acceptable results. That conversion may be welcome. It should not be mistaken for the same activity with less exertion. What has changed is who can help determine its standards through engagement with the artifact.

The practical test is therefore not whether a technology leaves a human at the end of the process. Ask instead: Which questions is this person authorized to settle? What experience would allow them to recognize that the current specification is inadequate? Can they obtain that experience and make it consequential, directly or through an accountable intermediary? Where these answers come apart, more efficient execution may conceal a narrowing of agency. Good tools can relieve us of effort while preserving the possibility of learning what our work should become.
