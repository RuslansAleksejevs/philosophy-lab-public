# What a Speaker Owes to an Interpretation They Use

A speaker can sometimes deny having asserted a proposition while still owing an audience an explanation for having relied on its understanding of that proposition. This possibility matters whenever communication is deliberately ambiguous, but also when an innocent ambiguity becomes useful after the fact. The speaker's first intention does not settle everything. Neither does the audience's interpretation. What the speaker subsequently does with that interpretation can generate a responsibility that neither party originally possessed.

Consider a committee deciding whether to approve a new archive. Its director says, “The archive will be open to everyone.” Some members understand this to mean that anyone will be permitted to apply for access. Others reasonably understand it to mean that anyone will actually be able to read the material without paying. Suppose neither reading is decisively excluded by the discussion so far. Before the vote, a member says that her support depends on readers not needing to pay. The director hears this, understands the dependence, and urges the committee to vote immediately. After obtaining approval, he introduces a charge and says that he only meant universal eligibility to apply.

It is tempting to resolve the dispute by deciding what “open” really meant. That question is relevant, but the committee need not answer it to identify a failure. Even if the director's narrow interpretation was genuinely available, he knowingly used support offered on the broader understanding without correcting its basis. His later explanation therefore cannot do all the work of an innocent clarification. He owes the committee an opportunity to reconsider what he obtained.

I defend a limited principle: when a speaker knowingly adopts an audience response as a means to an outcome, and knows that the response reasonably depends on taking the speaker to stand behind a particular content, the speaker must either sustain that basis or correct it while making possible an appropriate reconsideration of the response. I call this responsibility for adopted uptake. “Uptake” here means the audience's understanding of the speaker's communicative contribution; it need not be a correct identification of an original intention. Adoption concerns the speaker's subsequent use of that understanding. The principle does not turn every useful misunderstanding into an assertion. It identifies a responsibility that can arise while the initial speech act remains disputed.

## Three questions that should remain separate

We should distinguish what an utterance conventionally expresses, what a speaker means by it, and what the speaker may reasonably demand from others in light of its reception. These questions interact but are not interchangeable.

Grice distinguishes conversational implications from conventional meaning and explains how context can support an inference that is cancellable. His cancellation discussion permits an explicit addition or a context that blocks the usual implication (1975, pp. 57–58). Nothing in that diagnostic by itself establishes that an utterance made later can remove responsibilities accumulated through an earlier reception. A present denial and an earlier qualification can have different practical significance even when both are linguistically intelligible.

Nor should we identify a person's interpretation with a jointly accepted conversational fact. Stalnaker carefully distinguishes actual common belief from the presuppositions of individual speakers, which can diverge (2002, pp. 706–710). In the archive case, the director need not believe that access will be free. The committee need not unanimously believe it either. A theory of shared informational background can register these differences. It still needs a further normative account of what the director owes the member whose conditional support he knowingly uses.

Camp supplies the most important nearby account. She explains how indirect communication can succeed while preserving deniability and treats the availability of reasonable alternative interpretations as central to its limits (2022, pp. 237–240). This rules out any claim that responsibility beyond literal assertion is a new discovery. My question concerns a narrower possibility: a reasonable alternative interpretation can survive, while a subsequent use of an audience's understanding generates a reason to repair the transaction. That reason does not require defeating the alternative as an account of the original utterance.

Lidington's account of figleaves likewise makes audience-sensitive commitment and repair explicit, explaining conventionally attributed content and changes in commitment strength (2026, §3.1). The proposal here does not identify a new conventional meaning or merely grade the strength of an existing assertion. It asks whether a speaker has acquired a further obligation by taking up another person's response on its manifest basis.

## The argument from conditional participation

The member in the example is entitled to decide which proposal she supports. Her statement makes her participation conditional: her support is for an archive whose material readers can access without paying. She has not offered a detachable unit of support that the director may attach to whichever interpretation he privately prefers.

Three considerations explain why accepting that support changes the director's position.

First, the dependence is communicatively manifest. A reason relevant to the current decision has been stated in response to his contribution. The member is not privately entertaining an eccentric interpretation that the director would have to discover by questioning her. She has made clear which understanding licenses her response.

Second, the director takes the response forward for the very purpose it serves: obtaining approval. This is more than observing that someone misunderstood him. He incorporates the response into the progress of the exchange while knowing its stated basis. The responsibility rests on that practical adoption, not on the mere occurrence of a psychological effect.

Third, a correction was available before approval. The director could have said, “Applications will be unrestricted, but reading may require payment.” That would have preserved his right to mean the narrower thing and the member's right to withhold support. Instead, he lets the interaction proceed in a form that conceals their divergence at the point where it matters.

The conclusion follows from an ordinary constraint on conditional participation: one cannot knowingly solicit or accept a person's participation on an expressed condition, then treat the participation as unconditional merely because one never explicitly endorsed the condition. The participant's agency over her contribution supplies the reason. Speech is the mechanism by which the condition and its apparent satisfaction become salient; the normative force does not have to be manufactured by a disputed semantic analysis.

This argument does not presuppose that the director promised free access. It leaves him free to reject that policy. What it denies is his permission to keep counting the member's support as though it survived the discovery that she was supporting a different proposal. Depending on the setting, repair may require pausing the vote, reopening it, or acknowledging that approval no longer establishes consent to the charged-access version. The principle specifies a reason for reconsideration, not an unrestricted duty to deliver whatever an audience thought it heard.

The structure generalizes beyond voting. If a researcher says that a result is “independently checked,” and knowingly uses a colleague's endorsement after learning that the colleague understood this as a replication rather than a second reading of the same proof, the researcher owes a correction to the endorsement's basis. If a friend says that a gathering is “small,” learns that another friend takes this to exclude an audience, and uses the friend's agreement to announce a public performance, the same structure can arise without money, contracts, or institutions. The relevant outcome can be a favorable judgment or personal cooperation. In each case it matters that the speaker adopts a response which visibly depends on the interpretation.

## A temporal test for clarification

The principle predicts a distinction between four versions of the archive case.

In the first, the director immediately clarifies the charge before anyone offers support. There may have been transient misunderstanding, but no uptake has been adopted. In the second, he learns the member's interpretation and corrects it before urging a vote. He discharges the relevant responsibility by allowing informed reconsideration. In the third, he innocently discovers the divergence only after approval and promptly asks the committee to reassess. A remedial responsibility now exists, but there need have been no culpable exploitation. In the fourth, he knowingly moves the decision forward and invokes the narrow reading only when challenged. The duty to repair is accompanied by a justified complaint about his earlier conduct.

These contrasts hold fixed the sentence and its available readings. They vary knowledge, adoption, and correction. If the practical judgment varies with those factors, a theory that looks only at the initial sentence and intention omits a relevant source of responsibility. A theory can incorporate later events into a temporally extended speech act; it need not deny the analysis. But it must then preserve the distinction between evidence about what was originally meant and an event that creates a new obligation. Otherwise it will mistakenly infer that the later discovery of an obligation proves an original assertion.

Call a clarification prospective when it blocks an interpretation before the speaker adopts the response it supports. Call a repair restorative when it attempts to give the audience back a decision that the speaker has already taken forward on that interpretation. These are functional descriptions, not rigid time limits. A speaker may begin a correction quickly but conceal it from the people whose support mattered. Another may discover an old misunderstanding and correct it as soon as correction becomes possible. Elapsed time alone settles neither case.

A disclaimer addressed only to a second audience cannot normally repair the first audience's decision. Suppose the director tells the funders that “open” means unrestricted applications, while continuing to cite the committee's support to them. This narrows what the funders may attribute to him. It does not supply the committee with the opportunity it was denied. Responsibility follows the particular dependence, even when semantic interpretation varies across audiences. A single public sentence can therefore leave a speaker with several distinct repair obligations without making the speaker the assertor of every proposition inferred from it.

## Objections and limits

The strongest objection is that this is simply an application of a general duty not to mislead. If a speaker knowingly exploits a false belief, why add another principle?

The general duty identifies a broad moral category, but it does not yet specify the repair or the proper object of attribution. In the example, critics might accuse the director of asserting something false, demand that he abolish the charge, or merely insist that he state his private intention. These responses are not equivalent. The proposed principle explains why reopening the support is central even if the assertion charge is unproved and free access is no longer feasible. It also explains why a person who benefits accidentally from a misinterpretation is not automatically guilty of the same initial wrong. The contribution is a discriminating account within the broader morality of misleading speech, not a replacement for that morality.

A second objection charges that adoption smuggles in an implied assertion. By urging the vote after hearing the member's explanation, perhaps the director simply asserts, through silence, that access will be free. In many settings this will be the best interpretation. The argument does not exclude it. But suppose the local convention explicitly distinguishes a chair's procedural urging from agreement with any stated reasons. That weakens the inference to assertion. If the director nevertheless knowingly seeks authorization from a vote he knows is conditional on a false understanding of his proposal, he still owes reconsideration. An additional assertion would provide an additional route to responsibility; it is not necessary to this one.

A third objection concerns hostile or unreasonable listeners. Someone might announce, “I take your greeting as a promise to give me your house,” and then claim that any continued conversation adopts that uptake. This fails twice. The interpretation is unreasonable given the words, context, and ordinary communicative practice; and continuing a greeting does not adopt the purported response as a means to obtaining a relevant outcome. These restrictions should be assessed independently of whether the speaker dislikes the result. They require positive evidence of a reasonable interpretive route, a specific dependent response, the speaker's awareness, and the use made of it. Bare allegations do not establish them.

What if an unreasonable interpretation is knowingly exploited? There may still be a serious wrong. The present principle offers sufficient conditions for a distinctive conversational responsibility, not necessary conditions for every deception. Keeping reasonableness in the principle prevents it from making a speaker answerable for uncontrolled projections merely because some remote benefit follows.

A fourth objection worries that the principle destroys tact. Friends often float suggestions, receive tentative enthusiasm, and later explain that nothing definite was offered. They need room to explore without incurring every commitment an optimistic hearer projects. Here the difference is whether the response is understood as conditional on a definite endorsement or as participation in an experiment. If both parties understand “perhaps” as a tentative invitation to consider an option, the resulting enthusiasm does not reasonably depend on a guarantee. If one learns that the other has started making definite arrangements and actively uses those arrangements while maintaining the tentative appearance elsewhere, the duty to clarify becomes appropriate. Protecting exploration does not require permitting that transition to remain concealed.

Finally, speakers cannot monitor every audience. A broadcast may generate innumerable interpretations, and some errors cannot be repaired. The principle does not impose omniscience. Its central culpability claim requires actual awareness and knowing adoption. A weaker duty of inquiry may sometimes arise where a speaker has deliberately insulated themselves from predictable responses, but defending its exact threshold would require a further argument. Once an error is known, repair remains constrained by feasibility and competing obligations. One can owe an adequate attempt to reach affected participants without owing a guarantee of universal correction.

## Answerability without forced confession

The conclusion is modest about meaning and demanding about the use of meaning. An audience cannot decide by fiat what a speaker asserted. A speaker cannot decide by fiat that a response knowingly accepted on a manifest basis was offered on some other basis. Between those claims lies a kind of answerability that disputes over literal wording often conceal.

The practical question is not only, “Was that a possible meaning of your words?” It is also, “What understanding did you knowingly use to obtain this response, and have you allowed that response to be reconsidered?” This second question can have a determinate answer when the first remains unsettled. It gives a hearer a defensible way to demand repair without forcing a false confession of assertion, and gives an innocent speaker a way to correct a misunderstanding without pretending that its consequences disappear. The right to clarify an utterance is preserved. The right to retain every benefit obtained before clarifying it is not.
