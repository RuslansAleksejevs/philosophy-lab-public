# What a Speaker Owes to an Interpretation They Use

A speaker can sometimes deny having asserted a proposition while still owing an audience an explanation for having relied on its understanding of that proposition. This possibility matters whenever communication is deliberately ambiguous, but also when an innocent ambiguity becomes useful after the fact. The speaker's first intention does not settle everything. Neither does the audience's interpretation. What the speaker subsequently does with that interpretation can generate a responsibility that neither party originally possessed.

Consider a committee deciding whether to approve a new archive. Its director says, “The archive will be open to everyone.” Some members understand this to mean that anyone will be permitted to apply for access. Others reasonably understand it to mean that anyone will actually be able to read the material without paying. Suppose neither reading is decisively excluded by the discussion so far. The committee is authorizing a specific access policy, rather than delegating discretion over fees. Its procedure permits members to qualify their authorization and requires unresolved qualifications to be addressed before the result is recorded. A member explicitly makes her authorization conditional on access remaining free under the approved policy. The director already plans to charge readers. He understands her qualification but urges an immediate vote and records her support without it. Afterwards he introduces the planned charge and says that he only meant universal eligibility to apply.

It is tempting to resolve the dispute by deciding what “open” really meant. That question is relevant, but the committee need not answer it to identify a failure. Even if the director's narrow interpretation was genuinely available, he knowingly used support offered on the broader understanding without correcting its basis. His later explanation therefore cannot do all the work of an innocent clarification. He owes the committee an opportunity to reconsider what he obtained.

I defend a limited principle about a speaker who knows that an audience has offered a response on an express condition concerning the speaker’s contribution. If the speaker deliberately uses that response as authorization, endorsement, or cooperation while knowing the condition is unmet, the speaker owes disclosure and an appropriate opportunity to reconsider the response. The opportunity extends only as far as the participant’s actual authority and legitimate procedures allow. I call this responsibility for adopted uptake. “Uptake” here means the audience's understanding of the speaker's communicative contribution; it need not be a correct identification of an original intention. Adoption concerns the speaker's subsequent use of that understanding. I concentrate on reasonable interpretations, where the complaint arises within an ordinary communicative exchange. The principle does not turn every useful misunderstanding into an assertion. It identifies a responsibility that can arise while the initial speech act remains disputed.

## Three questions that should remain separate

We should distinguish what an utterance conventionally expresses, what a speaker means by it, and what the speaker may reasonably demand from others in light of its reception. These questions interact but are not interchangeable.

Grice distinguishes conversational implications from conventional meaning and explains how context can support an inference that is cancellable. His cancellation discussion permits an explicit addition or a context that blocks the usual implication (1975, pp. 57–58). Nothing in that diagnostic by itself establishes that an utterance made later can remove responsibilities accumulated through an earlier reception. A present denial and an earlier qualification can have different practical significance even when both are linguistically intelligible.

Nor should we identify a person's interpretation with a jointly accepted conversational fact. Stalnaker carefully distinguishes actual common belief from the presuppositions of individual speakers, which can diverge (2002, pp. 706–710). In the archive case, the director does not believe that access will be free. The committee need not unanimously believe it either. A theory of shared informational background can register these differences. A further normative account is needed to explain what the director owes the member whose conditional support he knowingly uses; this is an additional question, not a defect in Stalnaker’s project.

Camp supplies the most important nearby account. She explains how indirect communication can succeed while preserving deniability and treats the availability of reasonable alternative interpretations as central to its limits (2022, pp. 237–240). This rules out any claim that responsibility beyond literal assertion is a new discovery. My question concerns a narrower possibility: a reasonable alternative interpretation can survive, while a subsequent use of an audience's understanding generates a reason to repair the transaction. That reason does not require defeating the alternative as an account of the original utterance.

Lidington's account of figleaves likewise makes audience-sensitive commitment and repair explicit, explaining conventionally attributed content and changes in commitment strength (2026, §3.1). The proposal here does not identify a new conventional meaning or merely grade the strength of an existing assertion. It asks whether a speaker has acquired a further obligation by taking up another person's response on its manifest basis.

## The argument from conditional participation

The member in the example is entitled to decide which proposal she supports. Her explicit qualification makes her authorization conditional: it is for a policy under which readers can access material without paying. This is stronger than merely explaining why she favors the project. She has not offered a detachable unit of support that the director may attach to whichever interpretation he privately prefers.

Three considerations explain why accepting that support changes the director's position.

First, the condition is communicatively manifest. A qualification that the procedure permits has been stated in response to his contribution. The member is not privately entertaining an eccentric interpretation that the director would have to discover by questioning her. She has made clear which understanding licenses her response.

Second, the director takes the response forward for the very purpose it serves: obtaining approval. This is more than observing that someone misunderstood him. He incorporates the response into the progress of the exchange while knowing its stated basis. The responsibility rests on that practical adoption, not on the mere occurrence of a psychological effect.

Third, a correction was available before approval. The director could have said, “Applications will be unrestricted, but I plan to charge readers.” That would have preserved his right to mean the narrower thing and the member's right to withhold support. Instead, he uses the vote as though the qualification had been satisfied or withdrawn. The complaint concerns an existing boundary on the member’s authorization, not a new power created by her disappointment.

The conclusion follows from an ordinary constraint on conditional participation: one cannot knowingly solicit or accept a person's participation on an expressed condition, then treat the participation as unconditional merely because one never explicitly endorsed the condition. The participant's agency over her contribution supplies the reason. Speech is the mechanism by which the condition and its apparent satisfaction become salient; the normative force does not have to be manufactured by a disputed semantic analysis.

This argument does not presuppose that the director promised free access. It leaves him free to reject that policy. What it denies is his permission to keep counting the member's support as though it survived the discovery that she was supporting a different proposal. Given the specified procedure, repair may require pausing the vote, correcting the record, or allowing the member to revise her authorization before a new result is determined. The principle specifies a reason for reconsideration, not an unrestricted duty to deliver whatever an audience thought it heard.

A contrasting case shows why the qualification matters. Suppose members knowingly delegate fee-setting discretion to the director. One says she votes for the delegation because she expects him to keep access free, but accepts that he may decide otherwise. If he conceals his contrary plan, he may mislead her and owe correction or apology. Her expressed expectation does not by itself reserve a veto or establish a right to reverse the completed delegation. The present argument derives repair of a conditional contribution, not a universal remedy for disappointed reasons. Even where an authorization was improperly obtained, effects on other participants and legitimate procedures can constrain how it is reopened.

The condition’s content also matters. Compare relying on free access, relying on the director’s assurance of free access, and relying on his promise to maintain it. Quietly arranging a subsidy might satisfy the first condition if it actually secures the relevant policy. It cannot automatically satisfy the other two. A subsidy does not retrospectively supply an assurance or promise that was never given, and temporary free access may not secure a continuing policy. Repair must identify the actual condition instead of substituting whatever proposition is easiest to make true. If free access alone was the condition and it is genuinely secured before the response is used, private disagreement about terminology creates no violation of this principle. Where the director has already used support on an unmet condition, later performance does not erase that earlier defect or any remaining need to correct the record.

This distinction makes the claim more than an instruction to prevent false beliefs. The relevant object is a particular contribution and the scope on which it was offered. Correcting belief is one part of restoring control over that contribution; satisfying a policy can be another. Neither is invariably sufficient alone.

## A temporal test for clarification

The principle predicts a distinction between four versions of the archive case.

In the first, the director immediately clarifies the charge before anyone offers support. There may have been transient misunderstanding, but no uptake has been adopted. In the second, he learns the member's interpretation and corrects it before urging a vote. He discharges the relevant responsibility by allowing informed reconsideration. In the third, a director who initially overlooked the qualification learns of it after approval but before presenting that approval as authority to implement fees. He corrects the record and seeks reassessment before relying on it. His original conduct may have been innocent; knowingly using the approval without its qualification now would not be. The principle governs the proposed continuing use. Mere discovery of an irreversible past effect, without any further adoption, would require a separate argument for repair that I do not offer here. In the fourth, he knowingly moves the decision forward and invokes the narrow reading only when challenged. The duty to repair is accompanied by a justified complaint about his earlier conduct.

These contrasts hold fixed the sentence and its available readings, while specifying different stages at which the qualification becomes known. They vary knowledge, adoption, and correction. The practical differences cannot be read off the initial sentence and intention alone. A theory can incorporate later events into a temporally extended speech act; it need not deny the analysis. But it must then preserve the distinction between evidence about what was originally meant and an event that creates a new obligation. Otherwise it will mistakenly infer that the later discovery of an obligation proves an original assertion.

Call a clarification prospective when it blocks an interpretation before the speaker adopts the response it supports. Call a repair restorative when it attempts to give the audience back a decision that the speaker has already taken forward on that interpretation. These are functional descriptions, not rigid time limits. A speaker may begin a correction quickly but conceal it from the people whose support mattered. Another may discover an old misunderstanding and correct it as soon as correction becomes possible. Elapsed time alone settles neither case.

A disclaimer addressed only to a second audience cannot normally repair the first audience's decision. Suppose the director tells the funders that “open” means unrestricted applications, while continuing to cite the committee's support to them. This narrows what the funders may attribute to him. It does not supply the committee with the opportunity it was denied. Responsibility follows the particular dependence, even when semantic interpretation varies across audiences. A single public sentence can therefore leave a speaker with several distinct repair obligations without making the speaker the assertor of every proposition inferred from it.

## Objections and limits

The strongest objection is that this is simply an application of a general duty not to mislead. If a speaker knowingly exploits a false belief, why add another principle?

The general duty identifies a broad moral category, but it does not yet specify the repair or the proper object of attribution. In the example, critics might accuse the director of asserting something false, demand that he abolish the charge, or merely insist that he state his private intention. These responses are not equivalent. The proposed principle explains why correcting the scope of authorization and enabling procedurally legitimate reconsideration are central even if the assertion charge is unproved and free access is no longer feasible. It also explains why a person who benefits accidentally from a misinterpretation is not automatically guilty of the same initial wrong. The contribution is a discriminating account within the broader morality of misleading speech, not a replacement for that morality.

A second objection charges that adoption smuggles in an implied assertion. By urging the vote after hearing the member's explanation, perhaps the director simply asserts, through silence, that access will be free. In many settings this will be the best interpretation. The argument does not exclude it. But suppose the local convention explicitly distinguishes a chair's procedural urging from agreement with any stated reasons. That weakens the inference to assertion. If the director nevertheless records an explicitly qualified vote as unqualified authorization, he still owes correction and an appropriate chance to reconsider. An additional assertion would provide an additional route to responsibility; it is not necessary to this one.

A third objection concerns the boundary between using a response and merely benefiting from it. Imagine an unsolicited favorable review of the archive, based on a reasonable misunderstanding of “open.” The director knows of it but neither quotes it, submits it as an endorsement, nor directs anyone to rely on it. He continues a project authorized on independent grounds. Favorable publicity alone does not establish adoption in the sense defended here. There may be other duties to correct public error, but those need separate grounds. The result changes if he cites the review to obtain funding as though the reviewer endorsed the charged-access policy. He then presents a contribution with a known limited basis as support for something beyond that basis. Active solicitation, citation, recording as authorization, and directing reliance provide evidence of adoption; mere causal advantage does not.

What role does interpretive reasonableness play? It does not create the participant’s authority over a conditional offer. A speaker can deliberately misuse a conditional contribution grounded in a bizarre belief, and the agency argument may still apply. Reasonableness instead restricts the present inquiry to cases in which the disputed uptake is a defensible response to the original communication. Awareness, express conditionality, and deliberate use already do most of the work against uncontrolled projections. I therefore do not present reasonableness as a further premise derived from the agency argument. It marks the communicative problem being investigated, while the underlying constraint on conditional participation has wider scope.

A fourth objection worries that the principle destroys tact. Friends often float suggestions, receive tentative enthusiasm, and later explain that nothing definite was offered. They need room to explore without incurring every commitment an optimistic hearer projects. Here the difference is whether the response is understood as conditional on a definite endorsement or as participation in an experiment. If both parties understand “perhaps” as a tentative invitation to consider an option, the resulting enthusiasm does not reasonably depend on a guarantee. If one learns that the other has expressly conditioned cooperation on a definite undertaking and then uses that cooperation while maintaining the tentative appearance elsewhere, clarification becomes necessary before proceeding. Simply knowing that someone has made optimistic plans is not enough to establish the conditional offer defended here. Protecting exploration does not require permitting that transition to remain concealed.

Finally, speakers cannot monitor every audience. A broadcast may generate innumerable interpretations, and some errors cannot be repaired. The principle does not impose omniscience. Its central culpability claim requires actual awareness and knowing adoption. A weaker duty of inquiry may sometimes arise where a speaker has deliberately insulated themselves from predictable responses, but defending its exact threshold would require a further argument. Where the conditions of the principle are met, repair remains constrained by feasibility and competing obligations. One can owe an adequate attempt to reach affected participants without owing a guarantee of universal correction.

## Answerability without forced confession

The conclusion is modest about meaning and demanding about the use of meaning. An audience cannot decide by fiat what a speaker asserted. A speaker cannot decide by fiat that an expressly conditional response was offered unconditionally. Between those claims lies a kind of answerability that disputes over literal wording often conceal.

The practical question is not only, “Was that a possible meaning of your words?” It is also, “What condition governed the response you knowingly used, and have you respected its actual scope?” This second question can have a determinate answer when the first remains unsettled. It gives a hearer a defensible way to demand repair without forcing a false confession of assertion, and gives an innocent speaker a way to correct a misunderstanding without pretending that its consequences disappear. The right to clarify an utterance is preserved. The right to retain every benefit obtained before clarifying it is not.
