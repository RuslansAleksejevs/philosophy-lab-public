# Critique: What a Speaker Owes to an Interpretation They Use

Verdict: revise and retain the narrower contribution. The essay is unusually clear about the difference between attributing an assertion and demanding repair. Its core example supports a plausible moral result. The current general principle, however, outruns the case in two consequential ways: it slides between reasons for participation and conditions on participation, and between culpable appropriation and innocent post hoc discovery. Originality remains plausible at the level of a carefully specified repair argument, not established as a new theory of conversational accountability.

## Reconstruction and contribution

The strongest argument is this: a person has authority to offer cooperation conditionally; a speaker knows that a response to their ambiguous words is conditional on a particular understanding; the speaker deliberately advances a project using that response without disclosing a known divergence. The speaker therefore owes restoration, where feasible, of the participant's opportunity to withhold or revise the response. This duty can survive the speaker's successful defense that the disputed proposition was never asserted. An available semantic defense is not automatically a defense of subsequent conduct.

The contribution is the identification of the proper object of repair: the authorization, endorsement, or cooperation obtained on the understood basis. That distinguishes reconsideration from compelled fulfillment and from mere clarification. The several-audiences case usefully explains why correcting an interpretation for one audience does not necessarily discharge a duty to another. None of these points needs the broad claim that meaning is independent of intention, which is established background.

## Main vulnerabilities and precise repairs

1. **A reason for voting is not necessarily a condition on the authorization conferred by the vote.** A voter can say that she supports a candidate because she expects a policy, while knowingly authorizing the candidate to exercise discretion and accepting that expectations may prove false. Even an announced reason need not be an attempted reservation of power. In a committee, a formally unconditional vote may similarly authorize a range of policies despite the member's stated motivation. The draft moves from “her support depends on” to a claim that support may not be counted after discovery without specifying which relation holds.

This does not vindicate the director's deception. It separates the duty to disclose and apologize from a right to reopen the authorization. Revise the main case to establish that the committee is approving a specified policy and that the member's statement explicitly conditions her authorization on free access. Then add a contrasting case where participants knowingly delegate discretion: correction may be owed, but an automatic entitlement to retract the institutional decision does not follow. State that appropriate reconsideration respects the actual scope and procedures of the participant's authority, rather than creating a general veto over outcomes.

2. **The innocent-discovery case is not derived from the announced principle.** The principle requires knowing adoption; the third temporal case stipulates an innocent discovery after approval. If approval is completed and the director makes no further use of the support, its original acquisition does not satisfy that requirement. Calling a remedial responsibility present simply adds a second principle. A speaker who quotes an old approval after learning its basis does knowingly adopt it now; a speaker who merely discovers an irreversible past effect may instead owe repair because of causal responsibility, control, or continuing benefit.

Either restrict the temporal comparison to cases of continued reliance after discovery or expressly distinguish two claims. The defended one concerns knowing appropriation; a more tentative extension concerns innocent causation followed by feasible repair. Do not let a distinction between responsibility and blame substitute for the missing ground of responsibility.

3. **“Sustain that basis” can permit a quiet repair that still disrespects choice.** Suppose the director never endorsed free access, learns that the committee thinks he did, and quietly arranges a temporary subsidy so that readers pay nothing. The policy condition is satisfied, but the committee may also have relied on a belief about his endorsement, trustworthiness, or future intentions. Conversely, if the only relevant condition really was free access, his private lack of endorsement need not matter. The principle currently runs together dependence on a proposition's truth and dependence on the speaker standing behind it.

Specify the content of the relevant condition. Is the participant relying on p, on the speaker's assurance that p, or on a promise to make p true? Restoration should address whichever basis actually licensed the cooperation. Making p true does not always repair falsely borrowed assurance. This distinction could deepen the contribution without introducing a large new apparatus.

4. **Reasonableness is not justified by the argument from agency.** A director who intentionally exploits a bizarre but explicit misunderstanding still uses a person's condition without respecting it. The draft acknowledges a possible wrong but says reasonableness protects against uncontrolled projections. Yet awareness, a specific dependent response, and deliberate use already exclude most uncontrolled projections. Explain what independent work interpretive reasonableness performs. It may identify a specifically communicative claim against the original speaker, while deliberate exploitation grounds a wider duty even for unreasonable beliefs. Present it as a scope restriction rather than as something demonstrated by conditional agency.

5. **Adoption needs a non-question-begging threshold.** Actively soliciting a stated conditional endorsement is clear. Receiving favorable publicity from an endorsement one neither requested nor cites is less clear. Quietly proceeding with an independently justified project may be neither adoption nor rejection. Give one near-miss where the speaker knows of a reasonable misunderstanding but does not use the response. Explain what would change the result: citing the endorsement, presenting it as authorization, or directing others to rely on it. This is stronger than only the absurd house-promise example, which tests too many exclusion conditions at once.

The draft's limited attributions are accurate. Grice's linguistic diagnostic is not a retrospective absolution principle, and Stalnaker is not offering a competing ethics of reliance; keep these as distinctions rather than suggesting a refutation of either theory.

## Significance and readability

The stakes are practical and philosophical: an audience can have a justified repair demand without winning a disputed attribution of assertion. That is worth defending. The prose and opening example are strong; there is no need for more scene-setting or length. “Adopted uptake” is defined, but often “uses the response” is clearer. The main sentence is overloaded with knowledge, reasonableness, means, dependence, and endorsement: divide the conditions across two sentences. Replace the vague gathering example with the single near-miss requested above. Most urgently, specify what the director knew about the intended charge before the vote; merely introducing a charge later can otherwise be an honest later policy change.

Revision priorities: settle condition versus motivation; repair the innocent-discovery inference; distinguish a true policy from an attributed assurance; give adoption a close boundary case. These changes would make the narrower thesis substantially better defended. This critique is a separate AI contribution to essay 15 only, not an eligibility, originality, or truth certificate.
