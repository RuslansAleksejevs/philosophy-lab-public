# When Everyone Is Wrong: Institutional Reality and the Power to Correct

An association elects its chair by secret ballot. A copying error reverses the candidates’ totals. Everyone, including the candidates and election committee, sincerely believes that Mara won. They let her chair meetings. The original ballots, which nobody has inspected since the mistake, show that Leon received the majority. Who is the chair?

There is no answer until we know more about the association. Perhaps receiving the majority makes someone chair, and the announcement merely reports that result. Perhaps certification makes someone chair even when it misreports the majority. Perhaps the committee can choose a chair at its discretion. These arrangements can produce the same unanimous belief, announcement, and pattern of obedience while making different institutional facts true.

The familiar lesson is that social dependence does not entail dependence on present opinion. My concern is a further question: what must an account of an institution preserve in order to distinguish its surviving an error from its changing? I argue that it must preserve a structure of priorities among three activities: applying standards, settling consequences, and amending standards. A change in that structure can transform the institution before anyone receives a different classification. Conversely, even universal error need not transform it. Institutional continuity is partly a matter of what can defeat what, rather than only of which classifications people currently accept.

The argument concerns organized statuses such as officeholding and membership. It is not a definition of all social reality, and it does not assume that institutional validity produces moral authority.

## 1. Recognition needs an object

Searle distinguishes the dependence of institutional reality on human attitudes from the epistemic objectivity of particular institutional facts. He also describes interlocking constitutive rules and the powers attached to statuses. Thus it would be a mistake to attribute to him the simple thesis that whatever everyone presently believes about a particular officeholder must be true. The question is which attitudes, toward which objects, do the constituting. [Searle 2006, pp. 13–18](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/SPIG/SearleSO2006.pdf).

Epstein provides a useful distinction. Facts that establish the conditions for occupying an office need not be the facts that satisfy those conditions. His distinction between anchoring and grounding separates these inquiries; his discussion of historical grounds also makes room for present statuses partly constituted by past events. [Epstein 2016, pp. 153–158](https://epstein.org/wp-content/uploads/Epstein-A-Framework-for-Social-Ontology-in-Phil-of-Social-Sciences.pdf).

Neither distinction is my proposed contribution. Indeed, Dörge and Holweger explicitly describe a case in which an accepted succession rule makes someone heir despite concealment of the relevant family relationship. Their category of indirect recognition accommodates facts that participants do not directly recognize. Universal ignorance of a particular status is therefore an old and available possibility, not an objection that recognition theorists have simply overlooked. [Dörge and Holweger 2021, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-03010-6).

The further problem arises when recognition conflicts with recognition. The association accepts majority election. It also accepts the committee’s announcement. These are not two independent observations that happen to disagree. Each might plausibly be assigned a constitutive role. Saying that the accepted rules determine the answer moves the inquiry to a more informative level, but it does not yet tell us which accepted practice is a rule of appointment and which is a fallible way of applying one.

We cannot settle this by giving every accepted representation equal constitutive weight. If accepting majority election and accepting Mara’s victory each sufficed, without qualification, to establish the corresponding institutional result, the mistaken association would have incompatible unique chairs. Nor should we infer that the association must therefore have ceased to exist. Fallible administration is compatible with an institution’s continuing operation. What is missing from the flat inventory of accepted representations is their relative authority.

## 2. Three roles for the same announcement

Consider three versions of the association. In each, Leon received more votes, the committee announced Mara, and everyone currently accepts the announcement. Set aside ties, fraud, and interpretive uncertainty.

In the first association, its established arrangements make majority receipt sufficient for office. The committee is charged with discovering the result. Authenticated ballots override the announcement, and a later recount recognizes the winner’s title from the original election date. Mara is treated as chair but is not the elected chair. The institution can protect third parties who relied on her apparent authority; that would be a further rule, not evidence that the election rule has vanished.

In the second, certification appoints the chair. The committee must certify the majority winner, but a properly issued certificate is effective even when mistaken. No timely challenge was filed. Mara is chair, although her appointment resulted from the committee’s failure to satisfy its duty. A recount could establish that failure without removing her or making Leon retrospectively chair.

In the third, the committee’s designation is the only standard of selection. Members call the occasion an election, and the committee normally consults ballots, but the committee may disregard them without violating any rule of appointment. Mara is chair, and selecting her is not an incorrect application of the appointment standard.

The differences are not merely disagreements about words. In the first association, recognizing Leon requires no exercise of a power to replace the chair. In the second it would require a new institutional act, assuming such an act is available. In the third even condemning the committee for applying its appointment standard incorrectly misses the nature of its power. Moral condemnation and criticism of its wisdom remain possible in all three.

The second case is crucial. Hart’s distinction between a scorer’s final authority and the correctness of the scorer’s application prevents us from identifying institutional objectivity with the possibility of successful appeal. An adjudicator may have the last word while remaining bound by a standard the adjudicator can misapply. [Hart, *The Concept of Law*, VII.3](https://deepwrite.github.io/HartConcept/book-original/chapter_07).

An account based simply on available correction would wrongly merge the second and third associations. An account based simply on the rule that the majority ought to win would risk merging the first and second. We need to ask both what the official was required to determine and what the official’s act actually settles.

For these purposes, three roles should be kept separate. **Application** assesses a case against an existing standard. **Settlement** fixes an institutional consequence, sometimes despite defective application. **Amendment** changes the standard that future applications are to use. An act can perform more than one role. The distinctions concern its effects and constraints, not three necessarily separate offices.

This separation enables a precise description of institutional error. A status attribution is erroneous relative to a specified standard when the conditions that standard establishes are not satisfied. That error may or may not defeat the attribution’s practical consequences. It may also influence later standards, but it does not become an amendment merely by being accepted as correct on this occasion.

## 3. Priority before agreement

How do we establish these roles without assuming the answer? In an actual association, we would investigate how announcements, ballots, challenges, and amendments have been treated. An explicit charter would be evidence, sometimes very strong evidence, but not a metaphysical device that makes every printed provision effective forever.

Suppose officials preserve ballots specifically for recounts, accept the correction of transcription errors in analogous elections, distinguish correcting a name from appointing a successor, and reject purported rule changes made by an election committee. These features jointly support the first arrangement. They connect the election rule to a practiced distinction between finding an answer and making a new one. Present unanimity about Mara is compatible with this structure because everyone is mistaken about an input the structure makes decisive.

By contrast, suppose the association routinely treats certificates as sufficient for office, regards electoral irregularity as a ground for disciplining the committee but not for invalidating a completed appointment, and requires a removal vote to replace a certified chair. These features support the second arrangement. They show that two consequences have been separated: correction of the assessment and reversal of the appointment.

These investigations are fallible. People can be dishonest about the significance of their practices. A charter can conflict with entrenched administration. Some institutions may have no determinate answer. But the possibility of uncertainty is not an objection to the distinctions. An ontology that automatically derives a determinate outcome from unanimous current attribution would hide the uncertainty by selecting one source of authority in advance.

Nor do the distinctions require unanimous acceptance of the whole structure. Many members may not understand appointment rules; dissidents may reject them. What matters here is whether the relevant structure is maintained in the operation of the institution, not whether every affected person endorses its legitimacy. This restriction is especially important because contested institutional constraints need not disappear when their subjects reject them, a concern motivating Brännmark’s approach to institutional facts. [Brännmark 2019, introduction](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-018-9994-7).

We can now state a limited principle:

Where an institutional practice maintains a distinction between applying a standard and changing it, convergence on an application does not by itself establish a change of standard. To establish such a change, one must identify either an exercise of an amendment power or a change in the practices that sustain the distinction itself.

The principle follows from the functions being distinguished. If every accepted application automatically altered its standard to fit, there would no longer be a maintained possibility of incorrect application in the relevant cases. The practice might still exist as discretionary selection, but its earlier division between application and amendment would have been abandoned. Preserving that division therefore requires treating at least some accepted applications as potentially subordinate to what they apply.

This is not a claim that repetition cannot change rules. Repeated deviations may eventually establish a new arrangement. It is a claim that frequency and unanimity do not tell us, on their own, whether repetition is performing that role. Habitual failure to recount ballots can coexist with a maintained entitlement to a recount. Habitual disregard of an entitlement can also erode it. The difference must be investigated through its place in the wider practice.

## 4. Transformation without a changed verdict

The principle has a consequence that is easier to miss than the possibility of collective error: an institution can change while all its current verdicts remain fixed.

Imagine a second election in which Mara genuinely receives the majority and is correctly announced. Before the result, the association follows the first arrangement. Afterward, it validly adopts a rule making certification conclusive for office. Nobody’s current title changes. No present classification changes from false to true. The records, votes, and incumbent remain the same. Nonetheless, authority has moved. In a future mistaken election, the ballots will no longer defeat a completed appointment merely by showing that the committee miscounted.

The rule has not made the committee’s arithmetic infallible. It has changed which consequence an arithmetic correction can have. If a further amendment makes designation wholly discretionary, another transformation occurs: the ballots cease even to supply the appointment standard. These are two different changes, although neither need alter the next hundred outcomes if the committee continues to select majority winners.

An institution’s observed classifications therefore underdetermine its organization in a philosophically important way. Two institutions can be extensionally identical over their actual cases while assigning different roles to those classifications. They may differ over the same possible mistake, or over the permissible response to it. This is not just the general point that dispositions outrun manifestations. The specific difference is the allocation of powers to turn facts into statuses and to defeat the resulting consequences.

This allocation matters when institutions claim to be merely improving administrative efficiency. Eliminating an appeal can change who has settlement authority while leaving the underlying criterion untouched. Removing the criterion is a further change. Conversely, an institution can maintain formal appeals while depriving every relevant piece of evidence of its capacity to affect the result. The existence of a procedure bearing the name “review” does not show that the original relation of authority survives.

The appropriate question is consequently not just whether challenges are permitted. It is what a successful challenge would establish and what it would alter. Would it show that a person never occupied the office, terminate an otherwise effective appointment, expose an official’s misconduct without disturbing the result, or merely invite a discretionary favor? These differences specify the reality with which participants must deal.

## 5. What if nobody would correct the mistake?

A serious objection now presses. I have used patterns of correction to identify institutional structure. Suppose everybody would stubbornly refuse to correct the announcement even after seeing the ballots. Does the account have to say that Mara was chair after all?

No. An actual disposition to comply is not identical to the rule being complied with. Fear, self-interest, or corruption can block a response that remains institutionally required. A committee can prevent a recount by unlawfully locking the archive. Members may acknowledge Leon’s entitlement in private, keep the original rule for other purposes, and understand a refusal as abuse. The failure of an available remedy does not automatically erase either the remedy or the standard.

But the qualification cannot be expanded without limit. Imagine that over time all relevant practice treats announcements as appointments, correction as replacement, and ballots as nonbinding advice; the old charter is retained only as an artifact. There is no longer a reason to describe the operative association as forever governed by the first arrangement merely because it once was. The historical statement that Leon won an earlier election can remain true. The distinct claim that this indefinitely entitles him to exercise powers in the current institution requires an account of continuity that the old text alone does not supply.

There may be an interval during which institutional identity is disputed or unsettled. That is a real possibility, not a defect to be removed by stipulating a hidden exact moment of transformation. The present argument supplies constraints on describing the transition: ending effective remedies, ending valid remedies, and ending the underlying standard are not the same event. They can occur together, but their coincidence must be shown.

This also answers a regress worry. If a rule about correction can itself be misapplied, must there be an endless sequence of correction rules? There need not be. Practical settlement can terminate at an authority while correctness remains assessable under the standards governing that authority. At the level where those standards themselves are no longer sustained, institutional inquiry turns to the practices that maintain or abandon them. It need not invent a further official with unlimited jurisdiction.

## 6. A modest realism

Does distinguishing these roles establish that institutional entities are real? Only in a limited sense. It establishes a form of objectivity internal to an actually sustained institutional arrangement: a person’s status and the propriety of an official act need not coincide with anyone’s present opinion about them. The distinction does not create a realm of offices independent of all social activity.

An anti-realist can accept every described pattern of conduct while refusing to admit institutional facts in addition to those patterns. Dörge and Holweger’s introductory challenge makes clear why behavioral effects alone cannot settle that dispute: people can act on beliefs whose purported objects do not exist. [Dörge and Holweger 2021, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-03010-6). My argument does not refute their full metaphysical case.

Nevertheless, an adequate replacement description must preserve the contrasts established here. Reporting that everyone obeyed Mara leaves out whether recognizing Leon would be correction, replacement, or a request for favor. Those differences are not captured by adding that everyone believed Mara was chair, because that belief is shared across the cases. They require the relations among practices, standards, acts, and consequences. Calling those relations institutional does not add a mysterious substance. It identifies organization that a successful description must retain, even if that description ultimately reduces it to other facts.

The result also falls short of moral vindication. A discriminatory association may scrupulously distinguish valid appointment from error. Its standards can remain objectionable, and the people it excludes may have no moral duty to honor them. Equally, someone can have a compelling moral claim to authority without holding a particular institutional office. Precision about institutional reality enables those criticisms by stating what must be changed and what can already be demanded under existing arrangements.

The initial question therefore has a conditional but substantive answer. Everyone can be wrong about who holds an office when the maintained appointment standard assigns the office to someone else and current attribution is subordinate to that standard. Everyone can instead be right about the officeholder while wrong about whether the appointment was properly made. And the institution can change even while everyone continues to classify the same people in the same way. What determines these possibilities is not agreement alone, but the organized difference between finding an institutional answer, making it effective, and acquiring the power to change it.
