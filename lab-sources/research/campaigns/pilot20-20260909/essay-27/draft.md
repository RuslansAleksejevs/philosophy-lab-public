# What Eternity Cannot Make Up For

An indefinitely long life seems to promise time to make up for everything. A failed project can give way to a successful one; a missed pleasure can be followed by innumerable pleasures; a neglected relationship can be replaced or repaired. This prospect supports an argument against immortality: if later opportunities can always compensate for present losses, nothing we do now has quite the seriousness it has under the shadow of death. A life without an ultimate deadline would lack the pressures through which commitments acquire weight.

There is an established answer: an endless life need not contain endless opportunities to do each particular thing. Luca Ferrero explicitly distinguishes temporal finitude from scarcity of opportunities in the abstract of “Agency, Scarcity, and Mortality” (2015). More recently, Oskam and Buben argue that even an immortal's activities can have deadlines and that missing a chance can produce lasting regret (2026, pp.179–181). The broad distinction between an unlimited lifetime and limited opportunities is therefore not a new solution.

But a question remains within that solution. Why should losing this opportunity matter if the agent can obtain arbitrarily many equally valuable opportunities afterward? Simply pointing out that the past cannot be repeated does not answer it. Most past events are unrepeatable without being important. Nor should we need the psychological prediction that immortals would regret certain failures forever. They might heal, forget, or rightly cease to dwell on them.

I defend a narrower answer. Some practical reasons concern fulfillment of a claim rather than replacement of an amount of value. Later goods may compensate for the loss caused by violating such a claim without fulfilling it or retrospectively authorizing its violation. Consequently, immortality need not eliminate a distinctive seriousness of commitment: one can still be answerable for doing something for someone while it can be done. This is a contribution to the scarcity reply, not a proof that immortality is desirable. It identifies a way in which finite opportunities retain authority even against an unlimited compensatory future.

## 1. A deadline among immortals

Consider two people who know they cannot die. Neither can travel backward in time. They remain embodied agents who sometimes need assistance, undertake joint activities, and act in a shared sequence of events. Their immortality does not confer simultaneous presence everywhere or unlimited control over one another.

One of them, Mara, is learning a demanding form of collective performance. Tomorrow she will perform with a group whose members have prepared this particular work together. She asks her close friend, Ivo, to be present because she wants to undertake this uncertain first performance with his support. Ivo freely promises. The request is reasonable; attending imposes little cost; no more important claim competes with it. Tomorrow Ivo can attend, or remain at home for a mildly preferable diversion.

Mara will perform again. She will have good relationships and countless worthwhile experiences. Ivo could support a thousand subsequent performances. We can even stipulate that a later act of care will leave Mara happier overall than she would have been had he attended tomorrow. Does that make staying home permissible on the ground that there is always time to make up for it?

The ordinary answer is no. Ivo has not promised to deliver an unspecified quantity of happiness sometime. He has undertaken to accompany Mara in an activity whose temporal position partly determines what she is asking of him. Subsequent support can be generous and valuable, but it is not support during this undertaking. If Mara were indifferent about which occasion he attended and had authorized substitution, the case would change. The original case specifies that she has not.

The force of this example does not depend on a mystical value belonging to every first occasion. A first rehearsal can be trivial, while the hundredth can matter greatly. Here the occasion matters because a person has a reasonable interest in assistance through a particular uncertainty, has expressed it, and has received an assurance on which she may rely. The normative difference is relational, not merely numerical: this is the help requested and undertaken.

Nor does the case borrow urgency from someone else's mortality. Both participants survive forever. The group need not dissolve or suffer irreversible disaster. What expires is the opportunity to accompany this activity as it occurs. We should distinguish an event's ending from its participants' death. A life may contain indefinitely many endings without containing a last ending.

Fischer stresses the difference between medical immortality, which leaves accidental death possible, and true immortality, in which an individual is invulnerable to death and knows it (2022, “The Immortality Curmudgeons and their Concerns”). The example adopts the stronger condition. Its remaining vulnerabilities are vulnerabilities of action and relationship. Whether such beings could physically exist is not at issue. The relevant question is whether removing death alone also removes reasons to honor commitments on time.

## 2. Three things later goods can do

The example distinguishes three relations often compressed into the phrase “make up for.”

A later good can *compensate*: it can improve the affected person's life enough that, on some reasonable measure, the life is no worse overall. A later action can *repair*: acknowledgment, apology, and changed behavior can restore trust or settle what the wrongdoer now owes. An action can also *fulfill*: it can do what an existing claim calls for under its terms.

These relations sometimes coincide. If I owe you a transferable sum of money with no fixed payment date, a later transfer may both improve your position and fulfill the debt. But they can diverge. If I promise to accompany you tomorrow, providing a better experience next year can compensate without fulfilling. Your later forgiveness can repair our relationship without turning tomorrow's absence into the promised presence.

Why should this distinction have normative force rather than merely describe our vocabulary? Because an assurance gives the other person a basis for arranging her agency. The promise concerns what she may count on while making decisions, not just the eventual balance of her welfare. If I could unilaterally replace performance whenever I expected enough future benefit, I would retain control over a practical option that I represented as hers to rely upon. Her authority to release or amend the assurance would disappear precisely where it matters: when my preferences change.

This does not require an absolute prohibition on substitution. Emergencies can justify breaking promises. Some agreements expressly allow compensation instead of performance. Some requests are unreasonable; some purported promises are invalid. The argument requires only the modest claim that a valid, reasonable assurance normally supplies a reason that the promisor cannot erase by pointing to unspecified future generosity. In the example the cost of fulfillment is small and the proposed excuse is only the size of the remaining future. There is no independent justifying consideration.

The inference from unlimited future goods to present dispensability therefore requires an additional premise: that any reason arising now is exhausted by the total amount of value eventually delivered. Immortality does not establish that premise. It alters how much time the agent has, not who may authorize a change in what was undertaken. Even if the future contains infinitely much happiness, the proposition “Ivo did what he promised tomorrow” does not become true, and the question “What entitled him to disregard Mara's claim?” does not receive an answer.

The point is not that an unfulfilled promise creates an infinitely large disvalue. No magnitude needs to be infinite, and no finite wrong needs to outweigh every later benefit. The mistake is to use a comparison of whole lives to settle a question about the justification of a particular action. The happier history may include an unjustified breach. Evaluative superiority in one respect does not automatically confer permission in another.

## 3. From obligations to meaning

A reason to keep a promise is not yet a theory of meaningful life. Boring jobs produce deadlines, and bureaucracies can fill a lifetime with obligations whose fulfillment contributes little beyond avoiding sanctions. Why should this example bear on meaning rather than only morality?

It bears on a particular proposed obstacle to meaning. The objection under examination says that an unlimited future makes present commitments insufficiently serious. To answer that objection, it is enough to exhibit a recognizable kind of meaningful engagement whose demands remain serious without mortality. It is not necessary to prove that every obligation is meaningful or that all meaningfulness comes from obligations.

Supporting someone through an undertaking she values is a plausible example of such engagement. Its value is not merely that a rule has been obeyed. Two agents make possible a form of participation neither can supply alone: one undertakes an uncertain activity in reliance on the other's involvement, and the other makes that reliance warranted. The friend's attentive presence helps constitute the relationship through which the undertaking is lived. Such involvement is among the things for which people intelligibly organize significant parts of their lives.

The promise makes an existing relational good practically determinate. It does not manufacture significance from arbitrary wording. If Mara demanded that Ivo count dust particles tomorrow merely to test obedience, the claim to meaningful engagement would require another argument. In the performance case, the value of companionship explains why the assurance is worth giving; the assurance explains why later companionship is not an automatic substitute for this occasion. Neither the value nor its temporal specification depends on a final day of life.

An immortal need not make one promise for all eternity. A continuing commitment to be a dependable friend can be enacted in many bounded undertakings, with legitimate changes, releases, and intervals of distance. What persists is not an unchanging schedule but responsiveness to particular people as their lives develop. This supplies a possible structure of ongoing significance without demanding a fixed inventory of desires that never changes.

It would overreach to infer that such responsiveness could never become exhausting. Nor does one meaningful relationship make an otherwise dreadful existence desirable. Endless imprisonment, estrangement, or loss of motivation remain serious possibilities. The claim is conditional: so long as an agent can care about and respond to others in these ways, an indefinitely continuing life can retain this source of meaningful engagement. Mere knowledge that life will not end supplies no reason for regarding its next enactment as dispensable.

## 4. Does eternity make the claim too small?

An objector may concede that Ivo's conduct is technically wrong while denying that it has existential weight. Against infinitely many later instances of fidelity, one absence becomes negligible. Perhaps the promise remains binding in a narrow sense, but the immortal can no longer care about it with the seriousness characteristic of human life.

We should distinguish smallness in a total history from insignificance in a relationship at a time. Imagine a mortal who has supported a friend on many occasions and will do so on many more. She cannot ordinarily excuse a gratuitous betrayal by calculating that it affects a tiny fraction of their friendship. Increasing the number of good occasions strengthens the reason to value the relationship; it does not transform each new request into a negligible entry. If it did, the best-established friendships would progressively abolish reasons for fidelity.

The immortal case increases the extent of the history. It does not by itself change the object of attention. Mara is not asking whether tomorrow will form a substantial percentage of Ivo's eventual biography. She is asking whether she can count on him. An agent can answer that question without first locating the occasion's share of a completed total. Indeed, an endless life has no final completed total of lived occasions from which such a share could be calculated in the ordinary way.

There is a further objection: this response assumes that immortals preserve human practices of promising. Perhaps all their arrangements would instead permit unlimited postponement, because neither party ever runs out of life.

Such arrangements are conceivable, but their universal adoption does not follow from immortality. Participants still need to coordinate temporally extended activities. An assurance that one will join sometime is unsuitable for a joint undertaking that is happening tomorrow. They could abandon all such undertakings or cease to care about mutual reliance. They could also continue them. The latter possibility is sufficient against the claim that immortality itself destroys their significance. To exclude it, the critic needs a separate account of why an endless future must dissolve these concerns. Pointing again to the endless future repeats rather than supplies that account.

A more demanding consequentialist might deny that directed claims have independent authority at all. On this view Ivo should choose whichever history best serves the good, and compensation is not a special problem. The present argument does not settle that general dispute. It identifies where the mortality argument would then get its force: from a controversial reduction of practical reasons to an aggregate measure, together with an account of comparisons over infinite histories. Deathlessness alone is doing less argumentative work than initially appeared. Those who recognize ordinary constraints on unilateral substitution have a direct reason to reject its supposed consequence.

## 5. Seriousness without endless guilt

If tomorrow's failure can never be fulfilled afterward, must Ivo carry an ineradicable burden forever? An immortality defense that exchanges the tedium of endless repetition for endless moral haunting would offer a dubious consolation.

The distinction between fulfillment and repair prevents that exchange. A historical fact can remain unchanged while its present normative consequences change. Mara may forgive Ivo. He may apologize, repair the practical loss, and establish dependable conduct. They may later regard the incident as minor, and rightly stop dwelling on it. None of this makes him present tomorrow; none of it needs to.

The reason to act before a deadline does not have to borrow its force from a prediction of perpetual punishment afterward. Ivo should attend because Mara can reasonably rely on his assurance now. If he fails, the appropriate response afterward depends on the wrong's seriousness, its effects, and the participants' later relations. Permanent historical failure and permanent psychological distress are different claims. Defending the first as a possibility does not entail the second as a requirement.

This is also why the argument does not advise manufacturing irreversible losses to make life meaningful. The presence of a worthwhile opportunity is what matters. Its expiration explains why a later benefit cannot automatically replace the appropriate response; expiration is not itself a benefit. A world in which friends can support one another longer can be better than a world in which death routinely interrupts them. It need not become a world in which every act of support is optional.

The established scarcity reply tells us that immortality need not eliminate deadlines. The distinction developed here explains why certain deadlines can retain practical authority even when later goods are abundant and healing is possible. What eternity cannot supply is retrospective permission to substitute one's own future generosity for another person's present claim. A meaningful commitment can therefore have something final at stake without either participant having a final day.
