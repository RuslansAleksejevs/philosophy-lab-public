# Leaving Together: When the Duty to Notify Is Complete

Two volunteers are checking a transcription together. One reads the original aloud; the other corrects the text. They have explicitly agreed that either may stop whenever they wish, without giving a reason. Their arrangement is deliberately casual. It would defeat its purpose if leaving required the other person's permission. Yet it seems wrong for the reader to stop, switch to another task, and leave the editor waiting for the next sentence when a word would prevent the misunderstanding.

The editor's appropriate complaint is not that the reader left. It is that the reader left the editor operating with an obsolete understanding of their activity. But what exactly would satisfy this complaint? Must the reader secure the editor's actual awareness of the exit? Or is reasonable care in communicating enough, even when the message unexpectedly fails? These questions concern when communicative responsibility is fulfilled, not merely when the reader may stop reading.

I argue that, where unilateral exit is authorized and notice has not been waived, a participant normally owes affected partners reasonable, evidence-sensitive steps toward enabling timely revision of their understanding. This is a duty of care in communication, not a guarantee of actual access or understanding. Fulfilling it can leave a partner uninformed without leaving an unfulfilled claim to successful delivery. New evidence of misunderstanding can nevertheless generate further duties. The contribution is a specification of what notification requires: accepted coordination fixes its audience, content and urgency, while a fair division of communicative burdens fixes its completion.

## 1. The question left by permission to leave

The difference between notification and permission is already present in the literature. Gilbert's account makes concurrence a default requirement while expressly accommodating permissive background understandings, including walking together only while both wish to do so (2022, pp.401–402, 406). Alonso grounds obligations in mutually reinforced reliance and identifies warning, performance and compensation as relevant responses (2016, pp.289–290, 298). Löhr argues that respect can require notifying collaborators even when permission to leave is unnecessary and the joint intention has ended (2024, §4). These are substantial precedents. The issue here is how a notification requirement should operate when an exit entitlement has already been settled.

We should first distinguish three questions. Is the person entitled to stop contributing? Have they taken adequate steps to correct the others' understanding? Have the others actually understood? The first concerns authority over participation, the second communicative responsibility, and the third a resulting psychological state. Although ordinary departures answer all three questions at once, their answers can diverge.

In the volunteers' arrangement, the reader can stop immediately. Failure to communicate does not revive an obligation to read. Even a theorist who requires actual understanding can allow immediate cessation while maintaining a separate duty to communicate. Such a duty is not a veto over participation. The question is whether, after reasonable communicative efforts, an uninformed editor still has an unfulfilled claim against the reader, and what follows from that claim. Deliberate inattention makes this question vivid, but ordinary transmission failure is a more demanding test.

The argument concerns morally permissible, voluntary cooperation with a recognized entitlement to unilateral exit. It does not establish that all joint activities have such an entitlement. Nor does it settle contractual notice periods or duties to protect people from dangers one has created. These can supply independent reasons to continue or to arrange a replacement. The question is what remains when a participant is already entitled to cease the contribution itself.

## 2. What notice repairs

During cooperation, participants use each other's contributions as premises for practical reasoning. The editor treats a pause as a pause within the reading, not as evidence that the arrangement has ended. This can be reasonable even though the reader may leave at any time. Permission to change a condition does not make every inference based on its current existence unreasonable. A café may close early without owing me continued service; that does not make it sensible for me to treat its open sign as meaningless.

In our case, both volunteers recognize that they are coordinating with each other: the reader provides sentences for the editor to work on, and the editor treats those sentences and pauses as contributions to their undertaking. This reciprocal recognition distinguishes the case from reasonable predictions about a stranger's visible habits. Foreseeable reliance alone does not entitle an observer to updates. Here the reader accepts a role whose operation includes the editor's use of that role in deciding what to do. A brief correction enables the editor to decide whether to stop, continue alone, or seek another reader.

Why does this create a duty rather than merely a reason for kindness? Consider a proposed rule allowing each person to exploit a mutually recognized coordination practice while leaving partners to discover privately chosen changes entirely by themselves. In activities with intervals of silence or invisible work, partners would need to monitor continually whether apparent participation remained real. The rule would place the informational cost of my discretionary change on you, despite my privileged knowledge of it and my earlier acceptance of your reliance on the practice. Where correction is easy and your interpretation remains reasonable, I have no sufficient claim to impose that burden.

This argument relies on a moral premise: when people mutually recognize and use a coordination arrangement, each normally owes reasonable care in correcting the other's still-warranted practical understanding after changing a relevant condition. Cheapness alone does not establish the duty. Its ground combines the accepted relationship, privileged information about the change, and the partner's interest in directing their own activity. Its directedness follows from whose understanding the interaction supports. Its strength depends partly on the burdens reasonably allocated to each person.

The qualification about accepted arrangements matters. Suppose the volunteers explicitly agree that no departure notice is expected and that each must check whether the other remains. Silent departure is then permissible under the proposed duty. They have accepted the informational risk, not merely authorized cessation of work. Permission to exit and waiver of notice are distinct: saying “stop whenever you like” need not mean “leave me to discover that you have stopped.” Independent duties, such as preventing a danger, might survive a waiver, but they are outside the present case.

This justification need not introduce an irreducibly collective moral norm. General requirements of respect can acquire specific content through the history of an interaction. The resulting duty can also outlast the shared intention: a past contribution to another person's present misunderstanding does not disappear merely because one stops intending the joint activity. The explanatory work lies in identifying the relevant relationship and the correction it calls for, not in announcing that all partners must always say goodbye.

## 3. A standard of care, not a guaranteed result

A withdrawing participant fulfills the notification duty by taking the steps that a reasonably attentive participant, with the evidence available or reasonably obtainable, would take to make cessation clear to affected partners in time for the decisions presently at stake. Those steps must be proportionate to the urgency, likely consequences of delay, available channels, and accepted allocation of attention. The standard concerns warranted conduct. Actual access is its intended result; actual understanding is a further result. Neither is guaranteed by fulfillment.

This is stronger than permission merely to send a message. Choosing a channel requires attention to its normal use and known limitations. If both volunteers work on a voice call, saying clearly “I'm stopping now” will ordinarily suffice. If the reader knows that the editor has muted the call, speaking into it is inadequate when another effective channel is readily available. A conspicuous note in a document is also inadequate if the reader knows the editor will not consult it until tomorrow and must decide what to do now. The correction must be directed at the relevant decision, not merely deposited somewhere eventually discoverable.

Timeliness does not entail a period of continued reading. The reader can announce cessation while stopping. If communication takes longer, the task of communicating can continue after the reading has ended. In an urgent case, the necessary effort may be substantial; in an inconsequential exchange, a single clear indication may suffice. These differences concern the communicative duty, not the underlying exit entitlement.

The recipient's role also matters. Appropriate attention is the level and kind of attention that make participation on the accepted terms workable. It need not be constant vigilance. The reader must accommodate normal concentration on editing, but the editor cannot enlarge the reader's burden simply by refusing all ordinary channels. Nor does saying “I do not accept your departure” make an already understood notification incomplete. Acceptance and understanding are different outcomes.

Intentional obstruction, however, is not a decisive objection to every result-based account. Such an account might say that obstruction waives or forfeits the claim to successful communication. The more revealing case involves neither misconduct nor waiver. Suppose the reader sends a clear notice through the normal channel and obtains an ordinary delivery indication. At that instant, an unforeseeable fault freezes the editor's screen. No proportionate check would have revealed it. The editor has no actual access to the notice. On the proposed account, the reader has nonetheless fulfilled the duty owed at that time. Calling this an accessible opportunity would be inaccurate. It is reasonable care directed toward providing such an opportunity.

## 4. Fulfillment or excused failure?

The strongest rival requires successful understanding but excuses failures when reasonable efforts have been exhausted. It can recommend exactly the same immediate behavior. Why prefer fulfillment to an unfulfilled but excused duty?

The distinction matters only if an unfulfilled claim has consequences. Consider first what the editor could demand on learning about the frozen screen. They can ask what happened and request a clear statement of the reader's present participation. But they lack a complaint that the reader failed to provide something unconditionally owed. Nothing in the casual arrangement allocated unforeseeable transmission risks exclusively to the reader. The editor's ignorance is regrettable, and acknowledgment of it may be considerate. Regret about a bad result is not acknowledgment of a debt left unpaid.

Compare the original silent departure. There the reader knowingly leaves the editor without a correction that was easy to supply. The editor can properly demand acknowledgment of that failure, apart from any present need for information. Treating both cases as failures to discharge the same claim, distinguished only by blame, obscures the more basic difference: in one case the reader failed to bear their fair share of the communicative work; in the other they did not.

The reason for allocating the duty this way is not that agents can never owe outcomes beyond their control. Promises or special roles can allocate risks, and some relationships may warrant such allocation without explicit promises. But the default considered here must be justified by the coordination relationship itself. Its benefit is that participants can direct their activity using information appropriately supplied by each other. They can secure that benefit through reasonable care on both sides without making either party insure against every failure between conduct and understanding. A stronger claim would need a further justification for transferring those residual risks. Mere reliance does not supply it: relying on another's present participation is compatible with accepting ordinary uncertainty in how news of its ending reaches one.

The argument is therefore a presumption against an unearned guarantee, not a proof against every theory framed in terms of outcomes. If the rival says that exhausted reasonable efforts extinguish the editor's claim, leave no default remedial debt, and create no fault requiring acknowledgment, its verdict in the frozen-screen case is substantively the same. The disagreement then concerns how to describe the fulfilled requirement. If it instead preserves an unpaid claim or automatic remedial entitlement, it owes an account of why this risk falls on the reader. Distinguishing these alternatives is more useful than treating all result-based theories as demands for endless messaging.

There is still an important further responsibility. Suppose the reader later sees the editor waiting for another sentence. A cheap clarification is now required. The earlier notification was not retroactively inadequate. Rather, the accepted interaction remains part of the explanation of the editor's present error, and the reader now has new evidence and a new opportunity to correct it. Past participation leaves a standing susceptibility to further duties, not a continuously unpaid debt to achieve understanding.

This distinction makes the temporal structure explicit. After reasonable care, there is no outstanding requirement to keep testing the message indefinitely. Reasonable monitoring can be part of the original duty where failure is likely or consequential. Beyond that, a later demand depends on changed evidence or circumstances. Fresh knowledge of a misunderstanding can ground further work even though the earlier work was complete. Conversely, a later refusal to attend does not make further repetition necessary when it has no reasonable prospect of helping.

This account also limits remedial inferences. Violating the notice duty does not automatically establish an entitlement to renewed reading. More reading might be offered as compensation, or required by an independently justified remedial principle. But that requires an argument. It cannot follow simply by redescribing defective communication as defective exercise of the permission to stop.

## 5. Why the criterion is not merely loss prevention

Often the strongest reason to notify is obvious harm: wasted time, duplicated work, or a missed opportunity. But the criterion should not depend entirely on whether the partner finishes worse off.

Imagine that the volunteers are experimenting for amusement and the editor enjoys every minute. When the reader silently leaves, the editor continues correcting passages already heard, believing the reading remains underway. The work, enjoyment, external options, and eventual conduct are exactly as they would have been after an explicit notice. What differs is the opportunity to deliberate on accurate information about their arrangement. Later the editor learns what happened. Their complaint can still be intelligible: “You knew I was continuing on that understanding, and you could simply have told me.” It need not mean that they would have chosen differently. This judgment is contestable, but it has a rationale: by knowingly coordinating with the editor, the reader treats the editor as someone whose decisions help constitute the activity. Disregarding that decision-making role at its ending calls for criticism even if the withheld information would not have changed the decision. The criticism concerns the reader's avoidable failure to take that role seriously, not the badness of every false belief.

This is not a claim that every harmless mistaken belief generates an enforceable claim to correction. What distinguishes this case is that the mistaken understanding concerns the continuation of an interaction through which the departing person knowingly supported the partner's practical orientation. The duty is correspondingly narrow. It concerns participation and its relevant termination, not a comprehensive disclosure of the departing person's reasons, feelings, or new plans.

A defender of loss prevention can broaden “loss” to include the loss of an opportunity to decide with appropriate information. There is no objection to that vocabulary. But it incorporates the additional normative point rather than eliminating it. The account must then distinguish protected opportunities from every conceivable informational advantage, and it must explain why compensation does not invariably substitute for an easily available notice. The proposed standard supplies part of that specification: reasonable care is directed toward enabling the partner to guide their own ongoing activity. This also explains why offering money cannot normally excuse knowingly omitting an easy, timely correction. Compensation may remedy a failure without being a permission to fail in advance.

Löhr's appeal to respect is congenial to this result. The proposal is a specification within that approach, not a refutation of it. Its distinctive aim is to explain how a duty concerning another person's information can be fulfilled despite an unsuccessful message, yet generate renewed requirements after later discovery of the misunderstanding.

## 6. Boundaries and objections

Perhaps the notification duty is simply a tacit promise. The volunteers might indeed have promised notice, but the case need not include such a promise. They may explicitly reject promises of future conduct while accepting each other's present participation. The asymmetry in information then remains: one person knowingly changes a condition that the other reasonably uses in directing their activity. The justification above appeals to that relationship. Calling every resulting moral constraint a tacit promise obscures the distinction between undertaking a duty by assurance and becoming responsible through how one actually interacts.

Alternatively, perhaps a shared undertaking always includes a commitment to coordinate its own ending. That description can accommodate the proposed duty. It does not yet determine whether coordination requires consent, actual understanding, accessible information, or some ceremonial exchange. The present argument concerns precisely that determination. Nothing depends on denying that an activity may contain explicit norms for termination. Its accepted terms may strengthen, weaken, or waive the default duty proposed here.

A third objection concerns the account's apparent narrowness. Some departures are offensive although perfectly legible. A person who slams a laptop shut and walks away may leave no doubt about the termination. Does the criterion excuse that conduct? No. It identifies care in providing relevant information, not every requirement of respectful conduct. Humiliation, contempt, broken assurances, and unreasonable burdens can be wrong independently. Conflating these matters would make it impossible to ask whether a particular wrong concerns failure to inform or the manner of an unmistakable departure.

The converse is equally important. A departure may need no announcement if the activity already makes it unmistakable without placing extra burdens on the others. Participants can agree that a visible departure ends a casual game, or that a session expires automatically at a stated time. A norm insisting on verbal notification in such cases would mistake one common means for the interest it serves. The more reliably a practice makes termination publicly apparent, the less separate communicative work reasonable care requires.

Finally, a duty of notice can be exploited even without outright refusal to attend. Someone may insist on explanations, repeated meetings, or emotional reassurance as conditions of accepting an exit. Our criterion does not license these demands merely because they are described as clarification. An explanation may be owed on other grounds, but information that participation has ended normally does not require disclosure of why. Nor does adequate correction require the recipient to become pleased, reconciled, or persuaded that departure was best. Those are different outcomes, often outside the departing person's control.

## 7. The limits of communicative responsibility

An easy exit is compatible with a real interpersonal obligation. That obligation can concern the partner's ability to decide what to do after participation has ended, rather than preservation of the activity. Recognized coordination identifies who is owed care, what information matters, and when it matters. It does not by itself establish a guarantee that the information will arrive and be understood.

The proposed standard separates three matters that an ordinary successful goodbye conceals: justified communicative conduct, actual access, and understanding. Reasonable conduct can fail to produce the latter two without leaving an unpaid claim to success. Conversely, a sender's later discovery of confusion can create further responsibilities even after earlier duties were fulfilled. This is why completion need not mean immunity from every subsequent demand.

The argument does not establish a new category of collective obligation, and familiar notions of reasonable care do much of its work. It offers a limited account of their application where cooperation is deliberately easy to end. Partners must take care with the understandings their interaction supports, while bearing neither an automatic guarantee of successful communication nor an automatic entitlement to disregard a misunderstanding they later discover.
