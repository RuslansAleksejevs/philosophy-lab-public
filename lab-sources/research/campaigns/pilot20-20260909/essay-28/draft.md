# Leaving Together: Notice Without a Veto

Two volunteers are checking a transcription together. One reads the original aloud; the other corrects the text. They have explicitly agreed that either may stop whenever they wish, without giving a reason. Their arrangement is deliberately casual. It would defeat its purpose if leaving required the other person's permission. Yet it seems wrong for the reader to stop, switch to another task, and leave the editor waiting for the next sentence when a word would prevent the misunderstanding.

The editor's appropriate complaint is not that the reader left. It is that the reader left the editor operating with an obsolete understanding of their activity. But what exactly would satisfy this complaint? Must the reader secure the editor's actual awareness of the exit? If so, the editor can apparently block a blameless exit by refusing to acknowledge anything the reader says. Is sending a message sufficient? If so, a message placed where the editor predictably will not see it may count as discharging a duty it conspicuously fails to serve.

This is a problem about the content of a familiar obligation, rather than whether joint action creates an unfamiliar kind of agent. I argue that, where unilateral exit is authorized, a participant normally owes affected partners a reasonable opportunity to revise their practical understanding of the activity. The duty is neither a duty merely to emit a notice nor a duty to achieve another person's awareness. Its completion depends on the accessibility of the correction under appropriate attention by its recipient. This account identifies a constraint on the norms of shared action: protecting one participant's informational position must not quietly transfer authority over another participant's continued involvement.

## 1. The question left by permission to leave

The difference between notification and permission is already present in the literature. Gilbert's account makes concurrence a default requirement while expressly accommodating permissive background understandings, including walking together only while both wish to do so (2022, pp.401–402, 406). Alonso grounds obligations in mutually reinforced reliance and identifies warning, performance and compensation as relevant responses (2016, pp.289–290, 298). Löhr argues that respect can require notifying collaborators even when permission to leave is unnecessary and the joint intention has ended (2024, §4). These are substantial precedents. The issue here is how a notification requirement should operate when an exit entitlement has already been settled.

We should first distinguish three questions. Is the person entitled to stop contributing? Have they taken adequate steps to correct the others' understanding? Have the others actually understood? The first concerns authority over participation, the second communicative responsibility, and the third a resulting psychological state. Although ordinary departures answer all three questions at once, their answers can diverge.

In the volunteers' arrangement, the reader can stop immediately. A failure to communicate does not make the stopped reading continue, nor does it revive an obligation to read. It makes the departure defective in another respect. Conversely, the reader can communicate adequately while the editor fails to notice. The possibility of this second divergence is less obvious, but essential. If it is excluded, a recipient who has no authority to refuse permission can nonetheless obtain equivalent power by withholding attention.

The argument concerns morally permissible, voluntary cooperation with a recognized entitlement to unilateral exit. It does not establish that all joint activities have such an entitlement. Nor does it settle contractual notice periods or duties to protect people from dangers one has created. These can supply independent reasons to continue or to arrange a replacement. The question is what remains when a participant is already entitled to cease the contribution itself.

## 2. What notice repairs

During cooperation, participants use each other's contributions as premises for practical reasoning. The editor treats a pause as a pause within the reading, not as evidence that the arrangement has ended. This can be reasonable even though the reader may leave at any time. Permission to change a condition does not make every inference based on its current existence unreasonable. A café may close early without owing me continued service; that does not make it sensible for me to treat its open sign as meaningless.

In our case, the reader has knowingly participated in establishing the editor's way of interpreting the pauses. This matters. The editor is not merely making predictions about a stranger who happens to be nearby. The reader has accepted a role in an activity whose successful operation includes the editor's interpretation of that role. When the reader exits, they withdraw the basis for that interpretation. A brief correction enables the editor to decide whether to stop, continue alone, or seek another reader.

Why does this create a duty rather than merely a reason for kindness? Consider a proposed rule allowing each person to exploit a mutually recognized coordination practice while leaving partners to discover privately chosen changes entirely by themselves. In activities with intervals of silence or invisible work, partners would need to monitor continually whether apparent participation remained real. The rule would place the informational cost of my discretionary change on you, despite my privileged knowledge of it and my earlier acceptance of your reliance on the practice. Where correction is easy and your interpretation remains reasonable, I have no sufficient claim to impose that burden.

The relevant protected interest is your control over how your own activity continues. Notice restores your opportunity to exercise that control. It does not give you control over whether I continue. This difference explains both the directedness and the limit of the duty. You are its particular beneficiary because your practical understanding was supported by our interaction; unrelated observers are not entitled to reports of all my intentions. Its limit follows because the same agency that grounds your claim to an opportunity to revise also grounds my retained authority to leave.

This justification need not introduce an irreducibly collective moral norm. General requirements of respect can acquire specific content through the history of an interaction. The resulting duty can also outlast the shared intention: a past contribution to another person's present misunderstanding does not disappear merely because one stops intending the joint activity. The explanatory work lies in identifying the relevant relationship and the correction it calls for, not in announcing that all partners must always say goodbye.

## 3. A criterion of adequate correction

Call the relevant duty a duty of accessible correction. A withdrawing participant discharges it when they make the termination of their participation reasonably available to each affected partner through the established channels of the activity, with the clarity and salience needed for a partner attending as the activity reasonably calls for. If an established channel has foreseeably failed, reasonable use of an available alternative may be required. The standard is sensitive to what the departing person knows or should know, the costs of communication, the urgency of revision, and the participants' accepted arrangements.

The phrase “attending as the activity reasonably calls for” is crucial. It prevents two distortions. The sender cannot count a barely audible remark as adequate when the activity predictably absorbs the recipient's hearing. But the recipient cannot demand successful delivery while deliberately refusing every normal means of receiving it. Appropriate attention is not perfect vigilance. It is the level and kind of attention that make participation on the accepted terms workable.

Return to the volunteers. If both work on a voice call, saying clearly “I'm stopping now” will ordinarily be adequate. If the reader knows that the editor has temporarily muted the call to answer a question, speaking into the silence is not enough when a conspicuous message in their shared document is easy. If the editor hears the notice but declares that it does not count until formally acknowledged, nothing about the original exit entitlement supports this additional condition. Awareness is already present. If the editor instead deliberately silences every channel to keep the reader responsible for reaching them, their refusal cannot enlarge the reader's obligation.

These judgments are not three unrelated exceptions to a rule of successful notification. They follow from the same object of the duty: an opportunity for informed revision under a fair division of communicative responsibility. The reader must supply an accessible correction; the editor must not turn access into an impossible demand by declining the ordinary conditions of receiving it.

This does not make actual uptake irrelevant. An explicit response is strong evidence that correction succeeded. Conversely, a confused question can show that a superficially adequate notice was not clear enough. If the reader can resolve the confusion with two words, a reasonable opportunity has not yet been fully provided merely because a message was technically delivered. Evidence about uptake informs the assessment of accessibility. It does not replace accessibility with a requirement that the recipient certify satisfaction.

There is therefore no algorithm that makes a sent message conclusive in every setting. Yet the absence of an algorithm does not collapse the criterion into whatever the recipient demands. We can ask concrete questions: which channel was being used, what was known about the recipient's attention, what change was conveyed, what alternatives were available, and who produced the remaining obstacle? Those questions constrain the judgment independently of the recipient's desire to prolong the activity.

## 4. Why actual knowledge is too strong

Suppose the reader sends an unambiguous notice through the usual channel, checks that it was delivered, and stops. Unknown to the reader, the editor's screen freezes at that moment. Must the reader keep trying until the editor knows?

In ordinary circumstances, no. Making responsibility depend on actual knowledge would assign the sender risks that cannot be managed without repeated demands for confirmation. Such demands would themselves consume the attention that makes easy cooperation attractive. More fundamentally, certainty of uptake is not something a speaker can produce alone. Communication distributes work between its participants. It is unreasonable to treat one participant as solely responsible for an outcome requiring the other's cognitive participation, especially when the other has no entitlement to prevent the underlying decision.

There can nevertheless be a new duty when circumstances change. If the reader later sees that the editor is still waiting, the previous reasonable notice does not license knowingly maintaining that misunderstanding when correction is easy. This is not retroactive failure. A duty may have been adequately discharged at one time, while newly available information grounds a further requirement. Distinguishing these matters avoids two bad conclusions: that all earlier efforts were inadequate whenever a misunderstanding survives, or that one successful attempt immunizes the sender against all subsequent evidence.

What if the editor continues pretending not to understand? Further attempts need not continue indefinitely. The question remains whether the editor has a reasonable opportunity to understand, not whether they choose to take it. Repetition may be required where genuine confusion is plausible; it loses its point when the remaining obstacle is a refusal to accept a decision that does not require acceptance.

This distinction also determines what can properly be demanded after a defective departure. An editor left waiting may demand clarification, acknowledgment of the failure, and sometimes repair of losses. They cannot infer a right to renewed reading solely from the fact that notice was owed. A remedy should restore the interest the violated duty protected. Enforcing performance would protect a different interest—continued participation—that the parties expressly declined to secure.

## 5. Why the criterion is not merely loss prevention

Often the strongest reason to notify is obvious harm: wasted time, duplicated work, or a missed opportunity. But the criterion should not depend entirely on whether the partner finishes worse off.

Imagine that the volunteers are experimenting for amusement and the editor enjoys every minute. When the reader silently leaves, the editor continues correcting passages already heard, believing the reading remains underway. The work, enjoyment, and opportunities are exactly as they would have been after an explicit notice. Later the editor learns what happened. Their complaint can still be intelligible: “You knew I was continuing on that understanding, and you could simply have told me.” It need not mean that they would have chosen differently. A person's authority to choose is not exhausted by the difference that additional information happens to make to their eventual conduct.

This is not a claim that every harmless mistaken belief generates an enforceable claim to correction. What distinguishes this case is that the mistaken understanding concerns the continuation of an interaction through which the departing person knowingly supported the partner's practical orientation. The duty is correspondingly narrow. It concerns participation and its relevant termination, not a comprehensive disclosure of the departing person's reasons, feelings, or new plans.

A defender of loss prevention can broaden “loss” to include the loss of an opportunity to decide with appropriate information. There is no objection to that vocabulary. But it incorporates the additional normative point rather than eliminating it. The account must then distinguish protected opportunities from every conceivable informational advantage, and it must explain why compensation does not invariably substitute for an easily available notice. Accessible correction supplies that specification: its immediate object is enabling the partner to direct their own ongoing activity, not balancing a ledger after the fact.

Löhr's appeal to respect is congenial to this result. The present proposal is a specification within that approach, not a refutation of it. Its contribution is to connect the informational object of respect to a stopping condition for communicative responsibility. The receiver's agency supports an opportunity to understand; it does not support a veto exercised by refusing to understand.

## 6. Boundaries and objections

Perhaps the notification duty is simply a tacit promise. The volunteers might indeed have promised notice, but the case need not include such a promise. They may explicitly reject promises of future conduct while accepting each other's present participation. The asymmetry in information then remains: one person knowingly changes a condition that the other reasonably uses in directing their activity. The justification above appeals to that relationship. Calling every resulting moral constraint a tacit promise obscures the distinction between undertaking a duty by assurance and becoming responsible through how one actually interacts.

Alternatively, perhaps a shared undertaking always includes a commitment to coordinate its own ending. That description can accommodate the proposed duty. It does not yet determine whether coordination requires consent, actual understanding, accessible information, or some ceremonial exchange. The present argument concerns precisely that determination. Nothing depends on denying that an activity may contain explicit norms for termination; what matters is preventing those norms from contradicting a recognized entitlement to unilateral exit without argument.

A third objection concerns the account's apparent narrowness. Some departures are offensive although perfectly legible. A person who slams a laptop shut and walks away may leave no doubt about the termination. Does the criterion excuse that conduct? No. It identifies the informational duty and its completion, not every requirement of respectful conduct. Humiliation, contempt, broken assurances, and unreasonable burdens can be wrong independently. Conflating these matters would make it impossible to ask whether a particular wrong concerns failure to inform or the manner of an unmistakable departure.

The converse is equally important. A departure may need no announcement if the activity already makes it unmistakable without placing extra burdens on the others. Participants can agree that a visible departure ends a casual game, or that a session expires automatically at a stated time. A norm insisting on verbal notification in such cases would mistake one common means for the interest it serves. The more reliably a practice makes termination publicly apparent, the less separate communicative work it requires.

Finally, a duty of notice can be exploited even without outright refusal to attend. Someone may insist on explanations, repeated meetings, or emotional reassurance as conditions of accepting an exit. Our criterion does not license these demands merely because they are described as clarification. An explanation may be owed on other grounds, but information that participation has ended normally does not require disclosure of why. Nor does adequate correction require the recipient to become pleased, reconciled, or persuaded that departure was best. Those are different outcomes, often outside the departing person's control.

## 7. Shared action and the freedom to stop

An easy exit is compatible with a real interpersonal obligation. The obligation need not preserve the activity. It can instead concern restoring a partner's ability to choose what to do after the activity has changed. This remains a directed duty even when its fulfillment is brief, when departure is permitted without justification, and when it survives the intention that originally organized the interaction.

The difficult point is its completion. A bare transmission standard gives departing participants too little responsibility for foreseeable communicative failure. An actual-awareness standard gives recipients too much power over a responsibility they can prevent the sender from discharging. A reasonable opportunity for informed revision occupies the defensible middle: it requires clarity, accessible channels and responsiveness to known obstacles, while assigning recipients their share of the work of receiving information.

Shared action brings our practical reasoning into contact. Its ending can leave that contact uneven: one person has moved on while another still reasons from the former arrangement. Correcting the mismatch matters. But the correction is owed so that each can direct their own activity. It would defeat that rationale to turn it into a means by which either participant directs whether the other is allowed to leave.
