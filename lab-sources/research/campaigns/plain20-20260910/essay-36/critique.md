# Critique — essay 36

## Verdict and reconstruction

The narrow thesis is plausible, and the essay is already intelligible to a reader outside aesthetics. It is not yet a substantial new account of attention. Its best potential contribution is a precise limit on what repeated listening can support: familiarity and accurate identification do not by themselves show that this hearing supports praise of this musical feature. Revision should make that result explicit and defend it against harder cases.

The argument is: (1) new information is not necessary for attending to a familiar work; (2) an encounter can instead remain guided by the work's relevant details; (3) undergoing their temporal arrangement is an activity that a stored description does not perform; (4) therefore predictable encounters can have a good of their own, while mere repetition does not establish attentive acquaintance. Mira and Leon illustrate the distinction. The essay carefully limits the result to a respect in which attention or appreciation is claimed, allows memory and comfort, and denies that its thought experiment is a public diagnostic test. Those qualifications should be retained.

## Argument: priorities for revision

### 1. Separate the condition of attention from its value

The premise that hearing differs from knowing a description establishes a difference of activities. It does not yet establish that the extra activity is valuable. Many repeatable activities are distinct from their descriptions without deserving repetition. The dance comparison makes the value intuitive but does not supply the missing premise.

Add a short positive argument: when a passage is worth experiencing for how its parts develop together, accurately knowing its structure does not exhaust that reason because the valued thing includes the temporally extended experience. This is conditional on the passage and the experience having value; no general duty or guarantee follows. State whether “achievement” means merely successfully attending or doing something especially demanding. Mira need not improve, struggle, or deserve credit for each effortless hearing. “An attentive experience” may carry the actual claim more accurately than “fresh achievement.”

### 2. Actual influence is too broad; counterfactual sensitivity is too narrow unless qualified

A familiar recording can regulate Leon's breathing or mood while he works. His response is actually influenced by its pace, and an altered ending could disturb him. That does not necessarily mean he attends to the ending. The text blocks this by requiring that he be “actually following something,” but this risks putting the conclusion inside the criterion.

Explain following with the example: Mira experiences the second rise as answering the first, and the low note as holding back the ending. Leon's breathing might track tempo without those musical relations entering his experience. The distinction then concerns experienced relations, not merely causal influence. This is an explication, not a reductive theory, and the manuscript can say so.

The reverse case also matters. Mira may hear the actual ending attentively yet fail to respond differently to a small edit because two versions sound equally unsettled to her. Or an abnormal distraction could mask the difference. The draft already mentions coughing but not the former case, where sensitivity to an alteration of a relevant detail need not change the experienced property being appreciated. Use the edit only as defeasible illustration. Make clear that responsiveness does not require every physical change to produce a new feeling or judgment, even when a changed detail contributes to the passage's character.

### 3. The strongest objection currently slides from attention to judgment

Leon detects the shortened note but repeats his old description of the ending. He could nonetheless hear the altered ending and reasonably think the whole remains unsettled: several features may sustain the same character. He could also feel a change but describe both versions with the same coarse adjective. Failure to change a verbal answer therefore does not establish idle attention. The later caveat about public tests helps, but the central example still invites that inference.

Strengthen the objection by stipulating what the imagined case is meant to isolate: one listener hears the note's role in the passage, the other compares note identities without experiencing their relation to the ending. Acknowledge that unchanged judgment alone cannot tell which occurred. Also avoid calling the second listener shallow overall; accurate comparison is a real form of musical attention. The essay's respect-specific response is appropriate once the respect is fixed independently of whether the person passed the example.

### 4. Distinguish present attention from old justification

Leon may have excellent grounds from yesterday for saying that this recording has an unsettled ending. Inattention tonight does not remove those grounds. The essay mostly knows this but phrases its argument as concerning “the object of the claimed appreciation,” which can refer to the work rather than this encounter.

State the conclusion precisely: tonight's unattended play adds no new experiential support for his report that he followed the ending tonight. It need not undermine his judgment about the work. Moreover, an attentive repeat may sustain familiarity without being an independent additional reason, so avoid implying that ten attentive visits automatically supply ten separate confirmations. The interesting result concerns what an appeal to repeated encounters establishes, not a count of evidence units.

## Originality, significance, and literature

The significance is modest but real: the essay separates repeat exposure, discovery, appreciation, and warrant for claims of acquaintance. Its sharpest possible payoff is the asymmetry between stable judgment and continuing attention: neither a changed judgment nor a detected discrepancy is necessary or sufficient for the relevant attention. Develop that rather than presenting a comprehensive criterion.

The background is strongly anticipated by Dewey. Chapter III, paragraphs beginning “The end, the terminus” and the preceding discussion of a self-sufficient result, also anticipate the argument that an aesthetic ending cannot be detached from the process. Acknowledge this debt in the section on the known ending. The contribution must be the narrower discrimination and its consequences for repeat-listening claims, not the general contrast between knowledge and undergone form. [Dewey, chapter III](https://ddong.narod.ru/deweyen2/txt03.htm).

Mac Cumhaill's §2 already treats artistic form as leading perception and feeling; §5 makes present perception integral to a kind of recollection. The essay uses this accurately, but claiming a new work-guidance idea would overstate its distance. Its respect-specific and no-discovery emphasis is potentially a clarification, not established novelty. [Mac Cumhaill, §§2, 5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-020-00472-y).

A limited search also found O'Brien's 2019 primary empirical study, whose abstract explains underestimated repeat enjoyment partly through unnoticed discoveries. This provides an actual nearby explanation from which to distinguish the philosophical possibility defended here. It does not claim that all value requires novelty, and should not be made into that opponent. Full paper was not read. [O'Brien, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30896242/). Further literature work may alter the originality estimate; this search does not certify originality.

## Concrete language review

1. **Opening:** “You know a song so well that you can hear its next phrase before it arrives. Does this help you listen, or does it mean that you have begun to listen to yourself?” The situation is immediate. “Listen to yourself” is slightly ambiguous: self-absorption or inner speech could be intended. Optional clearer version: “You know a song so well that you can imagine its next phrase before it plays. Does that help you hear the music, or can the memory take the place of listening?” This preserves the inviting question without manufactured drama.

2. **Central thesis:** “Memory may prepare the route. It must not make the current journey dispensable.” These short metaphors conceal the condition the reader needs. Replace with: “Memory can help us follow the music. But if we enjoy only what we remember, we may stop hearing what is playing.” Keep the more precise claim about relevant details immediately before or after it.

3. **Attention section:** “We need to distinguish a settled result from a closed process.” The noun contrast is compact but asks readers to translate both terms. Replace with: “Mira can reach the same judgment each time while still listening closely each time.” Then explain that stability does not show she stopped listening. The next sentence currently repeats the abstraction.

4. **Positive argument:** “The distinction is between knowing the arrangement and undergoing its ordered presentation.” “Undergoing its ordered presentation” sounds academic and underspecifies the actual experience. Replace with: “Knowing which sounds come next is different from hearing them arrive, waiting through the pause, and feeling the ending settle.” This also keeps the temporal point concrete.

5. **Strongest objection:** “His attention to identity is working; his attention to the ending's character may be idle.” “Identity” sounds like personal identity, and “idle” personifies attention without explaining it. Replace with: “He notices which note has changed. But he may not hear what that change does to the ending.” Add the substantive qualification above: keeping the same description does not prove this failure.

6. **Comfort and authority:** “What would weaken the evidential value of later visits is a willingness to count each one as further confirmation even when her settled admiration prevented any real looking.” This combines the claim, mental cause, and exception in a long sentence. Replace with: “A later visit adds little support if she hardly looks. She cannot count the visit as another careful examination merely because she still admires the painting.” This also avoids assuming that stable admiration itself prevents perception.

7. **Conclusion:** “A known work can still lead us through an experience that knowing it does not perform on our behalf.” The meaning is recoverable, but knowledge “performing” an experience is artificial. Replace with: “Even when we know a piece well, listening lets us experience its movement again. Knowing the piece does not do that for us.” Consider reducing repeated route, journey, and work-to-do metaphors throughout.

Prioritize the thesis and objection rewrites, then revise the causal language and evidence claim throughout. Keep the concrete invented recording; it does more explanatory work than additional abstract labels. Do not simplify away the distinction between a stable judgment, a changed experience, and accurate detection of a note.
