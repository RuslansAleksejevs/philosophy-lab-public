# Critique: What a Complete Explanation Must Leave Open

## Strongest reconstruction

The essay makes a conditional argument about explanatory completeness. Its central claim is not that chance and free choice are impossible, nor that every ordinary explanation must explain its own explanans. It is this: if a theory promises that no contingent fact is unexplained, then explaining a broad outcome does not suffice when the explanation treats distinct possible realizations alike. If Anna and Ben were genuine alternatives, an account of why *someone* won does not thereby explain why Anna won. Moving the split to an earlier contingent selector state locates the difference but does not meet a promise to explain every contingent fact. The theory must explain that earlier difference, show it necessary, deny that it is real, or admit a basic contingency.

This is the strongest form of the argument because it limits the conclusion to theories that announce a universal scope. The essay also distinguishes four things that are often blurred: explaining a process, explaining a coarse-grained result, explaining a contrast between possible results, and explaining every contingency in the history. Its genuine contribution is the proposed audit of completeness claims: preserve the differences a theory recognizes while tracing an explanation backward, and require an explicit account of where the trace stops. The contrast-class framework, the idea that an answer must favor one alternative, and the regress-blocking point about ordinary causal explanation are established background. The originality, if any, lies in applying those ideas as a constraint on metaphysical promises of total explanation and in separating relocation of a contingency from its elimination.

## Main philosophical problems

The largest gap is that the essay does not establish that an unanswered contrast is itself a contingent fact within the strong theory's scope. It says that the stronger promise leaves “nothing about the actual result” without a reason, but this phrase is introduced as a reading rather than derived from “every contingent fact has an explanation.” Suppose the exclusive outcomes are A and B. The actual fact A already entails not-B. A defender can say that the stochastic mechanism explains A, and therefore the whole actual fact is explained, even though no fact raises A's probability over B's. The essay answers that this principle is “weaker,” but it has not yet shown that it fails to explain a fact rather than merely failing to answer an additional why-question. This is precisely the contested bridge. Without an argument for it, the conditional risks becoming: if completeness includes an answer to every genuine contrast, it includes an answer to every genuine contrast.

The essay should therefore state and defend a **difference-coverage premise** in ordinary language. One candidate is: when A and B are mutually exclusive genuine possibilities, a complete explanation of the obtaining of A must contain some explanatory feature that would not play the same role in an otherwise matched B-history. That premise is stronger than the claim that A has some cause. Defend it by explaining why explanatory completeness is not closed under arbitrary coarse-graining and why an explanation of A must explain the actualizing difference, not merely a property shared by every possible realization. Then concede that this is a substantive account of completeness, rather than the uncontested meaning of the principle of sufficient reason.

The second problem is an equivocation among “favor,” “basis,” “actual causal route,” and “reason.” The symmetry test initially asks whether the account treats the chances, causal roles, and reasons alike. The essay then lists “an actual causal route that differs” as something that can explain the contrast. But any genuinely random token outcome will have a different token-level realization: a photon went through one detector, a radioactive atom decayed at this time, or the selector entered A. If the actual route counts, a stochastic causal explanation can pass the test without favoring Anna in advance. If the route merely restates the outcome, it does not pass. The essay needs a criterion for when a token causal difference explains rather than redescribes the result.

A counterexample brings out the issue. A radioactive atom has a constant chance of decaying during each short interval. It decays in interval 10 rather than interval 11, triggers a detector, and opens Anna's box. The stochastic law, atom, decay, detector path, and mechanism give a detailed causal history of Anna's prize. No earlier state probabilistically favors interval 10 over interval 11. Does the decay event explain the prize but fail to explain its own timing? Probably yes. But does the stochastic law plus the actual decay explain the timing, or does citing the decay merely repeat that it occurred then? The essay cannot decide this by symmetry alone. It must say what kind of explanatory dependence is required at the last stochastic transition.

The third problem is that the test may mishandle symmetric deterministic systems. Imagine a perfectly symmetric pencil balanced on its tip. An arbitrarily tiny perturbation sends it left. At a coarse level, the laws and setup treat left and right alike; at the token level, a perturbation distinguishes them. The explanation need not contain a preference, purpose, or appreciable probability shift. The draft gestures toward “some other feature whose explanatory role can be stated,” but that phrase does most of the work. A revision should separate three questions: whether an explanation discriminates between alternatives, whether it makes one more probable, and whether it supplies a sufficient reason in Leibniz's demanding sense. These conditions are not equivalent.

The fourth problem concerns agency. Clarke's view may be stronger than “the shared purpose supplies the missing reason.” He can say the agent herself, through the exercise of active power, is the contrastive source of left rather than right even when her motives do not favor left. The essay allows a “basic act of selection,” but describes this mainly as an unexplained stopping point. That description assumes that explaining an event requires a prior contrastive reason. An agent-causal view denies that assumption: the agent causes this choice, and the causal explanation is not a prior motive. The revision should confront this answer directly. It can still argue that the choice remains contingent under a reason-demanding form of PSR, but it should not treat “no prior favoring reason” as equivalent to “no explanation” without addressing agent causation.

Finally, the essay moves too quickly between claims made by “a metaphysical theory,” “a universal principle of explanation,” and Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason. Those targets differ. A causal completeness thesis, an explanation-of-every-fact thesis, and a reason-for-every-contingent-difference thesis impose different burdens. The argument becomes sharper if it defines three versions and shows exactly which inferences each licenses. As written, the conclusion that the weaker principle cannot “exclude arbitrary selections among possible worlds” is plausible, but it needs an explicit inference: a common reason for creating some eligible world is compatible with each eligible world, so it cannot entail or differentially support the actual world; therefore uniqueness requires a further premise.

## Significance, originality, literature, and accuracy

The topic is significant because cosmological, theological, and grounding arguments often shift between explaining that there is some realization and explaining why the actual realization obtains. The essay offers a useful diagnostic for that shift. Its result will remain modest unless it defends the difference-coverage premise, but modesty is appropriate: showing that a metaphysical inference requires a stronger PSR is worthwhile even if the stronger PSR is not established.

Originality is uncertain. Van Fraassen explicitly says that a why-answer must favor its topic against its contrast class (*The Scientific Image*, chapter 5, printed pp. 126–130), and his account makes the contrast class and relevance relation constitutive of the question (pp. 141–147). The essay acknowledges this. The step from contrastive explanation to auditing total-explanation claims may be a useful synthesis, but the packet does not establish that it is new. A revision should avoid calling it novel and should either check primary work on contrastive PSR and stochastic explanation or describe the contribution as a clearly formulated application.

The source attributions I could verify are accurate. Leibniz's *Fourth Paper* §§1–6, 13, 15, 18–19 supports both the demand for a reason in choice and the denial that certain relabelled states are distinct. Clarke's *Fourth Reply* §§1–2 supports the claim that agents can act when the ways of acting are indifferent. Van Fraassen supports the contrast-class discussion, although his account also shows that “favoring” needs further analysis and does not reduce to probability raising. Lipton's official extract supports only the limited regress point used in the draft. The full Lipton article was inaccessible, so no broader attribution should be made.

## Language review

The opening is clear and immediate:

> “A machine gives a prize to one of two people. Its rules guarantee a winner, give each person an equal chance, and use a genuinely random process. Anna wins.”

An unfamiliar reader can see the problem at once. The last question, “Have we explained everything about the result?”, is useful because the next paragraph divides its meanings. An optional improvement would replace “use a genuinely random process,” which presupposes a disputed notion, with “the process contains no bias or earlier feature that favors either person.” Genuine randomness can then be introduced when the argument needs it.

The thesis is accurate but dense:

> “If an explanation claims to leave no contingent fact unexplained, it must keep track of the real differences that its broad descriptions leave open.”

“Keep track,” “real differences,” and “leave open” each need interpretation, so the sentence sounds clearer than the underlying claim is. A simpler and more exact rewrite is: “Suppose a theory says that it explains every contingent fact. If Anna and Ben really could have won, the theory must say whether it explains why Anna won, not only why one of them won.”

The symmetry test is the hardest passage:

> “Rather, ask whether exchanging the outcomes leaves their entire treatment by the offered explanation unchanged. That includes their chances, the relevant causal roles, and any reasons for preferring them.”

“Entire treatment” is abstract, and the list groups different standards together. Rewrite: “Imagine the same account ending with Ben's victory. Does anything in the account play a different explanatory role? A bias might. So might a distinct causal event. Equal prior chances, by themselves, do not.” This also creates space to explain which token causal events count.

The practical test compresses four large options into one sentence:

> “At each step ask whether that earlier difference is explained, declared necessary, denied to be a real difference, or accepted without further explanation.”

The list is important, but “declared” can sound as if assertion is enough. Rewrite: “At each step, ask what the theory says about the earlier split. Does it explain the split? Does it argue that only one option was possible? Does it show that the two options describe the same reality? Or does it stop and accept the split as basic?”

The strongest objection is introduced clearly but answered too abstractly:

> “Perhaps the fact that Anna won is fully explained by the process, and ‘rather than Ben’ simply imposes an optional demand. Every fact can have an explanation even if not every contrast does.”

This passage is already natural English. The problem is argumentative, not stylistic. Preserve it, then add a concrete reply before using “weaker” and “stronger” readings: “Because only one person can win, ‘Anna won’ already excludes Ben's victory. The issue is whether explaining that fact requires an account of what made the excluded possibility fail. My argument assumes that a complete explanation does; the defender denies it.” That tells the reader exactly where disagreement remains.

One later sentence hides the inference in abstract nouns:

> “The consequence is not verbal. A weak principle may support the conclusion that every event has a producing source. It does not, on that basis alone, exclude arbitrary selections among possible worlds, identify a uniquely preferred world, or show that all real differences reflect differences in their explanatory grounds.”

Rewrite: “This difference changes what we can infer. If the principle says only that every event has a cause, it may allow several worlds that the cause treats alike. It cannot by itself show why this world exists, why it was preferred, or why every difference in reality comes from a difference in reasons.”

The conclusion is readable, but “shortcut to completeness” remains metaphorical:

> “What is ruled out is a particular shortcut to completeness.”

Rewrite: “The argument rules out one way of defending a complete explanation: explain a broad outcome, then treat that as an explanation of every specific result.”

## Prioritized revision

1. State the difference-coverage premise and defend it against the objection that explaining the fact A already explains the exclusive fact A-rather-than-B.
2. Separate causal production, probability raising, contrastive discrimination, and sufficient reason. Use the radioactive-decay case to say which standard the argument needs.
3. Revise the symmetry test so that it distinguishes a genuine token causal difference from a renamed outcome, and explain why the proposed criterion is not simply van Fraassen's established view.
4. Address agent causation as Clarke's strongest reply: an agent may be offered as the cause of a selection even without a prior favoring motive.
5. Define the exact completeness thesis under attack and narrow the conclusion to the metaphysical inferences that thesis cannot support.
6. Apply the language rewrites above, especially to the thesis, symmetry test, and answer to the strongest objection. The prose is mostly accessible; its main readability problem is that key terms conceal unsettled philosophical choices.
