# Critique of “Leaving Care Without Leaving People”

## Overall verdict

The essay identifies a real and important moral distinction: a successor may have an immediate duty not to abandon a dependent person even though an earlier generation acted wrongly by making that successor indispensable. Its best contribution is the institutional claim that creators of durable care arrangements should provide not only resources for care but also a safe route by which carers can transfer tasks. The manuscript is strongest when it shows why continuity and exit are not simple opposites: training, overlap, records, shared knowledge, and a wider circle of trusted carers can protect both the person receiving care and the carer's control over their life.

The draft is not yet ready as a fully defended original argument. Its opening case makes the first plan so plainly coercive that the central conclusion follows from a familiar ban on hereditary forced labor. The essay then moves between several different objects—an occupation, particular care tasks, a loving relationship, parenthood, and an emergency rescue duty—without giving a rule for when transfer must be possible and when continuity defeats it. The feasibility qualifier, “where reasonable alternatives are available,” does most of the normative work but remains undefined. The revision should narrow the thesis, identify its moral ground, and test it in a case where the original assignment is not already obviously unjust.

## Reconstruction of the argument

The strongest version of the argument is:

1. Dependent people can have claims to continuing care, and those claims can generate immediate duties for whoever is presently indispensable.
2. An immediate duty created by dependency does not retroactively justify the choices that made a particular person indispensable.
3. People who design a durable institution can foresee that concentrating skills, authority, or relationships in one carer will make exit dangerous.
4. When those designers can preserve adequate continuity at reasonable cost while reducing that concentration, failing to do so foreseeably restricts the carer's self-direction and exposes the dependent person to avoidable risk.
5. Therefore, designers of durable care institutions have a pro tanto duty to create a safe capacity for role transfer: enough trained people, shared knowledge, transition time, authority, and institutional responsibility for relief.

This is more precise than saying simply that future people should be “free to leave.” The proposed object of inheritance is a resilient care capacity. A resilient system can survive a change of personnel without abandoning the people whose lives depend on it. This is the manuscript's genuine contribution. The broader ideas that carers require public support, that caring attachments can be exploited, that successors need capacities to discharge inherited duties, and that nonidentity does not erase all complaints are established themes rather than new results.

## Main philosophical problems

### 1. The opening proves too much by stipulation

The founders decree that daughters must care for life and may never hand the work to anyone. This combines sex discrimination, inherited status, occupational coercion, and denial of exit. Almost any plausible liberal, republican, egalitarian, or care-ethical view will condemn it before considering the special structure of care. The case therefore does not show that creators of a valuable care institution owe a distinctive handover capacity. It shows that they may not impose hereditary forced labor.

The revision needs a harder case. Suppose adults initially volunteer for a well-supported ten-year care role, residents form deep relationships with them, and the institution gradually lets its backup training lapse. A carer later has a serious but non-emergency reason to leave. The institution could rebuild shared capacity, but only through moderate cost and some distress during transition. Here there is no hereditary conscription, and the essay must explain why the institution now owes a transfer route. If the principle handles this case, it has content beyond the ban on coercion.

### 2. The moral ground is underspecified

The essay appeals in turn to meaningful choice, control of one's life, unfair assignment, and the impropriety of using future need to acquire authority. These ideas point toward different theories. An autonomy argument asks whether the carer retains an adequate range of valuable options. An anti-domination argument asks whether another party holds uncontrolled power over the carer's role. An exploitation argument asks whether others unfairly benefit from the carer's constrained response to need. A distributive argument asks whether unavoidable care burdens are shared fairly.

These grounds can support different conclusions. Good pay and a fixed term might answer an autonomy objection but leave a worker dominated by an institution with unilateral power to extend the term. Equal assignment by lottery might improve distribution but still impose excessive burdens. The final essay should state a primary principle. A promising formulation is: institutions that intentionally organize dependency must not foreseeably make a particular person's continued service the sole barrier against serious harm when they can create shared capacity at proportionate cost. Then explain why this follows from equal standing or non-domination and how care ethics adds attention to the dependent person's relationships.

### 3. “Reasonable” and “fair” conceal the decision rule

The thesis applies “where reasonable alternatives are available”; handover must be “fair”; notice must be “reasonable”; and the system may impose duties under “defensible rules.” These qualifications are sensible, but the essay never says what makes cost proportionate or allocation defensible. In contested cases, that is the whole question.

At minimum, specify the factors that must be weighed: severity and duration of the burden on the current carer; urgency and kind of the dependent person's needs; whether continuity concerns tasks, trust, communication, or the relationship itself; the institution's causal role in creating exclusivity; the cost and risk of transition; whether burdens can be distributed without targeting a powerless group; and whether delay will entrench the bottleneck. The principle can remain pro tanto, but it needs to say who bears the burden of justification. The draft's best suggestion is that an institution which created the bottleneck must show why it cannot repair it. Make that explicit.

### 4. The essay slides between relationships and tasks

The distinction between remaining in a relationship and performing every associated task is excellent, but it cannot cover all examples. A parent cannot hand over parenthood in the same way that an employee can hand over a shift. A spouse's intimate care may partly constitute the relationship. A professional carer can leave an occupation while still owing notice. An adult child may never have consented to either the relationship or the task assignment. These roles have different sources and limits.

The revision should confine the defended principle to *institutionally allocated care tasks* and say that it does not establish a general right to transfer relationships or role-specific duties. Then use parental continuity as a limiting analogy, not as if Alstott directly supports institutional role transfer. Alstott's “No Exit” concerns a voluntarily entered but socially structured parental relationship. The town's hereditary assignment is much stronger. That contrast can sharpen the paper: even when a relationship cannot be transferred, institutions can often redistribute much of its labor.

### 5. The emergency-duty analogy needs qualification

The injured-person-at-your-door example distinguishes the victim's present claim from the wrongdoer's authority. But it may also imply that any nearby person acquires a duty whenever others create danger. That conclusion needs limits. Proximity, competence, cost, the availability of public responders, and other agents' causal responsibility matter. More importantly, a one-off rescue does not explain how a temporary duty becomes, or fails to become, a continuing care duty.

The essay should distinguish three duties: the carer's short-term duty to prevent imminent grave harm; the institution's immediate duty to mobilize relief; and the founders' earlier duty not to create avoidable sole dependence. This produces a useful asymmetry. The more the current carer must remain briefly, the stronger the duty on others to end that necessity. It also supports the manuscript's excellent claim that conscientious non-abandonment must not become an excuse for institutional inaction.

### 6. The nonidentity reply is plausible but too quick

The draft says a worthwhile life does not make every restriction permissible. That is a familiar and defensible answer to one version of nonidentity, but it requires an account of wronging that does not compare the person with their own nonexistence. The essay appears to rely on a non-comparative principle: assigning a person a severe inherited role without adequate justification violates their equal standing, even if their life is worth living and they would not exist under another policy.

State that principle. Also separate two cases. If the same future people would exist under either staffing policy, nonidentity never arises. If staffing policy changes who is born, the non-comparative principle does the work. This would prevent the short section from seeming to solve the entire nonidentity problem.

## Counterexamples and tests

First, consider a remote island after a disaster. Only one adult has the skill to keep a child alive, and training another person would consume supplies needed for current patients. The principle should allow the adult to have a stringent temporary duty while denying that anyone has a present duty to create impossible spare capacity. This shows why feasibility matters and why the institution must revisit the arrangement when scarcity eases.

Second, consider a resident who autonomously refuses all replacement carers and will accept care only from one familiar person. The draft rightly gives the resident a voice, but it must deny them unilateral authority over another person's whole life. The institution should seek gradual trust-building and distinguish a claim to respectful transition from a veto over transfer. Explain why autonomy on one side does not erase autonomy on the other.

Third, imagine a rotating care system with excellent exit rights but poor relational continuity. Every worker may leave, yet residents repeatedly lose trusted relationships. This system satisfies transferability while failing care. The manuscript should therefore present transferability as one necessary institutional value among others, not a sufficient condition for justice.

Fourth, consider parents. They choose to have a child, then later find parenting far more burdensome than expected. The state can supply extensive help but cannot make their particular parental relationship fully transferable without harming the child. The essay should say that its principle requires reducing avoidable task concentration, not guaranteeing exit from every identity-forming commitment.

## Significance, originality, literature, and source accuracy

**Significance.** The problem matters well beyond the stylized town. Long-term disability support, elder care, medical teams, ecological stewardship, archives, dangerous infrastructure, and public institutions can all become dependent on a few people whose moral concern is then used as the system's last safeguard. The essay would be more significant if it briefly applied its principle to one real institutional class and showed what it changes.

**Originality.** The narrow idea of treating *transfer capacity* as part of what one generation must leave with a care-dependent good may be a useful synthesis. The current search does not establish priority. Kate McKittrick-Sweitzer's 2024 account of care exploitation already argues that respect requires openness to a caring person's choice to do otherwise and support for self-authorship. Marcel Wissenburg's 2011 intergenerational argument says one generation should give the next the capacity to fulfil parental or guardian duties while preserving freedom. Randall explicitly develops intergenerational care ethics. The revision should cite these close arguments, abandon any suggestion that self-authorship for carers is itself new, and claim only a specific institutional extension: safe transfer capacity as a component of intergenerational care provision.

**Engagement with literature.** Three sources are too thin for the reach of the essay. Kittay and Alstott ground support and continuity, but neither is presented as a theory of institutional handover. Randall's full argument was not accessed. The final version should engage at least one primary argument on care exploitation or relational autonomy and one on intergenerational capacity or inherited institutions. If full access remains unavailable, narrow claims accordingly rather than inferring absence.

**Source accuracy.** The summaries of the accessible Kittay and Alstott articles are accurate and restrained. Kittay criticizes support that neglects carers' present needs; Alstott links children's development to continuity and argues for public support that protects parents' opportunities. The manuscript should clarify that Alstott's “No Exit” is socially structured parenthood, not literal occupational non-exit. The Randall citation is appropriately limited to the abstract, whose bibliographic details and sufficientarian conclusion were verified. The full article remained inaccessible, so the manuscript cannot claim that role transfer is missing from it.

## Language review

The prose is generally controlled, but several compact claims make the reader supply an unstated premise. The central terms “inheritance,” “assignment,” “handover,” “role,” and “reasonable” also shift in meaning. Define the subject as durable institutions and the object as care tasks or care capacity early in the essay.

### Opening

> “A town builds a home for people who need daily help. The founders provide good rooms, reliable equipment, and a fund large enough to cover supplies. They leave one further instruction: the daughters of the town's current families are to provide the care.”

The reader understands the problem immediately. The artificial element is that the example stacks every moral defect into one rule, so it reads more like a demonstration of hereditary servitude than an ordinary care institution. A simpler opening can retain clarity while creating a real dispute:

> A town opens a home for people who need daily help. It pays the first carers well but trains no replacements. Years later, the residents depend on those carers, and leaving would put them at risk. Did the town owe both groups a safe way to change who does the work?

### Central thesis

> “We should judge an inheritance partly by whether its duties can be shared and handed over fairly, not only by the resources it contains.”

“Inheritance” is abstract here, and “its duties” sounds as though an object owns duties. Name the institution and the people:

> When one generation creates a care institution, it should leave future people enough capacity to share and transfer its care tasks safely, as well as enough money and equipment to continue them.

### The backward-reaching argument

> “Otherwise the decision-maker can use future need to secure authority that they lack now.”

This is memorable but compressed. It does not say how a need gives anyone authority. Make the mechanism explicit:

> A founder cannot justify choosing a future carer merely by making a dependent person rely on that carer. Creating the reliance does not create the right to control the carer's life.

### The nonidentity passage

> “That judgment concerns how people may be treated when they arrive. It does not require proving that a particular future person would have existed and been better off under another policy.”

The phrase “when they arrive” is vague, and the passage assumes a non-comparative account without naming it. A clearer version is:

> The complaint is not that the policy made a particular person worse off than nonexistence. It is that no person should receive a severe inherited assignment without adequate justification, whoever that person turns out to be.

### The institutional duty

> “Where an institution has organized the dependence, it should normally take responsibility for building and maintaining the route to relief.”

“Organized the dependence” and “route to relief” can sound bureaucratic. The point is concrete:

> If an institution made residents depend on a small group of carers, the institution should train replacements and arrange safe transitions. It should not leave each carer to build a new care service before they may leave.

### Strongest objection

> “The answer is to distinguish what must continue from every activity currently attached to it.”

The referent of “it” is unclear, and the sentence sounds like a formula before the reader gets the distinction. Rewrite it around people:

> A relationship may need to continue even when one person no longer performs every care task within it. A child may still need a parent's stable presence while other people provide meals, transport, or supervision.

### Scarcity

> “The duty defended here is sensitive to that cost. It is a reason to choose among feasible designs, not a demand to guarantee an effortless exit under every possible future.”

“Sensitive to” and “feasible designs” hide the practical standard. State the limit directly:

> A community need not fund spare capacity when doing so would deprive current residents of essential care. But when it can reduce a dangerous bottleneck at proportionate cost, it has a reason to do so.

### Final sentence

> “We care for future people partly by refusing to make their goodness the only thing holding our plans together.”

This is elegant but “goodness” is ambiguous: kindness, moral character, or willingness to self-sacrifice? Make the danger explicit:

> We care for future people partly by refusing to build institutions that work only because one conscientious person cannot bear to leave someone unprotected.

## Prioritized revision plan

1. Replace or supplement the opening with a non-coercive case that isolates failure to maintain transfer capacity.
2. State one primary moral principle and define its scope: institutionally allocated care tasks, proportional cost, and a presumption against avoidable sole dependence.
3. Separate relationship continuity, task continuity, and emergency rescue; explain which can be transferred and why.
4. Turn “reasonable” into a transparent set of factors and place the burden of justification on institutions that created the bottleneck.
5. Add the close literature on care exploitation/self-authorship and intergenerational capacity, while keeping the originality claim narrow and provisional.
6. Clarify the nonidentity response as a non-comparative complaint about unjustified assignment.
7. Apply the principle to one real kind of care institution and revise abstract sentences throughout using the patterns above.

## Verification limits

I checked the full visible Boston Review texts by Kittay and Alstott, Randall's publisher abstract and bibliographic record, the full visible text of McKittrick-Sweitzer's article, the relevant visible section of Wissenburg's article, and the Stanford Encyclopedia discussion of nonidentity. I could not access Randall's full article or conduct an exhaustive review of books and adjacent literatures. The originality assessment is therefore provisional, and nothing in this critique certifies the essay's truth, novelty, or competition readiness.
