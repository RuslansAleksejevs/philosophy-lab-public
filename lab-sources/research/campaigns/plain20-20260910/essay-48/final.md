# Leaving Care Without Leaving People

A town opens a home for people who need daily help. The first carers freely accept ten-year jobs. They receive good pay, training, and time off. Over the years, residents learn to trust them. Some residents communicate in ways that only these carers understand well.

The home was meant to train new staff before each ten-year term ended. But training costs money, and the managers keep postponing it. When one carer asks to leave at the end of her term, the managers tell her that the residents now depend on her. They are right about the dependence. If she simply disappears, several people may suffer. Yet the dependence is also the result of choices the home made. It let one person's sense of duty become its backup plan.

What does one generation owe the next when it creates an institution that needs continuing care? Money, buildings, and equipment are not enough. I argue that it should also leave what I will call **safe transfer capacity**: enough shared skill, knowledge, trust, and authority to change who performs care tasks without abandoning the people who receive care.

The principle is limited. It concerns care tasks allocated by durable institutions, such as residential homes, hospitals, and public support services. It does not establish a general right to transfer a relationship, parenthood, or every duty that grows within a personal commitment. Safe transfer is also only one condition of a just care system. A system can let workers leave and still treat residents terribly. But when an institution makes one person's continued service the sole barrier against serious harm, it owes an explanation. If it created that bottleneck and can reduce it at proportionate cost, it should do so.

## Support within a role is not enough

Care begins with dependence. All people depend on others at some points in life, and some people need daily help for many years. Their needs create real moral claims. A theory that talks only about independent adults choosing projects can miss both the value and the cost of the work that answers those claims.

Eva Kittay argues that carers can become dependent in turn because they need other people to supply the resources that care requires. She also argues that support must meet carers' present needs and extend beyond parents. Her point is not that society should help carers escape every demand. It is that care should not be left to the isolated person nearest to need. A society that values care must support the people who do it ([Kittay 2004](https://www.bostonreview.net/forum/anne-l-alstott-what-we-owe-parents/eva-kittay-falling-short/)).

That support matters, but it can leave a further wrong untouched. Return to the town's home. Suppose the carer has excellent pay, a pleasant room, generous holidays, and a secure pension. These goods may make her life much better. They do not answer her complaint that the institution can extend her service simply by refusing to train anyone else. The power to control a large part of one's life is not identical to comfort while living the life others require.

Kate McKittrick-Sweitzer makes a nearby argument about the exploitation of activists' care. A group can disrespect a caring person by assuming that she will always answer a call, rather than leaving her open to choose otherwise. Respect includes concern for her well-being and her ability to shape her own life ([McKittrick-Sweitzer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10677-024-10435-2)). Activism differs from hands-on care for a dependent person: refusal in the latter case may expose someone to immediate grave harm. Still, the same danger appears. Other people can use a carer's conscientiousness as a resource while avoiding their own duty to build a fair system.

My claim adds a specific institutional demand. Respecting a carer's choice is not only a matter of asking what she wants when the crisis arrives. It requires maintaining the practical capacity to act on a reasonable request for change. A formal right to leave is hollow if exercising it is the only thing standing between a resident and serious injury.

## The safe-transfer principle

The principle can now be stated more exactly:

> When an institution intentionally organizes continuing care, it has a duty not to make a particular person's service the sole barrier against serious harm if it can create shared capacity at proportionate cost.

This duty rests on equal standing. A resident and a carer each has a life that matters. The resident's dependence gives strong reasons to continue adequate care. It does not give the institution authority to turn one carer's concern into indefinite control over that carer's life. Nor can managers gain that authority by creating the very dependence they later cite. If the home chose not to train anyone else, it cannot justify extending a carer's term merely by saying that only she can now leave safely.

The principle also protects residents. A system with only one competent carer is fragile. Illness, death, burnout, or an urgent family need can remove that person without notice. Shared knowledge and relationships make care more reliable as well as making work transferable. The interests of residents and carers therefore often support the same preparation, even though they can conflict in a particular transition.

Safe transfer capacity will differ by setting. It may include several trained staff, readable records, paid periods of overlap, routes into training, and a public body with authority to arrange relief. It may also require a wider circle of people whom a resident knows and trusts. Money enables these things, but a bank account by itself cannot perform them.

Marcel Wissenburg argues, in a different intergenerational setting, that one generation should give the next the capacity to fulfil parental or guardian duties while preserving freedom ([Wissenburg 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10806-010-9283-1)). Thomas Randall's published abstract likewise argues that present people should sustain conditions for good caring relations in the future ([Randall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/hypa.12477)). The full Randall article was not available for this essay, so I do not claim that it omits transfer. The narrower proposal here is that the capacity passed forward should include a safe way to reallocate institutional care tasks. The literature check is too limited to establish priority for that proposal.

## What counts as a proportionate demand?

“Proportionate cost” cannot be reduced to one number. A defensible judgment must compare at least seven things: the seriousness and duration of the burden on the current carer; the urgency of the resident's needs; whether continuity depends on a task, a skill, trust, communication, or the relationship itself; the institution's part in creating exclusivity; the cost and risk of transition; the fairness of the burden placed on replacements; and the danger that delay will make the bottleneck worse.

These factors do not create a mechanical answer. They identify what managers must explain. The burden of justification should be heavier when the institution caused the problem. If a home saved money for years by cancelling backup training, it should have to show why rebuilding that training is now too costly or dangerous. The carer should not have to prove that every imaginable transition is safe before the home begins to act.

Two limits are clear. First, one person's freedom cannot be bought by trapping a poorer worker. Recruitment, compulsory service, or a lottery may spread burdens, but equal distribution alone does not make a severe burden acceptable. The terms, duration, voice, and alternatives of the new carers also matter. Second, a community need not fund spare capacity when doing so would deprive current residents of essential care. Imagine a remote island after a disaster. Only one adult can keep a child alive, and training someone else today would use supplies needed by current patients. The adult may have a stringent temporary duty to remain. No one has a duty to create impossible backup capacity. But scarcity should be reassessed when conditions improve. An emergency can delay repair without becoming a permanent excuse.

## Tasks, relationships, and emergencies

The argument becomes confused unless three kinds of continuity are separated.

First, **task continuity** concerns actions that someone must perform: giving medicine, preparing food, lifting a resident safely, or monitoring breathing. Many such tasks can be taught and shared, though the teaching may take time.

Second, **trust and communication continuity** concern knowledge built between particular people. A resident may use gestures that a familiar carer understands. A replacement who has the right certificate but cannot understand the resident is not yet adequate. Safe transfer may require overlap, the resident's participation, and gradual trust-building.

Third, **relationship continuity** concerns the continued presence of a particular person as a parent, partner, friend, or other loved one. Some duties help constitute these relationships and cannot be transferred like a shift. Anne Alstott argues that children need continuing parental care and that public support should protect parents' opportunities while respecting that continuity ([Alstott 2004](https://www.bostonreview.net/forum/anne-l-alstott-what-we-owe-parents/)). Her “No Exit” discussion concerns voluntarily entered but socially structured parenthood. It does not establish a rule for leaving an occupation.

Parents therefore mark a limit to my principle. The state cannot promise that every identity-forming commitment can be handed to someone else without loss. But even when a relationship must continue, many tasks within it can be shared. A child may need a parent's stable love and presence without needing that parent to provide every meal, journey, and hour of supervision. Public help can reduce avoidable concentration of work without treating the relationship as disposable.

Institutional carers can also form deep relationships that deserve respect. Safe transfer does not mean rapid rotation. A home in which workers constantly change may have excellent exit rules and still fail its residents. Transfer capacity is meant to make change possible when it is needed, not to maximize staff turnover. Good institutions should support stable relationships and prepare for change at the same time.

Suppose a resident refuses every possible replacement and wants care only from one familiar person. The refusal matters. It may express fear, past mistreatment, or a communication need. The home should respond with patience, gradual introductions, and serious efforts to find someone acceptable. But the resident does not gain unilateral authority over the carer's whole life. The resident has a claim to respectful and safe transition, not an unlimited veto. Equal standing applies to both people.

Emergencies produce a further distinction among duties. If a carer alone can prevent imminent grave harm at modest personal cost, she may have a short-term duty to stay. At the same time, the institution has an immediate duty to mobilize relief. Earlier managers had a different duty: not to create avoidable sole dependence in the first place. These duties point in opposite directions only at first glance. The stronger the reason for the carer to remain today, the stronger the reason for everyone able to help to end that necessity. Her refusal to abandon a resident must not become permission for institutional delay.

This does not mean the carer must sacrifice without limit. Competence, cost, the availability of other responders, and the severity of the threatened harm all affect a rescue duty. A one-day emergency duty cannot silently become an indefinite occupation. Every extension needs a fresh reason and a plan for relief.

## Why future identity does not erase the duty

Sometimes a staffing policy will not affect who later exists. Then the familiar nonidentity problem does not arise: the same person can complain that the institution imposed a severe and avoidable restriction on her.

In other cases, long-term policies may change who meets, has children, or is born. A future carer might not have existed under another policy. It would then be strange to say that the policy made her worse off than she otherwise would have been, since her alternative may have been nonexistence.

The complaint defended here does not need that comparison. It rests on a rule of equal standing: no person's birth into a group is enough to justify assigning that person a severe institutional role. The policy must be justified to whoever occupies the role. A worthwhile life does not make every condition placed within it permissible. This is a non-comparative claim about how a person may be treated, not a complete solution to the nonidentity problem. It leaves many questions about duties to possible future people unresolved (see the overview in [Gosseries and Meyer 2026](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-intergenerational/)).

## What the principle changes

Consider a residential disability service with one long-serving worker who understands a resident's uncommon signs of pain. A budget review might count beds, wages, food, and equipment and conclude that the service is fully funded. The safe-transfer principle asks further questions. Who else is learning the resident's signs? Is that knowledge recorded in a form the resident has approved? Does the schedule allow trusted colleagues to work alongside the long-serving carer? Who is responsible for arranging a transition if the carer becomes ill or wants to leave?

These questions do not assume that the resident is interchangeable or that the worker's job is casual. They reveal a risk that ordinary resource measures miss. If the answer to each question is “no one,” the service has passed a hidden debt forward. Future people will inherit a valuable institution whose survival depends on one conscientious person's inability to bear the consequences of leaving.

An older generation cannot protect every future person from every unchosen duty. Care will always make claims across time. Some relationships rightly ask much of particular people, and genuine scarcity can create harsh temporary demands. The argument here is narrower: creators of durable care institutions should not add avoidable captivity to unavoidable dependence.

A responsible institution therefore leaves more than supplies. It leaves people who can share skills, records that preserve knowledge, time for trust to grow, fair terms for new carers, and someone with the duty and authority to arrange change. It prepares for a future person to say, “This care must continue, but I cannot remain its only safeguard.” We care for future people partly by refusing to build institutions that work only because one conscientious person cannot bear to leave someone unprotected.
