# Repair Cannot Wait for the Future

A wooden table has been repaired many times. Each worn part was replaced while the rest remained assembled. On Monday the last old part is removed. The table stands in the kitchen, and every piece of its original wood lies in a shed. On Tuesday someone decides what to do with that wood. They might burn it. They might put it back together as a second table.

Can Tuesday's choice determine whether the table in the kitchen on Monday was the table bought years earlier?

I argue that it cannot, provided the two possible histories agree through Monday, including the repair process, the purposes of the people involved, and the conventions governing this kind of object. This does not establish that the kitchen table survived. Perhaps replacement destroyed it. The claim is that its survival through Monday should have the same answer in both histories. What happens to the discarded wood later cannot by itself settle that earlier question differently.

This restriction has a useful consequence. We cannot defend ordinary repair by saying that a repaired object survives just when its old parts never become a rival. If that qualification reaches indefinitely into the future, it gives later reconstruction more power than it has earned. A theory should instead explain what happened during the repair, or explain why an ordinary object must be identified by its whole future history.

## What is at issue

Being the same table means being one particular thing at different times. It does not mean looking the same, serving the same purpose, or containing the same wood. Each of those facts may help us identify a table, but each can also mislead. Two tables can look alike. One table can acquire a new purpose. A pile of wood can contain all the material of a table without presently being a table.

The classic puzzle makes these considerations compete. Hobbes considers a ship whose planks are gradually replaced and another assembled from the removed planks. Both cannot be the original ship if they are two distinct ships. His challenge is to the combination of the claims, rather than merely to the possibility of replacement. See [*De Corpore*, II, XI, excerpt p. 1](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil1020/Hobbes.pdf).

A recent defence of the reconstructed object supplies a direct target. Lundgren argues that complete reconstruction from the old parts preserves the original, whereas gradual replacement can preserve it when those parts are destroyed. He also explicitly considers delayed reconstruction. His explanation treats the competing histories as different forms of change: disassembly followed by assembly in one case, repair through replacement in the other. See [Lundgren 2025, §§3–5](https://doi.org/10.1111/theo.70013).

The mere introduction of a delay is therefore not an objection he has overlooked. My objection concerns where the histories first differ. A discovery after eighty years might reveal an earlier theft or substitution. That would change our evidence about what happened. But suppose we already know everything relevant about the earlier handling of the table and its parts. We then vary only a later choice. An appeal to discovery no longer explains the different verdicts.

## Two histories with one past

Make the example precise. No single table part has a special status, such as the irreplaceable surface of a signed artwork. The replacements occur over years. The table remains usable throughout. Removed pieces are labelled and stored, without any settled plan to rebuild them. On Monday, after the last replacement, the workers leave. Through Monday, the two histories are alike in every relevant physical and social detail.

In the first history, a decision on Tuesday leads to the destruction of the stored wood. The kitchen table receives no further repair or alteration. In the second, a decision on Tuesday leads to the wood's careful assembly elsewhere. The kitchen table again receives no further repair or alteration. We ask about the kitchen table on Monday, before either decision. We are not comparing the two tables on Tuesday and asking which deserves a family name.

Three claims now pull apart.

First, ordinary replacement of the kind described can preserve the table when the removed parts are later destroyed. Second, assembly of all the old wood in its old arrangement must recover the original table, even while the kitchen table remains standing. Third, changing only events after Monday cannot alter which earlier table was present in the kitchen on Monday.

The first and third claims together say that the kitchen table on Monday is the original in both histories. In the reconstruction history, the second claim says that the table assembled on Tuesday is also the original. We also have the plain assumption that the kitchen table persists through Tuesday while nothing happens to it. It follows that the two separate tables on Tuesday are one table. That is the conflict.

This argument does not derive a theory of tables from logic alone. It reveals a choice. We can deny survival through replacement, deny that reconstruction always recovers the original, deny the third claim, or deny that the kitchen table persists while standing undisturbed. Saying that the original parts take priority merely chooses among these options. It does not explain the cost of the choice.

The last option is especially unattractive. On that account, assembling wood across town destroys the kitchen table and brings a different table into existence exactly where it stood. No change occurs in the kitchen, in its occupants' practices, or in any causal process maintaining the furniture. The alleged destruction does all its work in the account of identity. It needs a reason beyond the desire to reserve the original table for the other site.

## Why the past should remain fixed

It is tempting to say that Tuesday cannot affect Monday because causes run forward. That would be too quick. Dependence need not be causal. Whether a Monday meeting was the last meeting of a committee depends on what happens later. No backward cause is involved. A defender of future-dependent identity can make the same reply about the table.

The important question is whether being the original table is relevantly like being the last meeting. The latter description openly selects an event by its position in a completed sequence. The meeting can take place, be attended, and end before it is settled that no later meeting occurs. None of those facts requires it to have been the last.

Our use of an ordinary table is different. We follow a thing while it undergoes changes. We ask what was repaired, what was moved, and what was damaged. A judgement that the table survived is part of explaining the repair episode itself. If the answer depends on a future reconstruction, the repair history is insufficient even when we know every event in it. To obtain the answer, we must first choose between whole possible careers for the original object.

This approach is possible. But it reverses the usual explanatory order. Instead of identifying an object as we trace its changes and then describing its career, we select a career and use it to say which object was there. The reversal matters because the two approaches license different claims of knowledge. On the second, complete observation of an ordinary repair may never establish survival. Old parts could remain capable of reconstruction for decades. Their eventual destruction could certify an earlier survival that no feature of the repair had established.

Limited knowledge is not itself a refutation. Many facts are difficult to know. The objection is that the proposed source of difficulty has no demonstrated connection with the kind of thing being investigated. Tables need support, joints and suitable materials. Their histories involve making, repairing, storing and moving them. Why should the persistence of one also require the permanent failure of another assembly project? The condition does not identify a further process sustaining the table. It grants a future competitor a veto.

This is a reason to prefer the fixed-past claim, not a proof that every imaginable object must obey it. We sometimes introduce objects whose boundaries depend on a complete history. The longest continuous performance of a tune is an example. To defend the same treatment of an ordinary table, we need an account of why that treatment helps explain tables. Avoiding the two-table contradiction alone is not enough. Other options avoid it too.

There is an established concern nearby. Wiggins distinguishes what grounds identity from what helps us discover it, and requires grounds of identity to respect its logical consequences. He also allows later practices to refine our conception of an artefact. These are different matters. See [Wiggins 2012, §§4 and 16, note 7](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0378.2012.00516.x). The present proposal is narrower than a ban on all outside facts. The workers, storage practices and kind of artefact can matter. They are held fixed through Monday. What needs justification is the power assigned to a new event after that common history.

## What reconstruction can show

A defender of reconstruction may reply that a table can survive disassembly. If its original parts are stored and later rejoined, surely we can get the table back. Why should concurrent replacement change that?

I agree that disassembly need not destroy a table. But the familiar case and the present case differ. In the familiar case, people take a table apart, preserve its components as the components of an item in storage, and later assemble it. There is no continuing assembled object competing for its history. Our ordinary acceptance of that case does not establish that the same verdict is mandatory when the parts have been removed through years of ordinary repair.

The question is not whether the wood is authentic. It is whether every route by which the wood becomes separated must count as the table's disassembly. Removing a leg from a table and retaining it as a spare does not by itself place the table into storage. After the table loses another part, that need not retroactively change the first removal into the beginning of a move. Reconstructing all the removed parts does not automatically establish that those removals were stages of taking one whole table elsewhere.

Someone may insist that they were such stages, although no one knew this. That returns us to the original dispute. It offers a description of the completed history, not an independent reason why that history must determine the earlier object.

There is also a practical source of confusion. A museum may prefer the assembled old wood because it carries tool marks, stains and traces of its maker. Those are real reasons to value and exhibit it. A family may prefer the continuously repaired table because meals were eaten around it for fifty years. Those too are real historical connections. Neither preference establishes numerical identity. Calling the preferred item “the original” can express authenticity or inheritance without settling whether it is literally the earlier table.

This distinction allows us to take the old material seriously without giving it unlimited authority. Even if the reconstruction is a new table, it can be the best surviving collection of evidence about the old one. Even if the kitchen table is the old table, it can contain none of that evidence. Survival and evidential value can come apart.

## Four limits

The argument has limits that prevent it from becoming an easy solution to every persistence puzzle.

First, it does not say that gradual change always preserves an object. Suppose a working table is slowly rebuilt into a furnace. Small steps alone do not show that the table survives the entire process. A defender of continuity must still say what makes the sequence a continued table rather than its gradual destruction. The present argument allows the verdict “it did not survive” in both of Monday's histories.

Second, the argument does not cover every case of branching. If a machine is actually divided into two working machines, that event changes its physical organisation. Facts about both results may matter in deciding whether the earlier machine survived. In our example, reconstruction occurs only after the interval under examination. The claim that later events cannot revise survival through that interval does not decide survival through the division itself.

Third, shared appearances are insufficient. If, before Monday, one project is a secret replacement and another a public repair, the histories do not satisfy the condition. Intentions and established practices may be relevant to artefacts. The example holds them fixed precisely to avoid drawing conclusions from histories that only look alike.

Fourth, a theory that treats an ordinary object as an extended whole across time can reject the fixed-past claim. It might say that two whole histories share their early stretches without containing the same whole table. My argument does not rule out such a theory by definition. It asks its defender to explain why the complete career is the right unit here, and why rebuilding old parts selects that career. Simply adopting a view of objects across time does not yet answer the second question. There can be many possible careers through the same early events.

These limits leave a substantive conclusion. The reconstruction puzzle cannot be resolved merely by adding an exception to repair: “unless the old parts are ever reassembled.” That exception commits us to a disputed account of how future events determine past identity. If we retain ordinary survival through repair and reject that account, reconstruction must lose its unconditional claim. Sometimes it restores a disassembled table. Sometimes it produces a new table from old material. The difference needs to be explained through the relevant histories, rather than settled by whichever rival appears last.

The table's past can be hard to discover. Our descriptions of it can also change. But a later decision about spare wood should not, without a further account, decide what already stood in the kitchen.
