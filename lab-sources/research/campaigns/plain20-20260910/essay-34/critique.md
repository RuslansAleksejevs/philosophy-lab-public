# Critique: Repair Cannot Wait for the Future

## Reconstruction and verdict

The essay asks whether two histories alike through Monday may differ about the repaired table’s survival through Monday solely because old parts are rebuilt on Tuesday in one history. It prefers a negative answer. Given survival in the destruction history, that preference excludes unconditional priority for reconstruction, unless the unchanged kitchen table ceases to exist when reconstruction occurs elsewhere.

This is a promising, restrained challenge to one proposed solution, not yet a demonstration that later reconstruction cannot determine earlier identity. The strongest contribution is isolating completed repair from later material reuse and asking the reconstruction theorist to justify their explanatory priority. The draft’s title and opening claim are more categorical than the eventual burden-of-proof conclusion. Revise toward the latter unless a stronger argument can be supplied.

## Argument: main revisions

1. **Repair can survive versus this repair did survive.** The first of the three claims says this kind of replacement “can preserve” the table. The inference immediately treats the kitchen table as having survived in the first history. Possibility for a kind is insufficient for survival in this instance. State the premise directly: in the destruction history, this table survives through Monday. Explain that this is a verdict borrowed from the targeted account, or make the whole argument conditional upon it. Do not smuggle that verdict into the word “repair.” Calling both sequences replacements until survival is under discussion would help.

2. **The fixed-past principle needs a clearer independent reason.** The claim that following an object normally precedes describing its whole career describes how we investigate and speak. It does not by itself establish what makes identity true. The essay acknowledges this, but then repeatedly asks why the opposing account should be accepted rather than developing a comparative argument. The best available repair is methodological: an account grounded in ordinary handling should explain why a later, causally separate use of discarded wood defeats the earlier repair verdict, especially when intentions and treatment of the assembled table remain unchanged. Then concede that readers who already identify artifacts by completed material histories will reject the preference. This is still a useful criticism of an insufficient explanation.

3. **Develop the strongest opponent beyond the “last meeting” comparison.** A reconstruction defender can say the stored parts already constituted the disassembled original on Monday in both histories. The kitchen item therefore failed to survive in both. Tuesday burns or reassembles the stored original; it does not change the past. This retains reconstruction priority and your fixed-past principle by abandoning the particular repair verdict. You list this logical option but should give it an actual paragraph: why accept ordinary repairs when old parts are destroyed immediately but reject them when all are retained? It is costly and perhaps sensitive to storage, but coherent. It shows the conclusion is a trilemma with a preferred exit, not an argument against reconstruction alone.

A second opponent treats the concepts applicable to this unusual table as not yet settled. Shared practices through Monday may fail to decide the case; later practice can settle how people apply “table,” without changing a previously definite identity fact. Your example fixes past conventions but does not require that they already settle this unprecedented combination of repair and retention. Either allow the same unresolved verdict in both histories or explicitly explain what further assumption yields a definite answer. Do not call every later conceptual settlement merely naming: your own account allows social facts to matter.

4. **Separate counterfactual dependence from change in actual truth.** Neither whole-history view needs to say an identity was true on Monday and became false on Tuesday. It can say it was false throughout the reconstruction history and true throughout the destruction history. The draft correctly distinguishes noncausal dependence early, yet “retroactively,” “veto,” and the final sentence repeatedly encourage the misleading temporal-change reading. One explicit sentence would prevent this: the objection concerns different verdicts in two possible histories, not a truth switching after the fact.

5. **Avoid overstating the knowledge consequence.** “Complete observation … may never establish survival” is too broad if “establish” permits defeasible knowledge. Ordinary knowledge often excludes strange alternatives without certainty. Specify that the proposed account blocks a guarantee based solely on events through Monday, not necessarily ordinary knowledge. The epistemic cost is secondary to the alleged failure to explain what constitutes survival.

## Source accuracy and scholarship

[Hobbes, De Corpore II.XI, teaching excerpt p. 1](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil1020/Hobbes.pdf) supports the stated contradiction. The draft wisely does not treat this short passage as a full interpretation of Hobbes.

[Lundgren 2025, §§4–5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/theo.70013) supports the target. But §5 already compares reconstruction and destruction worlds and explains them as different changes through damage. Present the essay as challenging that explanation, not as introducing the comparison. The distinction between a later decision and an earlier secret substitution is useful, but does not exhaust his case. Add a short direct response to the damage explanation: why does later use of the pieces establish what the earlier damage was damage to?

[Wiggins 2012, §§4 and 16](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-0378.2012.00516.x) is handled cautiously. His artifact discussion nevertheless puts pressure on the assumption that earlier conventions fully settle the object’s nature. Address that pressure rather than presenting him only as background support. I checked these sections, not the exact note 7 wording. The draft’s broad source claims are supported; bibliographic completeness could be improved if verified, without inventing page ranges.

## Originality and significance

The contribution is a focused explanatory objection within a familiar family of identity puzzles. The shared-past comparison and resistance to competitor-dependent identity have close predecessors; the draft already admits delayed reconstruction is not new. Searches found older extrinsic-identity and closest-continuer discussions but did not retrieve Nozick’s primary text. Do not claim priority. The modest result can matter: a theory’s reconstruction exception owes a reason compatible with its appeal to everyday repair judgments. Strengthening that exact criticism is more valuable than adding broad persistence literature decoratively.

## Concrete language review

**Opening.** “A wooden table has been repaired many times. Each worn part was replaced while the rest remained assembled. On Monday the last old part is removed.” This is immediately understandable and the example serves the argument. Optional improvement: “Over the years, every part of a wooden table is replaced. On Monday the last original piece comes off.” Keep the detail about the rest remaining assembled nearby; it explains why this looks like repair. No dramatic scene-setting is needed.

**Central thesis.** “I argue that it cannot, provided the two possible histories agree through Monday, including the repair process, the purposes of the people involved, and the conventions governing this kind of object.” The reader must hold the claim while decoding a dense proviso. Rewrite: “My claim is that Tuesday’s choice should not change the answer about Monday. Suppose everything up to Monday is the same: what the workers did, what they intended, and how people understood the table.” Then explicitly mark this as a principle being defended rather than proved by stipulation.

**Logical core.** “The first and third claims together say that the kitchen table on Monday is the original in both histories.” Clear syntax, but the reference numbers make readers look backward and conceal the modal gap. Rewrite after repairing premise one: “Suppose the table survived in the history where the old wood is burned. If later events cannot make a difference to Monday’s identity, it also survived through Monday in the reconstruction history.”

**Explanation section.** “But it reverses the usual explanatory order.” “Instead of identifying an object as we trace its changes and then describing its career, we select a career and use it to say which object was there.” “Career” is understandable but abstract, and “select” can imply arbitrary choosing. Rewrite: “On this view, what happened during the repair does not settle which table was there. The answer also depends on what later happened to the removed wood.” Follow with the actual reason to object, rather than assuming this restatement proves a defect.

**Strongest objection.** “A theory that treats an ordinary object as an extended whole across time can reject the fixed-past claim.” This is the essay’s most demanding passage and needs an example before the label. Rewrite: “Someone might say that a table is its entire history, from its making to its destruction. On that view, two histories can match through Monday yet concern different tables because their later events differ.” Then clarify this is counterfactual difference, not a Monday truth becoming false.

**Reconstruction section.** “Reconstructing all the removed parts does not automatically establish that those removals were stages of taking one whole table elsewhere.” Correct but cumbersome. Rewrite: “Putting the old wood together later does not by itself show that the workers had been moving the original table piece by piece.” Add that this is about what their actions accomplished, not solely their intention, lest the simpler version accidentally make intention decisive.

**Conclusion.** “That exception commits us to a disputed account of how future events determine past identity.” “Commits us” omits the other premise choices. Rewrite: “If we also accept that this repair preserved the table, the exception makes Monday’s answer depend on later events. That dependence needs an explanation.” This is both simpler and more accurate.

Priorities: repair the first premise; strengthen and narrow the main conclusion; give the stored-original and unsettled-concept replies real space; then revise the language throughout for counterfactual accuracy. Preserve the accessible opening and the distinction between survival, authenticity, and evidence.
