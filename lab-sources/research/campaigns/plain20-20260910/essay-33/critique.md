# Critique: A True Answer Is Not Always the End

## Reconstruction and verdict

The essay distinguishes knowing an answer to a narrow question, achieving the broader task for which that question was asked, and being justified in stopping work. Its principle is that a known narrow answer cannot alone justify claiming broader completion when available, unanswered evidence undermines its relevance or coverage. The register example and the contrast between two researchers support that principle. The discussion of abandoning a goal adds the useful point that a permission to stop does not retrospectively establish achievement.

Verdict: revise. The principle is plausible and the central example is intelligible, but the essay has not yet established a substantial new philosophical result. Its most serious problem is not falsity but explanatory redundancy: ordinary evidential reasoning about the wider question already yields nearly everything claimed. A narrower, candid account of this as a diagnostic distinction could survive. A stronger contribution requires a harder case and a clearer account of what existing norms fail to capture.

## Argument vulnerabilities and repairs

1. **The two-question objection is not answered strongly enough.** The objection need not merely list two questions. It can say: the investigator's target is whether all letters received replies; the register is evidence; the note defeats that evidence's coverage; therefore she lacks adequate grounds for the target answer. This explains the conduct as well as the logical difference. No new inquiry-specific rule is required. The paired investigators have identical register knowledge but different target propositions, so ordinary question-relative standards predict the contrast. Remove the claim that a careful question-directed account “needs this addition” unless a specific omission is demonstrated. State instead that the proposed rule makes explicit how a familiar evidential requirement applies when people substitute manageable questions for their targets.

2. **The force of “undefeated reason to doubt” is partly built into the conclusion.** If “undefeated” means a consideration that defeats justification for claiming completion, the rule is circular. If it means any unanswered counterconsideration, it is too strong. Imagine an inquiry seeking an estimate within five percent: a register is known to omit at most one letter in ten thousand. That is a specific unrefuted coverage doubt but may be immaterial to success. The later paragraph on standards gestures at this repair. Build materiality into the initial rule and explain that a doubt matters only if it threatens the standard the inquiry reasonably aimed to meet. Distinguish refuting the doubt from showing that its truth would not affect the warranted answer.

3. **“Only if the relevant letters are covered” is too categorical.** Reliable random sampling could support a broader estimate despite known omissions; a negative answer to “did every letter receive a reply?” needs only one counterexample. Coverage is required by the particular positive universal inference, not by every move from a smaller inquiry to a larger one. Add a counterexample case: one registered letter lacks a reply, so the note about unregistered letters does not prevent answering the original universal question negatively. This contrast gives the rule real precision: discoveries matter through the inference actually used, not merely because the source is incomplete.

4. **The essay moves between completion of inquiry and warranted belief about completion.** It rightly specifies that its norm governs reasonable assessment. Keep that limitation consistent. A person may know all letters were answered from an independent source, yet have an unresolved doubt about what the register establishes. She can claim that the wider issue is resolved, but cannot attribute that achievement to the register alone. The draft acknowledges this; the headline thesis and repeated “must leave the larger issue open” need the same explicit qualification. Separate “the issue is settled” from “this investigation settled it.” Otherwise the rule sometimes appears to demand unnecessary suspension.

5. **The institutional and memory examples introduce a promising but undeveloped consequence.** One possible further argument is that a report should retain the original target alongside the achieved result because later users may otherwise overgeneralize. But that would be a norm of reporting or record preservation, and it needs premises about foreseeable reliance. The current essay should either develop it with a case where every local assertion is true yet the overall completion claim misleads, or leave it as an application rather than a derived obligation.

## Significance and originality

The strongest potential contribution is the separation of a defensible search, a defensible stop, and an indefensible claim of achievement. That helps classify an ordinary failure without demanding endless work. The weakest novelty claim is that an account focused on questions needs a new relation: it can already use the broader question and standard evidential support.

The instrumental-rationality reply shows that a prospective means requirement does not exhaust retrospective assessment. It does not show that ordinary epistemic norms plus an account of goals cannot explain that assessment. Acknowledge this difference. I recommend a candid explanatory contribution rather than announcing a new fundamental norm. A limited search found a nearer discussion the draft should consider: Daniel C. Friedman's *Intentions and Inquiry*, especially §§2.1–2.2 and §6, links inquiry with plans and ordinary instrumental requirements. In the passages I read it does not state the essay's precise retrospective rule. It nevertheless makes the contrast with “effective means” less straightforward. Primary PDF: https://philpapers.org/archive/FRIIAI-8.pdf; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056. This is a lead requiring author reading, not an established priority claim.

## Concrete language review

**Opening:** “An archivist wants to find out whether every letter sent to a small expedition received a reply. She searches the expedition's register. Every letter in it has a reply attached.” The reader immediately understands the task. The setup is functional, not artificial. An optional simplification is “An archivist wants to know whether the expedition replied to every letter it received. She checks its register. Each listed letter has a reply attached.” Keep the omitted-letter discovery close to these sentences.

**Central thesis:** “When an inquirer has a specific, undefeated reason to doubt that an answer settles the issue for which it was sought, the inquirer may not treat that issue as successfully settled just because the answer is known.” “Undefeated,” nested references, and repeated “settles” burden the central sentence. Rewrite: “If the evidence gives you reason to think your answer falls short of your original question, knowing that answer is not enough. You need other grounds for saying the original question is settled.” Explain immediately that a doubt too small to affect the required accuracy does not count.

**Task distinction:** “Call the relation an assumption about the task: answering this question will settle the issue I am investigating.” The label sounds more technical than the idea. Rewrite: “The archivist relies on an assumption: learning about the registered letters will tell her about all the letters.” Then explain how that differs from what the narrow question presupposes.

**Reasoning section:** “The rule concerns reasonable treatment as complete. It does not say that completion becomes metaphysically impossible whenever doubt appears.” “Treatment as complete” is awkward; “metaphysically impossible” adds needless terminology. Rewrite: “The rule concerns what she has grounds to claim. The missing letters might all have received replies, even though she does not know that.” Preserve the separate independent-source exception next.

**Strongest objection:** “The issue is why answering the first question can initially be a reasonable route to answering the second, and why a discovery during that route changes the inquirer's permission to regard the project as finished.” The sentence is understandable but long, and “permission” obscures the ordinary evidential claim. Rewrite: “At first, she had reason to trust the register as a guide to all the letters. The note removes that reason. The question is whether describing this loss tells us anything beyond ordinary rules about evidence.” The final sentence honestly states the objection the essay still needs to answer.

**Instrumental reply:** “The assessment survives the abandonment of the intention.” Short but abstract. Rewrite: “Even after she gives up the search, she can still be mistaken about what she found out.” This preserves the strongest point in that section.

**Changing the task:** “It applies most clearly where the broader issue and the narrow question's role are settled enough for an inquirer to recognize that the connection has failed.” Too many abstract roles and relations. Rewrite: “The rule is clearest when she knows what she originally wanted to find out and why she consulted this source.” Follow with the note showing why that source is inadequate.

Priorities: first fix the novelty claim and materiality condition; then add the negative-answer or sampling contrast; then revise the thesis and abstract passages throughout. Cut repeated defenses of the rule's modesty to make room for these arguments. Plain language should reveal the rule's limits rather than conceal them.
