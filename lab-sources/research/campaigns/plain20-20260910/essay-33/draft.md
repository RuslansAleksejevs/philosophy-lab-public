# A True Answer Is Not Always the End

An archivist wants to find out whether every letter sent to a small expedition received a reply. She searches the expedition's register. Every letter in it has a reply attached. Then she finds a note explaining that some letters arrived while the clerk was away and were never entered in the register. She does not know whether those letters received replies.

The archivist now knows the answer to a precise question: did every registered letter receive a reply? Yes. Her evidence for that answer is excellent. Yet she cannot reasonably regard her original investigation as successfully finished. The problem is not that her answer might be false. It is that she has learned something which breaks the connection between that answer and what she was trying to find out.

This distinction matters for how we judge inquiry. We often make a large question manageable by asking a smaller one. We inspect a sample, consult a record, choose a measure, or test one proposed explanation. These choices can be sensible. But the investigation can reveal that the smaller question no longer does the work for which we chose it. A theory concerned only with whether we know its answer can miss this failure.

I defend a limited rule. When an inquirer has a specific, undefeated reason to doubt that an answer settles the issue for which it was sought, the inquirer may not treat that issue as successfully settled just because the answer is known. The inquirer must address the doubt or leave the larger issue open. This does not require endless research. It distinguishes a reason to stop working from a reason to count the work as finished.

## The question we ask and the task we give it

Some questions are pursued for their own sake. I might wonder how many windows a building has simply because I want to know. If I count them reliably, my inquiry may be over. I need no further purpose to justify it.

Other questions have an assigned job. The archivist asks about entries in the register because she expects them to tell her about the expedition's correspondence. The narrower question is a way of pursuing the wider one. This relation does not have to appear in a written plan. It can be clear from what the person says, which evidence she gathers, and what would satisfy her.

Call the relation an assumption about the task: answering this question will settle the issue I am investigating. That assumption is different from a presupposition of the question itself. A question's presupposition is something its asking takes for granted, such as there having been a theft when we ask who committed it. The archivist's narrower question can have a perfectly sound presupposition and a fully known answer. What fails is the expectation that the register covers the letters she cares about.

This is why the case does not reduce to the familiar advice to correct false premises. No false answer need be accepted. The inquirer might even reject the faulty assumption in words while continuing to treat her project as complete. “Of course the register misses some letters,” she says, “but I have answered the question in my research notes.” She has corrected a belief without correcting her assessment of the inquiry.

Existing work helps locate the problem. Jane Friedman connects inquiry with attitudes directed toward questions and explains the role of belief in settling a question. Her claim concerns belief in a complete answer and its relation to continued inquiry into that question. Nothing in my argument requires denying that relation. The archivist can be finished with the question about registered letters while remaining unfinished with the issue that led her to ask it. The disputed step is the move from one assessment to the other. [Friedman 2019, §§1 and discussion of settledness, author version pp. 18–19.](https://jfriedmanphilo.github.io/IB.pdf)

Arianna Falbo argues that inquiry can be proper even when a question has a false presupposition. Learning through inquiry includes correcting mistakes, not only acquiring an answer. Her proposed norm gives a necessary condition for permissible inquiry in terms of possible epistemic improvement. The present question comes later: after an improvement has occurred, what entitles us to treat the task as done? A person can learn something valuable without achieving the particular result sought. [Falbo 2024, §§1, 3.1, 5.](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.13089)

Nor is it new to treat inquiry as a process whose parts have different roles. Benoit Guilielmo gives an account in which questioning attitudes both initiate inquiry and guide its actions. My concern is one norm within such a process: how discoveries about a question's assigned role should affect the assessment of completion. [Guilielmo 2024, §§3–4.1.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S001221732400012X)

The proposed contribution is therefore narrow. The success of a smaller inquiry does not automatically carry over to the inquiry it serves. Evidence may block that transfer while leaving all the smaller inquiry's knowledge intact. We need a rule for that case, not a general celebration of asking better questions.

## Why the rule follows

Consider two people reading the same register. The first is studying the clerk's filing habits. She wants to know whether every entry has a reply attached. The second is the archivist studying the expedition's correspondence. Both inspect the same entries. Both learn that every entry has a reply. Both read the note about letters that were never registered.

The first can finish successfully. The unregistered letters are outside the task she undertook. The second cannot finish on that basis. The note bears directly on whether the register can answer her question about all letters.

This difference is not a difference in the quality of their knowledge about the register. It comes from the different work that this knowledge was meant to do. We should therefore assess the completion of an inquiry partly by its relation to the issue pursued, rather than by the final answer considered alone.

The reasoning has three steps.

First, a claim of successful completion is a claim of achievement. It says that the relevant task has been accomplished, not merely that some effort has ended. We can dispute what a person achieved while fully agreeing that she has good reason to stop. An exhausted climber may rightly turn back without reaching the summit. An archivist may rightly close her notebook without discovering whether all letters received replies.

Second, success in a part establishes success in a whole only when the part does enough of the required work. Counting the registered replies settles the wider issue only if the relevant letters are covered, or if some other evidence accounts for the omissions. The connection is not automatic. It is something the inquirer relies on.

Third, a specific reason to doubt that connection defeats an inference from the smaller achievement to the larger one. The note does not undermine the count. It undermines what the archivist can conclude from the count. If she treats the original question as settled anyway, she ignores evidence relevant to that assessment.

The rule concerns reasonable treatment as complete. It does not say that completion becomes metaphysically impossible whenever doubt appears. Perhaps every unregistered letter did receive a reply. The wider proposition may be true. Perhaps the archivist could even know it from a second, independent source. The point is that the known answer about the register, by itself, no longer supports the larger assessment in the circumstances described.

“Address the doubt” must also be understood carefully. It does not mean eliminate every possible error. The archivist could discover that the clerk's note refers to a different year. She could find a second archive covering the missing period. Either discovery might restore the answer's ability to settle her issue. Alternatively, she could report that registered correspondence was answered while the remaining correspondence is unresolved. In that case she preserves what she learned without pretending it answers more than it does.

An inquirer need not state all this in philosophical terms. She can simply say, “This tells me about the register, but it does not yet tell me about those other letters.” That ordinary recognition is enough.

## Why this is not a demand to investigate everything

Every answer leaves things unknown. Finding a building's number of windows does not reveal who made them, how often they are cleaned, or whether their arrangement is beautiful. None of this prevents the counting inquiry from succeeding.

The relevant doubt must have a narrower character. It must concern whether the result achieves the task actually pursued. It must have some evidential basis available to the inquirer. And it must remain unanswered by the other information she has. A bare possibility that records could be imperfect does not play the same role as a note identifying omitted letters.

These conditions do not provide a mechanical threshold for every case. How much doubt is enough depends partly on the kind of question and the standard of resolution appropriate to it. An inquiry into an exact total has different requirements from an inquiry seeking a rough estimate. That is not a defect peculiar to the rule. The rule takes the inquiry's reasonable standard as given and asks whether evidence blocks the move from the narrow answer to satisfaction of that standard.

There is also no general duty here to rescue an inquiry once its original route fails. The missing letters may be impossible to trace. The archivist may have another assignment of greater value. She may simply lose interest. In all these cases she can stop. What she cannot gain from a shortage of time, money, or interest is a new evidential connection between the registered and the missing letters.

It can be entirely rational to decide, “I have learned enough to justify spending no more time on this.” That judgment concerns the value of further work. “I have found out whether every letter was answered” concerns what the work established. One may be warranted when the other is not.

This separation also prevents a familiar pressure from distorting inquiry. Institutions often need projects to end on schedule. If stopping and succeeding are treated as the same status, a deadline supplies an incentive to redraw the question until the evidence counts as a full answer. The rule allows a more accurate result: the project ended, this part succeeded, and this part remains open. A deadline need not be denied, and a gap need not be disguised.

## Is this merely a second question?

A natural objection says that nothing interesting has happened. There are two questions: one about registered letters and one about all letters. The first has been answered; the second has not. Ordinary logic is enough.

The description is correct. But it leaves out the conduct requiring explanation. The issue is why answering the first question can initially be a reasonable route to answering the second, and why a discovery during that route changes the inquirer's permission to regard the project as finished. Listing two questions does not yet identify the relation that matters between them.

There can be hundreds of unanswered questions connected to a project. Most need not affect its completion. The missed letters matter because they challenge a connection on which this particular investigation depends. That is the distinction the rule supplies.

The objection does establish a limit. The case does not refute a careful view on which inquiry is always question-directed. Such a view can describe the wider task as another question and add a rule governing the evidence that carries answers between them. My claim is that it needs this addition to assess the whole investigation. It cannot simply identify a question the person has answered and infer that her inquiry has succeeded.

Nor does the argument depend on a controversial account of when one inquiry ends and another begins. We can call the whole sequence a single inquiry with stages, or call it a project containing several inquiries. The same normative fact remains: success with the register does not warrant claiming success about the whole correspondence once the omission has become a live issue.

## Is this just instrumental rationality?

Another objection is stronger. Rational people revise their means when those means stop serving their ends. If the register no longer answers the larger question, the archivist should use another source. Why present this as an epistemological contribution?

The rule does draw on a general thought about means and ends. It would be misleading to claim discovery of that thought. But the present rule is not simply a command to choose more effective means.

A rule about effective means would normally tell the archivist what to do if she keeps pursuing the end. This rule also governs what she may conclude about the achievement when she stops pursuing it. She may abandon the end entirely and still be wrong to think she achieved it. The assessment survives the abandonment of the intention.

Further, the defect need not involve any inefficient action. Suppose the register was the best available source and searching it was the only reasonable first step. After the note appears, no affordable further search is possible. The archivist's sequence of actions can be beyond reasonable criticism. The error begins only when she counts the original issue as resolved.

This gives the rule a distinct use. It separates criticism of an inquirer's conduct from criticism of her assessment of what that conduct established. We can praise her method, accept her decision to stop, and reject her claim of completion. A broad appeal to instrumental rationality may accommodate that distinction, but it does not make the distinction dispensable.

The rule is epistemic in a straightforward sense: it asks the inquirer to respect evidence about the reach of what she has learned. Its source need not be unique to epistemology for its application to clarify an epistemic failure.

## Can the inquirer change her mind about the task?

Of course she can. Curiosity is not a contract requiring someone to retain the same interests forever. The archivist may discover that the registered correspondence is more interesting than the full collection. She may decide to write only about it.

The rule must not forbid that change. It requires only that changing the task not be mistaken for completing the former task. “I am now studying the register” is compatible with “I did not resolve my question about all letters.” The new project can succeed on its own terms.

This matters even when there is no dishonesty and no audience. A person can quietly lower a target and feel that she has reached it. There need be no intention to deceive. Yet when she later relies on the result, she may remember the broad question and forget the narrower basis. A careful assessment should preserve the distinction before memory or reporting makes it harder to recover.

Not every refinement changes the task in this way. Suppose the archivist discovers that by “every letter” she and her colleagues always meant official letters received during one specified month. Making that scope explicit may merely clarify the existing question. Evidence about their prior purpose can establish this. By contrast, narrowing the scope only because difficult letters have appeared does not show that those letters were irrelevant all along.

There will be borderline cases. An early curiosity may be vague, and there may be no determinate answer to what would have satisfied it. In those cases the right description may be that the task developed during the work. My rule should not manufacture a fixed original target where none existed. It applies most clearly where the broader issue and the narrow question's role are settled enough for an inquirer to recognize that the connection has failed.

This limit is useful. We need not solve every problem about the identity of research questions to identify the archivist's mistake. Her own reason for consulting the register, together with the note about omitted letters, supplies enough structure.

## What the end of inquiry should preserve

A good inquiry can end with an answer, a corrected question, a partial result, or a justified decision to leave the matter alone. These endings are not interchangeable. Calling them all “success” can hide the very information the inquiry produced about its limits.

The archivist's discovery is not a failure of curiosity. It gives her a better picture of the evidence. The failure would be to let a known answer silence that discovery. Respect for inquiry therefore sometimes requires keeping an issue open after the work stops.

The central requirement is modest but consequential: preserve the difference between what has been answered and what that answer was supposed to settle. When the investigation itself reveals a gap between them, an inquirer needs grounds for crossing it. Finishing the smaller task supplies no such grounds on its own.
