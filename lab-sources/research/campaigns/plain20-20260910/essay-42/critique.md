# Critique of What Did You Forgive?

## Reconstruction and verdict

The draft argues that what someone forgives depends on the wrong she understood and the possibilities she knowingly included. This allows a discovery to reveal a grievance that was never forgiven without making every new description a new grievance. Two Leah cases supply the central contrast: unsuspected sabotage versus sabotage explicitly considered and forgiven. Concealment cannot make broad words evidence of a broader release than the victim understood.

This is a promising, limited contribution with a clear motivating contrast. It is not yet a defended general boundary. The hardest question is whether new information reveals another grievance or corrects the victim's understanding of the grievance she actually forgave. The draft repeatedly states that distinction but does not yet give enough independent reason to decide it. Revise, preserving the core contrast and avoiding a novelty claim stronger than the evidence supports.

## Main argumentative revisions

1. **Confront the closest contrary case in the main source.** Scarre §3 Example 1 supports the draft's aspect distinction. But §4, paragraph beginning “Category c),” treats discovering malicious intent after forgiving apparent carelessness as withdrawal. That is structurally close to Leah's discovery. The draft's discussion makes Scarre look more unambiguously supportive than he is. Explain why deliberate sabotage adds a distinct wrong while a correction about motive might instead change the whole wrong already forgiven. Or acknowledge that the first Leah's classification is disputed and establish only that explicit acceptance of sabotage defeats one argument that it lay outside forgiveness. Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-015-9651-z, §§3–4.

2. **Separate false explanation from incomplete explanation.** Suppose Leah forgives Sam for deliberately sending a private letter, while mistakenly believing his motive was carelessness. She may have forgiven the actual betrayal under a false belief about its motive, just as someone can trust an actual person under a false belief about his character. Having an understood object does not imply that only its correctly understood features belong to that object. The draft's private-trust analogy therefore supports a rival reading too. Add a case where Sam discloses no motive and Leah simply does not consider it; compare this with affirmative deception and explicit acceptance. Say what distinguishes mistaken forgiveness from partial forgiveness without assuming the intended answer.

3. **Do not make voluntariness do all the work.** The one-click argument correctly rejects physical action-counting, but it defeats only a crude alternative. A rival may individuate wrongs by the interests violated, independently of both the offender's packaging and the victim's chosen description. The essay needs an affirmative reason that the victim's actual stance settles scope. Explain why the actual interests violated and her understanding must work together. At present “morally important” plus “accepted” leaves their relationship unresolved.

4. **Test accepted uncertainty against mistaken scale and significance.** Leah explicitly accepts the possibility of hostility about her job but imagines an angry impulse. She later discovers a calculated attempt to destroy the career supporting her dependent family. Did she accept sabotage, or only a smaller version? Conversely, she forgives losing an object before understanding its enormous practical importance. Kind versus extent cannot settle these examples without further argument. A useful narrower principle would say that explicit acceptance is strong evidence of inclusion, defeasible where the accepted description hid a materially different concern. Explain how that avoids letting every vivid realization reopen scope. Alternatively retain a stronger principle and own its demanding consequence.

5. **Defend the practical importance without assuming a promise.** The draft says real withdrawal creates a special question that protesting an unforgiven grievance does not. This is plausible as a question of consistency, but it is not yet a moral burden. Wonderly's §4 offers explanations involving the quality of one's responses and the relationship, rather than a binding release. Explain whether Leah owes an explanation, has a reason against resentment, or merely needs an accurate description of her history. The manuscript's phrase “permission to undo” overstates the result unless that normative premise is defended. https://www.pgrim.org/philosophersannual/41articles/wonderly-forgiving.pdf, §4, PDF pp.10–12.

6. **Limit the concealment result to what has been established.** The careful section says concealment defeats an inference from broad words, and also allows genuinely knowing broad forgiveness. Keep this formulation. Concealment alone cannot show lack of scope if Leah knowingly forgives whatever Sam concealed. The abstract thesis should say deception cannot supply evidence that she accepted a possibility; it should not suggest offenders lose all ability to point to an independently established broad forgiveness. This is primarily an evidential consequence of the account, not yet a separate normative asymmetry.

## Contribution, significance, literature, accuracy

The aspect distinction is inherited; the proposed addition is the treatment of knowingly accepted uncertainty and its effect on later claims. Its significance is practical and conceptual: it separates disagreement over what was forgiven from disagreement over justified withdrawal. The current draft should present this as a clarification of difficult cases, not a complete new theory.

Independent source checks confirm the relevant Scarre contrast and the broad Wonderly attribution. Wonderly presents her positive alternative tentatively, so “sketches” would be more accurate than language implying a settled account. ## Concrete language review

### Opening

> Leah tells her friend Sam that she forgives him for sharing a private letter. Months later she learns that he shared it to make her lose a job. Sam says that she has already forgiven him for sending the letter. He did not send it twice. Does Leah have to take her forgiveness back before she can properly resent what he did?

The situation is immediately intelligible and not artificial scene-setting. The final question introduces the central distinction before explaining why it matters; “properly resent” is less natural than the preceding prose. Suggested ending: “Sam says she has already forgiven this act. Can Leah now hold his attempt to get her fired against him while still forgiving the breach of confidence?” This is more concrete, though it should not pre-decide whether those wrongs are separable.

### Central thesis, introduction

> When a person forgives, the scope of that forgiveness includes both the wrong she understood and the relevant uncertainty she knowingly accepted.

“Scope” and “relevant uncertainty” make the thesis harder than its example. Rewrite: “What she forgave includes the wrong she understood, along with possibilities she deliberately chose to forgive even without knowing whether they were true.” Follow with a concrete example; the sentence alone still needs it.

### An act and a grievance

> Resentment, as I use the word, is a response to being wronged that continues to press the wrong against the person responsible.

“Press the wrong” is an unfamiliar metaphor, and “as I use the word” makes a simple distinction sound stipulated. Rewrite: “To resent someone is, in part, to keep holding a wrong against them. This need not mean wanting revenge.” Preserve the acknowledgment that this is the sense being discussed, rather than a complete definition.

### How to draw the boundary

> The important distinction is between grasping a kind of injury with an uncertain extent and being unaware of a further kind of mistreatment.

Abstract “kind” and “extent” conceal the hardest judgment. Rewrite: “She might forgive the damage while knowing that its full cost is still unclear. That differs from never realizing that he also meant to hurt her.” Then discuss the hard case where the difference is unclear; easier wording must not hide it.

### Why forgiveness has this shape

> If the identity of the physical action alone settled its scope, Sam could secure a larger release merely by ensuring that several injuries resulted from one click rather than several.

The idea is good but the first clause needs decoding and “secure a larger release” sounds legal. Rewrite: “Suppose forgiving one action covered every wrong done through it. Sam could then get more forgiven simply by causing several harms with one click.” Immediately clarify that this defeats action-counting, not all rival accounts.

### The strongest objection, final section

> In a true withdrawal, the earlier forgiveness itself counts among the considerations to address. Why is it now right to return to a grievance that was released?

“Counts among the considerations” is bureaucratic, and the passage conceals the normative issue. Rewrite: “If Leah is taking back forgiveness, she must explain why she now holds against Sam something she had forgiven. But why must she offer that explanation? Does forgiveness give him a claim on her, or does she simply need a good reason to change her response?” The second and third sentences expose the strongest objection rather than assuming it away.

### Concealment

> This also explains an asymmetry in reasonable expectations.

An unnecessary label interrupts an otherwise concrete section. Rewrite: “The two Sams therefore have different reasons to expect the matter to be settled.” This passage needs no new terminology.

## Priorities

First confront Scarre's malicious-intent example and distinguish mistaken from partial forgiveness. Second test the proposed boundary on an accepted possibility whose significance was misunderstood. Third clarify the normative force of earlier forgiveness and narrow the concealment claim. Finally simplify the recurring language of release, scope, content and stance across the entire essay, retaining useful distinctions through examples. These changes matter more than adding length or a larger bibliography.
