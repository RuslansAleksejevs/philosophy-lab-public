# What Did You Forgive?

Leah forgives her friend Sam for sharing a private letter. Months later she learns that he shared it to make her lose a job. Sam says that she has already forgiven this act. Leah says that she never forgave an attempt to get her fired. Is she taking forgiveness back, or objecting to something she had not forgiven?

Now change the story. Before forgiving Sam, Leah considers the possibility that he meant to get her fired. She understands what that would mean for her livelihood. After taking time, she tells him that she forgives him even if that was his purpose. Later she learns that it was. It is much harder for this Leah to say that the attempted sabotage was never included in her forgiveness.

The difference matters even if both women may now have good reasons to resent Sam. In the second case, the truth of a possibility she deliberately forgave does not by itself show that she never forgave it. In the first, there is a further question about what she actually forgave. Neither the identity of Sam’s action nor Leah’s lack of knowledge settles that question.

I will defend this limited claim about forgiving under uncertainty. Deliberately forgiving a reasonably understood possibility is strong evidence that it was included in one’s forgiveness. The evidence can fail when the person misunderstood the possibility itself. It does not fail merely because learning the truth hurts more than she expected. This claim helps separate disputes about what was forgiven from disputes about whether forgiveness should now be withdrawn. It also limits what an offender can infer from broad words spoken after he has concealed important facts.

## What can remain after forgiveness

To resent someone is, in part, to keep holding a wrong against them. This need not mean wanting revenge. A person may want the wrong acknowledged without wanting its author to suffer. Pamela Hieronymi explains how resentment can protest the meaning of a wrongful event rather than seek harm to its author. This helps us understand how forgiving can leave the judgment that the action was wrong intact. [Hieronymi, preprint pp. 23–25](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0cm3336m/qt0cm3336m_noSplash_b7bb0ca49bb703866019c79cd5dde416.pdf).

I focus on personal forgiveness in which someone stops holding a particular wrong against its author. I do not offer a definition of everything called forgiveness. Someone who forgives a breach of confidence can still remember it and decide to share fewer secrets. Precaution need not be a way of continuing the accusation. Likewise, renewed pain need not be renewed resentment. A person can grieve a loss without reopening her complaint against the person who caused it.

A further wrong creates a different question. Forgiving yesterday’s insult need not cover today’s. But new information about yesterday’s insult is harder to place. It may reveal something not previously forgiven. It may instead correct the victim’s view of the very wrong she forgave.

Geoffrey Scarre recognizes both possibilities. In one example, forgiveness regarding harm to a woman leaves room for resentment concerning harm to her son caused by the same action. Elsewhere he describes discovery of malicious intent behind apparent carelessness as grounds for withdrawing forgiveness. His discussion therefore supports attention to different aspects of a wrong, but does not establish that every newly discovered aspect was previously outside forgiveness. [Scarre, §§3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-015-9651-z).

Leah’s first case falls near this boundary. She might have forgiven only the betrayal of her confidence. But she might have forgiven Sam’s actual treatment of her while misunderstanding his reason for it. We can forgive an actual wrong under a mistaken belief, just as we can trust an actual person while misunderstanding their character. A change in our understanding does not automatically change the object of our earlier response.

The better starting point is the second case. Whatever the limits of the first Leah’s forgiveness, the second has done something that bears directly on them: she has deliberately addressed the possibility later confirmed.

## Forgiving a possibility

Suppose someone asks the second Leah whether she is postponing her decision until she knows why Sam shared the letter. She answers that she is not. She understands that he may have aimed at her dismissal, recognizes this as a serious wrong, and chooses to forgive that too if it occurred. Assume that she means it and that her response is more than a wish to forgive later.

Why count the possible sabotage as included? Because her response is already directed toward it. She need not first believe that it happened. She can recognize what would be wrong about it and stop treating its occurrence as a reason to hold a grievance against Sam. Otherwise her words would amount only to readiness to forgive once the facts arrived. That is specifically not the response described.

This does not make knowledge irrelevant. Leah must understand enough about the possibility for us to identify what she addressed. Hearing the word “sabotage” without knowing what it means would not suffice. Nor would imagining a harmless misunderstanding and calling it sabotage. But exact information is unnecessary. People can understand a threat to their livelihood without knowing each step in the plan.

The argument has a demanding consequence. If Leah understood and forgave that threat, merely confirming that Sam posed it does not establish that it was unforgiven. The confirmation may distress her terribly. Her forgiveness may fail to endure, or she may have grounds to withdraw it. Those possibilities concern what happened to an earlier response that included the sabotage. They do not turn the earlier response into one that never addressed it.

Compare three ways Leah might arrive at forgiveness. Sam might falsely tell her that he acted carelessly. He might disclose no motive, leaving her not to think about it. Or Leah might knowingly forgive the attempt to harm her work without waiting to learn whether it occurred. The first supplies a mistaken belief, the second an unexamined question, and the third a considered answer. Only the third clearly blocks the argument that the motive was absent from forgiveness simply because it was unknown.

I do not infer that the first two cases must be partial forgiveness. They require more evidence. Leah’s words, understanding, and response might show that she addressed only the broken confidence. They might show that she meant to forgive the treatment as a whole despite incomplete knowledge. The difference between false and incomplete information matters, but neither gives a complete rule by itself.

## Understanding enough

The obvious objection is that a person may accept a possibility without understanding its seriousness. Suppose Leah says that she forgives Sam’s hostility about her job. She imagines a passing angry impulse. Later she learns of a calculated effort to destroy the career on which her family depends. Did she forgive that?

The word “hostility” cannot answer. We need to know which possibility she had before her. If she understood that Sam might deliberately seek her dismissal, and understood the threat to her livelihood, she need not also have imagined every planning detail. Learning how coldly he acted may make the accepted possibility harder to bear without making it a different concern. If she understood only that he felt annoyed about her promotion, the larger plan was not established as part of what she considered. A shared word can hide this difference.

There is no rule that every difference in degree stays inside forgiveness while every difference in kind falls outside it. Suppose Leah forgives Sam for destroying a box, imagining that it holds replaceable stationery. It actually holds the only records supporting a claim essential to her financial security. Calling both results property loss conceals what she did not understand. Conversely, forgiving the loss of a box whose value she knows to be uncertain can include a high cost she did not predict.

We should therefore ask what concern she grasped, rather than whether she used the right label. Did she understand that the action might endanger her income? Did she understand that her private words might be used deliberately against her? Did she choose to forgive despite those possibilities? These questions leave difficult borderline cases, but they do more work than simply asking whether later news feels important.

They also constrain later reinterpretation. A person cannot show that she misunderstood merely by pointing out that the harm now seems more vivid. If Leah plainly recognized the threat to her livelihood, its later confirmation does not erase that recognition. Evidence of misunderstanding should identify something she got wrong or failed to grasp about the concern, not merely a stronger feeling about what she already understood.

Where the evidence is unclear, the conclusion should be unclear too. The victim need not have kept a record or spoken with technical precision. But neither party is entitled to manufacture a definite history from ambiguous words. This is a limit on what they can reasonably claim, not a demand that injured people prepare careful statements before forgiving.

## Why facts and understanding both matter

One might object that wrongs are determined by the interests violated, independently of the victim’s understanding. Sam violated Leah’s confidence and threatened her work. Why should her view determine what was forgiven?

The objection is right about what makes the conduct wrong. Leah cannot create an additional injury by describing the same event with another synonym. Nor can she make the attempt to get her fired harmless by failing to notice it. The actual conduct fixes what there is to forgive.

But it does not fix how far her forgiveness reached. A person may forgive one wrong and decline to forgive another. That is possible even when both wrongs arise from one action. Suppose forgiving one action automatically covered everything wrong done through it. Sam could then get more forgiven simply by causing several harms with one click. How he combined his conduct would determine the breadth of her forgiveness. This rejects counting physical actions; it does not reject every account based on the interests violated.

Indeed, those interests help identify the concerns Leah could address. Her response then tells us which she did address, including concerns she knowingly considered as possibilities. The wrong and the response have different roles. Facts about Sam’s conduct prevent Leah from inventing distinct injuries at will. Facts about her forgiveness prevent Sam from treating every actual injury as forgiven merely because it arose through the same conduct.

The resulting view is deliberately incomplete. It gives a clear reason to count a consciously addressed possibility as included, not an exhaustive test for all mistaken forgiveness. This is still useful. Without it, one might assume that forgiving requires knowing each forgiven feature as a fact. Then even the second Leah would have to forgive anew when uncertainty ended. That mistakes the acquisition of certainty for the first occasion on which she could respond to the wrong.

## What concealment can and cannot establish

Return to Sam’s false story that he shared the letter carelessly. He later points out that Leah said “I forgive you,” without adding an exception for an attempt to get her fired.

The omission provides little evidence that she knowingly forgave that possibility. He had encouraged her to think it was absent. He cannot reasonably infer deliberate acceptance from her failure to mention what his account kept out of view. Broad words acquire their meaning in a setting; “everything” does not explain its own boundaries.

This is a conclusion about evidence. It does not show that concealment always prevents broad forgiveness. Leah might say that she knows Sam is withholding something and forgives whatever he did, including an attempt on her livelihood. Independent evidence of such a response could establish that the concealed feature was included. The point is that deception cannot itself supply the missing evidence of her acceptance.

Nor does treating the deception as a separate wrong settle the question. Leah might later forgive the lie while continuing to ask what she had forgiven about the original action. Forgiving the concealment does not automatically expand or contract the earlier forgiveness. The history contains distinct responses that should not be collapsed into one.

The two Sams therefore have different reasons to expect the attempted sabotage to be treated as forgiven. The one who heard Leah knowingly forgive that possibility can point to her response. The one who merely heard general words after offering a misleading account cannot point to the same evidence.

## What follows for renewed resentment?

Monique Wonderly argues that forgiveness can be genuine yet later justifiably withdrawn. She challenges strong commitments against future blame and sketches an alternative that places forgiving and ceasing to forgive within an ongoing moral relationship. [Wonderly, §§2–4](https://www.pgrim.org/philosophersannual/41articles/wonderly-forgiving.pdf). The present argument leaves that possibility open.

Does the second Leah owe Sam a reason before resenting him again? Nothing established here gives him an unconditional right to demand an explanation or immunity from blame. It would be especially wrong to infer that right just from his desire for reassurance.

There is nevertheless a practical difference between acknowledging a change and denying that anything changed. If Leah seeks an honest account of her own response, she should recognize that she once addressed the sabotage. If she discusses their history with Sam, she should not claim that she never considered it when she plainly did. These are requirements of accuracy. They do not require her to keep forgiving.

A moral reason against withdrawal may also arise from circumstances surrounding forgiveness. Perhaps Leah gave a specific assurance which Sam reasonably relied on. Perhaps her present response is vindictive or careless. But such reasons need their own defence. They do not follow simply from calling her present response a withdrawal. Likewise, an unforgiven grievance does not license any intensity or manner of resentment. Accuracy, proportionality, and respect still matter.

The distinction thus changes the questions we ask without deciding the answers in advance. If the wrong was already included, we ask what explains the change and whether the present response is sound. If it was not included, we ask how to respond to an injury still unaddressed. In both cases the victim may be justified. What she should not have to do is obtain permission to feel resentment from the person who wronged her.

Forgiving under uncertainty can be a real response, not merely preparation for one. A person can knowingly forgive a possible wrong before knowing that it occurred. When she later learns the truth, we should take that earlier response seriously without making it an unbreakable promise. The right starting question is not simply whether the facts are new. It is whether the wrong those facts reveal had already been understood and forgiven as a possibility.
