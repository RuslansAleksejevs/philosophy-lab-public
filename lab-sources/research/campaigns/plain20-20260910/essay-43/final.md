# Learning Enough to Disagree

A teacher shows a student how to check a calculation. Months later, the student uses that method and finds a mistake in the teacher’s work. The teacher replies: “You learned that method from me. You cannot use it to decide that I am wrong.”

Something has gone wrong in this reply. A more plausible reply would be: “You have learned the check, but when our answers differ, yours is still more likely to be wrong.” The difference between these replies is the central issue. Learning a method from someone does not give that person permanent control over what the method can show. Yet the student cannot settle every disagreement by announcing that she now thinks for herself. She may have misunderstood the method. The teacher may still know far more than she does.

The difficult question lies between these points. When can a capacity acquired through trust give us a reason to revise that trust?

I will defend a limited answer. A student can have evidence that a teacher has made a particular mistake even while the teacher knows much more overall. That evidence deserves consideration, though it may not settle who is right. If the teacher has treated her work as competent, he cannot make its results count for nothing merely because they now challenge him.

Two claims need to stay separate. One concerns what grounds the student has for believing a particular claim. The other concerns how the teacher assesses her work. A student might have good evidence which her teacher has never recognized. Or a teacher might consistently overestimate her skill. My main argument concerns the second claim: prior recognition of competence constrains how the teacher can dismiss a later challenge. It does not establish that the student must be right.

To say that her result counts as evidence means that it supplies some reason to reconsider his result or reduce confidence in it. Other reasons may outweigh it. This is different from permission to speak, equal expertise, or a right to win the dispute. It also does not require an explanation on demand. A teacher can reasonably lack the time or words to explain everything he knows. The issue is whether his assessment takes account of what the learner can now do.

## The problem with permanent students

Much of what we reasonably believe depends on people whose work we cannot assess. Hardwig makes this dependence central to his account of expertise. He also argues that a novice may raise an objection without being able to judge its force; the relevant expert must make that assessment. He explicitly allows that we can escape dependence on particular experts and choose where to establish autonomy (p. 340). The possibility of local independence is therefore already part of his discussion. My question is finer: what does recognizing a learner’s competence commit a teacher to when they later disagree? [Hardwig 1985, pp. 338–342](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523).

Consider a trainee in a workshop. At first, she takes her instructor’s word about the width of a metal part. She does not know how to use the measuring tool. Later, she learns where to place it, how tightly to close it, how to read its scale, and what can make a reading misleading. She practices on different objects, repeats measurements, and compares results. Eventually, her instructor routinely accepts her measurements without repeating them.

One day the instructor says a particular part is ten millimetres wide. She measures it and obtains twelve. She checks the tool, measures again, and gets the same result. Suppose this is an ordinary case of the kind she has learned to handle.

The instructor’s greater skill still matters. He might notice a damaged edge, a temperature issue, or a confusion about which dimension is needed. These are possible reasons for rejecting her result. His stronger record of accurate measurement could also give him a reason to favor his reading before he has identified her mistake. But the bare fact that he taught her adds no such reason. If he treats her measurements as having no evidential value whenever they disagree with him, simply because she is his student, he is doing more than giving them less weight. He is making her training incapable of providing any challenge to his view.

This can happen without a deliberate plan to dominate. An instructor may sincerely take disagreement as a sign that the learner has not understood. Sometimes that inference is reasonable. A beginner who repeatedly disagrees with a skilled instructor is often making mistakes. The problem is making the inference immune to evidence of learning. If her work counts as learning only when she agrees with him, she can never learn enough to correct him.

That arrangement creates a peculiar kind of permanent student. She can perform the work, perhaps even perform it on the teacher’s behalf. Yet she can never gain a standing to assess his claims. Her work may be worth using when he has no competing answer, yet be denied even a little weight once she challenges him. This total exclusion, rather than unequal weight, is the problem.

## Acquiring a method and judging with it

Two forms of dependence need to be separated. One concerns how a capacity was acquired. The other concerns why a present conclusion should be accepted.

I may depend on a teacher for learning to read. Once I can read a timetable, my reason for thinking a train leaves at eight can be what the timetable says. It need not be a fresh assurance from the teacher. I do not need my teacher to confirm what I read each time I use the skill.

The same point applies when the conclusion concerns the teacher. If he says the timetable reads nine, I can look and see that it reads eight. I do not have to prove that the entire education he gave me was sound. I need enough competence to read this entry, enough care in applying it, and no stronger reason to think this is an unusual case.

Not all learned capacities work as plainly as reading a timetable. Interpreting a poem or assessing a historical explanation leaves much more room for disagreement about what counts as success. But the simple case establishes something important. A general ban on judging a teacher through capacities learned from that teacher is false. The origin of a capacity cannot alone settle whether its present use provides a reason.

This does not escape all dependence. The reader still depends on language conventions, printing, memory, and other people. The trainee depends on the tool’s manufacture and the practice of measurement. Independence from every other person would be an absurd demand. The relevant question is whether the particular disputed judgment must be accepted because this teacher approves it.

A useful way to ask the question is: could the learner explain why she accepts the result without treating the teacher’s agreement with this result as decisive? She might explain how the scale works, what she observed, and how she checked likely sources of error. Her explanation can contain things she originally accepted from the instructor. What matters is whether those things give her a way to distinguish correct from incorrect performance, including his performance.

This is more demanding than possessing a method that sometimes produces disagreement. A broken tool can do that. It is also more demanding than being told that criticism is welcome. A teacher can welcome objections while keeping the sole power to decide whether any objection counts. The learner needs grounds for confidence in a specific skill, as well as room to let its results bear on the teacher’s claims.

## Why this need not be a circle

But why should the student trust her own check? Perhaps her only reason for thinking she can do it is that the teacher told her so. If her result makes her doubt him, should it also make her doubt his praise?

Sometimes it should. Suppose her discovery suggests that the teacher has systematically taught a false method. Using that very method to declare his teaching unreliable may undermine the grounds for her discovery. She may need to suspend judgment about both the result and the training, then seek a different way to investigate.

A local mistake is different. Believing that someone can teach measurement well does not require believing that he measures correctly every time. His instruction might establish a skill whose careful use detects one of his lapses. The student can consistently retain trust in the training while doubting this application. If he has taught her to check that the tool is set to zero, she can notice that he has skipped that step. The conclusion need only be that his present reading needs checking, not that everything he taught is false.

The danger returns if she uses every agreement produced by the training to increase her confidence in him. That would be a different move, and one the present argument does not license.

There is a familiar danger here. Weisberg discusses how accepting a source’s reports and then counting those accepted reports as confirmations of the source can create an apparent evidential gain. His analysis also rejects an indiscriminate ban on learning about a source through that source. The structure of the support matters. [Weisberg 2010, §§1, 3.1, 3.3; author manuscript](https://philosophy.rutgers.edu/joomlatools-files/docman-files/BootstrappinginGeneral.pdf).

The proposed answer must not be: “My teacher says that I can now verify my teacher, so verification is complete.” Praise alone cannot do this work. Nor can the student count every agreement produced by instruction as a separate reason to believe the instructor reliable. A class trained to repeat one answer does not become a panel of independent witnesses merely because it has many members.

But successful learning can introduce something beyond repeated assurances. In the workshop, a part may or may not fit an opening. The trainee can test a measurement against that result. The teacher may have introduced the test, but he does not make a wrongly measured part fit by approving it. Different applications can expose misunderstandings which rehearsed examples conceal. Other competent people can check a disputed result without owing their verdict to this instructor. These are possible ways to gain grounds for confidence; no single one is required in every case.

The claim about the learner’s evidence is consequently conditional. Where the learner has no grounds for assessing her performance apart from the teacher’s current verdict, I have not shown that she has gained the standing at issue. Where she does have such grounds, their being acquired during instruction does not cancel them.

Even this limited change can matter before the learner can assess the method as a whole. She may know enough to establish that the teacher has misapplied a rule while remaining unable to justify every assumption behind the practice. To find an addition error in a long calculation, she need not understand the whole project. Her correction may therefore be narrower than “the teacher’s conclusion is false.” It may be: “This step has not been established as written.”

That distinction prevents local learning from becoming a claim to general authority. A student can be right about an error and wrong about its importance. The teacher can repair a step without abandoning the conclusion. What has changed is that the learner’s criticism now gives a reason to reconsider the work. Whether the teacher must explain his response is a further question about teaching. It does not follow from this claim about evidence alone.

## What a teacher’s own commitments establish

There is a further argument which does not require the learner to prove her competence from scratch. It begins from the instructor’s own treatment of her work.

Suppose he regularly accepts her use of a method as competent. He assigns her tasks which require it and relies on her results. She uses the same tool, follows the same steps, and checks the same kinds of parts. This time, however, her reading differs from his.

We must not infer that he should now give their readings equal weight. Imagine that her routine readings are accurate about 95 percent of the time, while his are accurate about 99.9 percent of the time. These figures are an invented example, not a claim about trainees. They make it reasonable to accept much of her unattended work and also to suspect her reading more strongly when the two conflict. The precise weight depends on matters such as whether their errors tend to occur together. Still, delegation need not imply equality.

He can therefore say: “Your check gives me some reason to look again, although I have stronger grounds for trusting my reading.” He need not retract his earlier recognition of her competence. Nor must he identify a procedural mistake before retaining greater confidence in his answer. The difference between having some evidence and having the better evidence does real work here.

What he cannot reasonably say is: “Your result has no value as evidence because you learned the check from me.” The earlier recognition established, by his own assessment, that her use of the method was capable of tracking the size of a part. Her learning it from him was already known. Repeating that fact supplies no new reason to erase its evidential value.

Here is the argument. First, treating her work as competent commits him to treating its results as at least potentially informative in suitable cases. Second, the new case remains within that range unless there is reason to think otherwise. Third, being his student was part of the accepted situation; it does not become a defect just because her answer now differs. So that relationship alone cannot justify assigning the result no weight. Relative reliability may justify unequal weight. A discovered limit, or evidence that prior success was misleading, may justify withdrawing confidence. Neither is the blanket exclusion under discussion.

There is an important qualification. Agreement with a very reliable teacher can itself be evidence of competence, and repeated disagreement can be evidence of its absence. The teacher need not ignore those facts. But he must assess them alongside other evidence of learning. A rule that recognizes no possible demonstration of competence once it challenges him is not an assessment of that evidence. It prevents such assessment.

This argument leaves room for uncertainty, including different confidence levels in teacher and student. Each may know things about the present check that the other does not. The teacher may have tacit expertise he cannot readily explain. The student may know that she checked unusually carefully. My claim is not that rationality forces immediate agreement. It is that one familiar reason for refusing to reconsider—the learner’s continuing relationship to the teacher—cannot undo evidence of learning by itself.

A teacher might avoid inconsistency by never recognizing any independent competence. He might say that every student result is acceptable only because he has approved that particular result. Such a position escapes this specific argument, but at a price. Agreement with him then provides the learner with no new test of his reliability. He cannot fairly advertise the student’s growing agreement as evidence that she has learned to judge for herself.

There are thus two separate conclusions. A learner with adequately grounded competence may have reason to challenge a teacher. And a teacher who has recognized such competence cannot dismiss its results simply by pointing out that he is still her teacher. The second conclusion is about consistency in the teacher’s reasons. It does not by itself establish the first. A teacher can consistently overestimate a student, and both can be mistaken.

## Trust can survive correction

A worry remains that this picture makes learning too suspicious. Students often need confidence that mistakes will be met with patience. Asking them continually to test the teacher could damage the relationship that makes learning possible.

Platz argues that trust in educational relationships helps students acquire confidence in their own capacities. She also distinguishes that confidence from actual competence and notes the importance of room for students’ own judgment. These claims show why trust and the ability to assess one’s work should not be treated as rival educational goals. [Platz 2021, “Trust and the student’s development of epistemic confidence,” and note 8](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9752.12560).

My argument imposes no duty to conduct continual tests. A student can accept almost everything a teacher says while possessing a reason to question one claim. Nor does correction require a withdrawal of personal trust. An instructor who explains a mistake, acknowledges a limit, or accepts a correction can give the student further reason to trust his willingness to help her learn.

Still, willingness to accept small corrections does not establish trustworthiness everywhere. A manipulative teacher might concede harmless errors to protect central claims from scrutiny. We must ask whether the competence demonstrated in one area bears on the disputed matter. The ability to correct spelling says little about an instructor’s account of justice. Small victories do not license a large conclusion about the whole relationship.

Conversely, refusing one correction does not establish dishonesty. The instructor may be tired or may reasonably see a complication the student has missed. The proper response may be to postpone judgment and return to the check. The target is a pattern of reasoning which makes disagreement disqualifying by itself, not every impatient classroom exchange.

The institutional point is easy to miss. Permission to speak does not ensure that a contribution can change anyone’s mind. A classroom could invite unlimited questions while treating every challenge as proof that its author still needs instruction. Conversely, a class with little disagreement might allow real correction whenever a good reason appears. Counting objections would not distinguish them. We need to ask whether demonstrated learning can make a challenge count.

## The learner’s burden has limits

The hardest cases concern methods whose success cannot be readily checked. A philosophical teacher might reshape the student’s standards for what counts as a good argument. A religious teacher might reshape what the student takes to be evidence at all. Can the student ever decide that this is improvement rather than training in loyalty?

The present argument does not supply a universal test. A sincere and careful learner may lack enough grounds to resolve the question. She may need contact with other teachers, a wider range of cases, or time away from the relationship. None of these guarantees success, and each can introduce new dependencies.

What the argument does supply is a constraint on one response to this difficulty. The difficulty of evaluating an education cannot entitle its provider to make agreement the only possible evidence of maturity. Agreement on basic matters may be a useful sign of learning; persistent elementary error may show that more teaching is needed. What is excluded is a rule which refuses to let any other evidence of competence alter that judgment. Such a rule would prevent learning from ever qualifying the teacher’s authority.

Nor must the learner settle the teacher’s overall reliability before taking a particular concern seriously. She can retain much of what she has learned and suspend judgment about a disputed part. She can say that she does not yet know whether the teacher is wrong, but that his answer has not resolved the problem. This is often the appropriate halfway point between obedience and rejection.

The philosophical gain is modest but real. Trust need not justify itself by producing more trust. It can justify giving someone time to teach us, and that teaching can give us capacities whose later use changes what we have reason to accept. She may now be able to check some of his claims while still needing his help with others. Further learning can change that balance.

A teacher does not own the reasons a student learns to recognize. Once those reasons are available to her, his role in making them available cannot alone take them away.
