# Critique: Learning Enough to Disagree

## Reconstruction and verdict

The essay defends two different claims. First, acquiring a skill from a teacher does not make the teacher’s approval necessary for every justified use of it. Second, a teacher who has recognized a learner’s competence cannot erase that recognition merely because the learner challenges him. The first is a claim about the learner’s evidence; the second is a constraint on the teacher’s reasoning. Their separation near the end of the commitments section is essential and should appear earlier.

Revise, with the second claim as the center. The narrow prohibition on treating disagreement as automatic disqualification is defensible. The stronger suggestion that accepted competence obliges a teacher to supply a procedural answer needs qualification. The source review also finds a more explicit precursor in Hardwig than the draft acknowledges.

## Argument: consequential vulnerabilities

1. **Delegation is not a commitment to equal weight in disagreement.** Imagine a trainee who gets ordinary measurements right 95% of the time and an instructor who gets them right 99.9% of the time. It is rational to accept her unattended routine work and also rational, on discovering a conflict, to suspect her result more strongly. No new defect in her procedure need yet be identified. Disagreement is new evidence even when the task type is unchanged. The teacher need not retract his earlier assessment of her competence. This is a counterexample to the apparent dilemma in “Either her earlier work did not warrant the confidence he placed in it, or his new rejection lacks a relevant basis.”

The draft already says disagreement can be evidence, but it does not let that concession change the dilemma. Distinguish three acts: accepting that her result is evidence, deciding how much weight it has, and accepting its conclusion. Preserve the first without automatically requiring the third. A teacher may say, reasonably, “Your check gives us a reason to look again, although my record gives me more reason to trust my result.” He must not say her result has no evidential force because he taught her. Discuss this case explicitly rather than only the plainly arrogant reply in the opening.

2. **A duty to answer is not yet derived from reasons for belief.** The claim that a criticism “requires an answer at the level of the work” sounds interpersonal or pedagogical. A person can possess adequate evidence against a criticism but lack time to explain it; an expert can have tacit skill she cannot fully articulate. If the thesis concerns rational belief alone, require appropriate consideration and weighing, not invariably a communicable diagnosis. If teaching imposes a further duty to explain, argue for that duty separately. An unresolved disagreement can rationally leave teacher and student with different confidence levels.

3. **The bootstrapping objection changes the target.** The opening question concerns revising trust, while the strongest-worry paragraph asks whether a student can use mastery to confirm reliability. Those are different moves. The most relevant objection asks whether teacher-dependent confidence can support *disconfirmation*. A learner may accept a teacher’s competent instruction without believing him infallible; a particular error can coexist with trustworthy instruction. Explain this local compatibility. A more serious case occurs when the finding undermines the very training on which the check depends. Then the learner may need to suspend judgment about both. The current physical-fit checks help, but the distinction between correcting an application and discrediting the method deserves a direct argument.

4. **The hard-domain conclusion exceeds the easy-domain support.** The closing ban on counting agreement as maturity can sound unconditional. Yet agreement about very basic matters can legitimately count toward competence, and repeated elementary disagreement against a strong expert consensus can count against it. The bad rule is not using agreement as evidence; it is refusing any possible contrary evidence of acquired competence. Explicitly retain that distinction in the final section. Otherwise the careful “alone” qualifications disappear at the point where the essay generalizes.

## Originality and significance

The contribution is best described as a focused argument about selective withdrawal of recognized competence. Learning skills that permit local independence, and combining trust with autonomy, are background rather than new results. Hardwig already explicitly permits escaping dependence on particular experts and choosing where to establish autonomy (1985, p.340). The draft’s “temporal limit” paragraph should acknowledge this, not merely say that he fails to prove permanent novice status. The advance must be finer: what prior recognition commits a teacher to when a particular challenge arises.

A targeted search also found the primary-work lead “Intellectual Authority and Education,” linked in the retrieval notes, on educational objections to preemption of learners’ reasons. Both full-text routes failed. It would be useful to investigate before making a strong originality claim. This is a lead only; I cannot establish its exact relation. The present search neither demonstrates novelty nor establishes that the narrow argument is already published.

The issue matters because institutional permission to speak can coexist with systematic refusal to treat speech as evidence. Develop that implication through the revised distinction between evidential standing and winning a dispute. No additional dramatic example is needed.

- Weisberg: §§1, 3.1 and 3.3 support the account of feedback and rejection of an unrestricted circularity ban. The essay should use this as a constraint, not imply that it has supplied Weisberg’s full theory.
- Platz: the subsection on developing epistemic confidence distinguishes confidence from actual capacity and connects the former with trust. Verified HTML subsection and conclusion at https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1467-9752.12560 . The underlying empirical research was not independently checked; the manuscript appropriately attributes these claims rather than announcing causal proof.
- Nguyen: verified abstract at https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/abs/echo-chambers-and-epistemic-bubbles/5D4AC3A808C538E17C50A7C09EC706F0 . The analogy is fair but peripheral. The sentence announcing abstract-only consultation interrupts the essay; the reference already states the limit. It can be removed from the body without overstating engagement.

## Concrete language review

### Opening

“A teacher shows a student how to check a calculation. Months later, the student uses that method and finds a mistake in the teacher’s work. The teacher replies: ‘You learned that method from me. You cannot use it to decide that I am wrong.’”

The people, action and problem are immediately understandable. This is useful scene-setting, not artificial atmosphere. The weakness is argumentative: the reply is so unreasonable that it makes the problem look easier than it is. Keep the opening, but soon add the more plausible reply: “You have learned the check, but when our answers differ, yours is still more likely to be wrong.” Optional simpler opening: “A student uses a check her teacher taught her and finds a mistake in his work. He replies: ‘I taught you that check. You cannot use it to show that I am wrong.’” The existing version is equally readable.

### Thesis paragraph

“A learner can acquire a right to judge particular matters before she acquires the teacher’s general expertise.”

“Right to judge” can mean permission to speak, justified belief, or equal authority. The following disclaimer does not fully choose between them. Rewrite: “A student can have evidence that a teacher has made a particular mistake even while the teacher knows much more overall.” Add: “That evidence deserves consideration, though it may not settle who is right.” This clarifies both the philosophy and ordinary meaning.

### The problem with permanent students

“If every successful performance counts as progress until it produces disagreement, there is no route by which progress can alter the distribution of authority.”

The conditional is readable, but “alter the distribution of authority” makes a concrete problem sound administrative. Rewrite: “If her work counts as learning only when she agrees with him, she can never learn enough to correct him.” Retain “only” to target the inflexible rule rather than ordinary evidence from agreement.

### Acquiring a method and judging with it

“The causal history of my ability does not remain a premise in every use of it.”

“Causal history” and “premise” are unexplained technical terms where ordinary speech suffices. Rewrite: “I do not need my teacher to confirm what I read each time I use the skill.” If the deeper distinction is needed: “Who taught me to read is one question. What this timetable says is another.”

### Strongest objection: Why this need not be a circle

“The learner thinks she has mastered the method because the teacher has praised her. She trusts that praise because she trusts the teacher. She then uses her supposed mastery to conclude that the teacher deserves trust.”

The chain is exceptionally easy to follow; no jargon needs removing. Its difficulty is relevance: it argues against confirming trust, not challenging a teacher. Replace or supplement with: “But why should she trust her own check? Perhaps her only reason for thinking she can do it is that the teacher told her so. If her result makes her doubt him, should it also make her doubt his praise?” Then distinguish a local mistake from wholesale unreliability. This makes the strongest objection address the thesis.

### What a teacher’s own commitments establish

“A new performance matches the relevant features of those accepted performances. Its only identified difference is that it conflicts with a claim he has made.”

“Relevant features” conceals the contested premise, and “performance” is distant from the workshop. Rewrite: “She uses the same tool, follows the same steps, and checks the same kinds of parts. This time, however, her reading differs from his.” Then explain why disagreement is still new evidence, as objection 1 requires. Plain language should expose this difficulty rather than stipulate it away.

### The learner’s burden has limits

“The resulting authority is uneven: broad in some matters, narrow in others, and open to revision.”

Whose authority? The learner’s or teacher’s? Rewrite: “She may now be able to check some of his claims while still needing his help with others. Further learning can change that balance.” This gives the ending a clear subject and avoids an abstract summary.

## Prioritized revision

First, repair the delegation/disagreement argument and define what it means for evidence to count. Second, separate a reason to question from a duty to provide an explanation. Third, turn the circularity objection toward loss of trust and explicitly acknowledge Hardwig’s local autonomy passage. Finally, simplify the abstract passages throughout, keeping the already clear examples and narrowing the final generalization. The essay can become more significant through this precision; adding length or more citations alone will not accomplish it.
