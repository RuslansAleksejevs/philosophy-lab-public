# What an Archive Cannot Tell Us

A town council votes to raise a tax. The surviving register gives the decision and the names of those who signed it. Did the councillors support the tax, accept it reluctantly, or sign under threat? The register says nothing about this. Its calm wording may tempt a historian to picture a calm meeting.

Now suppose we learn how the register was made. Clerks copied final decisions from rough notes. Their instructions told them to leave out speeches and disagreements. Comparisons from other years show that they followed this rule after both peaceful and bitter meetings. The rough notes for the year in question were later destroyed.

This discovery does not tell us what happened in the room. But it changes what the surviving page can support. Its formal wording no longer counts in favour of a peaceful meeting, because the same wording would have survived after an angry one.

The danger does not end when a historian avoids saying, “The meeting was peaceful.” An explanation may still run like this: the councillors shared a commitment to the town, so they accepted the tax and secured stable government. The decision itself may be well supported. The shared commitment is not. Yet placing the two inside one smooth explanation can make the support for the decision seem to cover the claimed motive as well.

I will call this a transfer of warrant. A coarse fact—here, that the council adopted the tax—has good evidence. A finer link—here, that willing cooperation caused the decision—separates two rival accounts. The narrative lets the finer link borrow the authority of the coarse fact even though the record-making process erased the evidence needed to support that link.

This essay defends a modest bridge principle. Suppose we have good evidence that the way a record was made or preserved would produce roughly the same specified surviving features under either of two serious accounts. Those features provide little or no reason to choose between the accounts. If the disputed difference does important work in a historical explanation, the historian must support it with other evidence, defeat the account of how the record was made, or make its conjectural status clear enough for readers to assess the explanation. This principle joins a familiar point about evidence to a less often stated duty in explanatory writing. It is a proposed refinement, not a claim to have discovered the logic of archival silence.

## Knowing why the record is silent

Consider two historians with the same council register. The first has searched for descriptions of the debate and found none. The second has also found the copying instruction, checked examples of its use, and traced the loss of the rough notes. Both withhold judgment about the debate. Their knowledge is still different.

The first may reasonably keep searching similar register entries for a revealing phrase. The second has a reason to doubt that this search will help. She can say which new evidence would matter: a participant's letter, a rough note, or perhaps an unusual delay whose place in the record was not standardized. She can also explain why polite official wording is weak evidence for polite conduct.

This knowledge concerns the path from event to record. Evidence about that path can survive even when evidence about the event does not. A copying rule may remain in an administrative manual. Other years may preserve both rough notes and fair copies. The destruction of documents may itself have been recorded. We need not know what a lost speech said in order to know that speeches were normally excluded.

The argument depends on independent support for the copying practice. Uniform entries alone would not prove it, because uniform entries might reflect uniform meetings. It would be circular to infer suppression from uniformity and then use suppression to explain the same uniformity. In the example, the instruction and comparisons between rough and final records do the work. A real inquiry must also ask whether the practice continued in the year being studied.

This yields useful knowledge even when it yields no answer about the meeting. It identifies a failed route of inference and points toward better evidence. It can also reveal how an institution presented itself. A register designed to preserve binding decisions may have had no dishonest purpose. Still, later readers can mistake its administrative calm for social harmony. Explaining that mistake is a historical result.

## Neutrality is local and often graded

The phrase “the archive erased the difference” is too broad. A claim of evidential neutrality must name four things: two rival accounts, the process that produced the record, the surviving features under consideration, and the shared facts on which the comparison is conditional.

In the council case, let one account say that a calm debate ended in willing agreement. Let the other say that an angry debate ended in reluctant agreement. Assume that both accounts end with the same decision and signatures, and that the clerks follow the same copying rule. Whether the debate was calm or angry, the official wording would then look the same. That wording does not favour either account.

The conclusion changes if the rivals predict different retained features. Suppose coercion usually prevents some councillors from signing, while peaceful agreement usually produces all signatures. Since the register preserves signatures, a fully signed page could favour peace over that version of coercion. The copying rule removes speeches; it does not remove every clue. A fair comparison cannot hold fixed a feature on which the rivals actually differ and then announce that the whole document is neutral.

Real cases are also less exact than the invented one. Clerks may follow a rule unevenly. Angry meetings may leave marginal marks slightly more often than peaceful meetings. If so, the record is not wholly neutral. The right conclusion is graded. Evidence about standardization weakens an inference to the extent that it makes the observed feature similarly likely under the rivals. An indirect clue matters when we can explain both how it entered the record and why it would be more likely under one account.

Timothy McGrew makes the underlying comparative point about silence. An omission has evidential force only in relation to competing expectations of that omission. His account separates whether an event would be noticed, recorded, preserved, and known to present investigators. The present argument uses that logic rather than adding a new rule of probability. Its question is what a historian may carry from that logic into an explanation. [McGrew, author manuscript, pp. 8–13](https://timothymcgrew.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/The-Argument-from-Silence-Acta-Analytica-Tim-2013.pdf).

## The bridge from evidence to explanation

Why does local neutrality constrain a narrative? Begin with an ordinary evidential norm: confidence in a claim should answer to the total evidence for it. If the official wording would be the same after peace or conflict, that wording cannot raise confidence in peace over conflict. This is the evidential part of the bridge principle.

Historical explanations create a further risk because their parts do not appear as isolated claims. Consider two sentences:

“The council adopted the tax. The town then entered a period of stable government.”

“The councillors' shared commitment enabled them to adopt the tax and secure stable government.”

The first sentence reports two events. The second adds an attitude and makes it the cause that connects them. Readers may reasonably take that connection to be part of what the evidence supports. But the register supports the decision, not the shared attitude. The explanation has transferred warrant from a supported outcome to a link that distinguishes the peaceful account from the reluctant one.

This transfer is invalid for a simple reason. Evidence for a claim shared by two rivals cannot, by itself, support the feature on which they differ. A tax decision appears in both accounts. Its evidence therefore cannot settle whether consent or reluctant compliance produced it. Coherence does not fix the problem. Both causal stories may be coherent, and a story's smoothness is not independent evidence for its disputed link.

The historian has three direct replies. She may cite letters, diaries, voting patterns, or later conduct that support willing cooperation. She may show that the alleged copying practice did not apply, or that a retained feature remains more likely after a peaceful meeting. Or she may present cooperation as a conjecture and explain what would change if agreement was reluctant. Each reply lets a reader see where the explanation is firm and where it is vulnerable.

This does not mean that every uncertainty belongs in public prose. We should distinguish an evidential duty from a presentational duty. The historian must not count a neutralized feature as comparative evidence, whether or not she discusses the matter in print. A duty to tell readers arises when three conditions meet. The rival preserves the well-supported coarse facts; independent evidence or established background knowledge gives it more than negligible plausibility; and the disputed link materially affects the explanation. The need for explicit treatment grows with the importance of the link and the strength of the case for neutrality.

This threshold excludes idle possibilities. A story about impostors replacing the council has no claim on the reader merely because it is logically possible. Reluctant agreement does have a claim when conflict is common in such bodies, the record-making process suppresses debate, and willing cooperation is doing central causal work. A peripheral possibility may require no mention. A central causal claim requires enough disclosure for the reader to judge it.

Space still matters. A short work may handle the duty in a sentence or note. But silence is not harmless when an ordinary reader would otherwise take a central conjecture as established. Readers need to know which link is evidenced because they cannot assess the explanation without that information.

## Granularity and narrative

Christophe Malaterre argues that historical justification depends on the level of detail in the question, the evidence, and the background theories. A coarse claim can be justified while a finer one remains underdetermined. He also treats local underdetermination as relative to a claim and its network of justification, and notes that a connected narrative can be locally underdetermined when one of its statements is. [Malaterre, sections 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-024-00583-8).

That argument comes close to the present one and limits any claim of originality. My proposed addition is a specific diagnosis and response. A production process may leave evidence for a coarse event while neutralizing evidence for a finer causal or motivational link. When a narrative makes that link appear to inherit support from the event, warrant has been transferred where the justification network does not allow it. The response is not always to retreat to the coarser claim. It may be to mark the link as conjectural, test the explanation under a serious rival, or seek evidence that escaped the production process.

Simply saying less can hide the problem. If the historical question is how the council maintained power, “the tax was adopted” is true but does not answer it. The difference between consent and reluctant compliance matters to the announced explanation. The historian should either change the question or show where the answer runs out.

The reason for the missing detail may itself become part of the answer. Michel-Rolph Trouillot identifies silences at four moments: fact creation, fact assembly, fact retrieval, and retrospective significance. Different silences call for different inquiries. [Trouillot, chapter 1, “Theorizing Ambiguity and Tracking Power”](https://nyu.manifoldapp.org/read/silencing-the-past-excerpt-trulliot/section/2f251038-7193-4e95-aba1-01953ed871c3). The bridge principle connects two of these moments. An omission made while a source or archive was formed should constrain the later retrieval and explanation of the past. Otherwise, a known limit of the record can reappear as an apparent fact in the narrative.

## A less tidy case: protest in Venice

The council example is designed to make the logic visible. A real case shows why the principle is useful when evidence is messy. Maartje van Gelder and Filippo de Vivo study a 1569 revolt by workers at the Venetian Arsenal. Government records gave a thin and muted picture of the unrest. If those records were read alone, their official calm could easily pass into an explanation of political calm.

Van Gelder and de Vivo do two things that the bridge principle recommends. First, they investigate how the official record was produced and how protest was suppressed in it. This weakens the inference from a quiet file to a quiet city. Second, they use non-governmental sources to reconstruct the revolt. Those sources do not merely add detail to the same official story. They supply evidence from a route that was not neutralized by the government's recording practice. [Van Gelder and de Vivo, introduction and conclusion](https://academic.oup.com/past/article/258/1/44/6532405).

The relevant rivals can be stated carefully. Both may accept that the government continued to act and that its archive preserved official decisions. They differ over whether this surface continuity reflected social peace or papered over serious conflict. The selected calm features of the government record have weak force between those rivals once archival suppression is established. Non-governmental reports and surviving indirect traces then change the evidential balance.

This case also shows why the conclusion is neither global scepticism nor permanent ignorance. The official archive was limited; the whole evidential world was not. The production history redirected inquiry toward other sources and supported a stronger account. A historian contributes something valuable by showing how an omission during record-making later came to look like a fact about public order, even when full recovery remains impossible.

This application is only schematic. It relies on the article's introduction and conclusion rather than a new study of the Venetian documents. It therefore illustrates the bridge principle but does not independently settle the history of the revolt.

## Objections

One objection is that attention to neutralization may discourage research. Records often preserve indirect evidence that their makers did not intend to keep. A clerk may omit speeches but retain delays, changes of order, or unusual signatures.

That objection defeats a claim that an archive is simply mute. It supports the narrower formulation offered here. The historian must identify the surviving features at issue. If an indirect feature is more likely after conflict and its path into the record is credible, it can distinguish the accounts. Learning how a record was made may guide research by showing where leakage is possible.

A second objection appeals to inference to the best explanation. Perhaps peaceful cooperation best explains the town's later stability even if the register does not distinguish peace from conflict.

That inference is allowed. Later stability, institutional structure, and knowledge of the councillors may favour cooperation. But those considerations must bear the weight. Stable government may also result from strong procedures that contain deep disagreement. The historian should ask whether stability itself is more likely under one process. Repeating that the council successfully made decisions adds nothing if successful decisions are shared by both accounts.

A third objection is that the history of the archive may be as uncertain as the history of the event. A copying instruction does not prove obedience, and examples from other years may not apply.

This is correct. The strength of the conclusion cannot exceed the evidence for the production model. Firm evidence of uniform copying may neutralize a feature; weaker evidence may only reduce its force. The bridge principle does not turn uncertainty into certainty. It tells us how uncertainty about production should change the support assigned to a historical link.

A final objection says that the principle is only a case of the general rule against claiming more than the evidence supports. At the deepest level, it is. The archive-specific contribution is diagnostic rather than foundational. It shows a recurring way in which an explanation can violate that rule while every coarse event claim remains true. It also gives a practical test: name the rivals, the production process, the surviving features, and the explanatory link; then ask whether support for shared facts has been made to carry the disputed link.

## An answer with a boundary

A historical inquiry can succeed by showing why an attractive explanation is not supported by the evidence usually offered for it. This is more than admitting failure. It identifies the blocked inference and tells us what a better defence would require.

The result must remain local. A council register may establish an adopted tax while leaving willing cooperation unresolved. A signed page may still matter if signatures are retained and predicted differently by the rivals. Other sources may later settle the dispute. Until then, an explanation that makes cooperation central must support it elsewhere or let readers see that it remains a proposal.

The history of a record therefore belongs inside the assessment of the history written from it. We should ask both which traces survived and which differences the recording process was capable of preserving. When a process makes selected features look the same under serious rivals, those features cannot choose between them. A narrative must not let the authority of what the record does establish spill over onto what it cannot.
