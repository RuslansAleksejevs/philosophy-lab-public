# Critique of “What an Archive Cannot Tell Us”

## Overall verdict

The essay has a clear and defensible core: evidence about how a record was produced can show that the record is unable to distinguish two accounts, even when the record fits either account; a historical explanation that relies on the distinction must therefore supply another reason or mark the link as conjectural. The essay also handles two common overreaches well. It does not infer that the rival accounts are equally probable overall, and it does not infer that no future evidence could decide between them.

The main problem is that the essay has not yet shown that its “demand” is a distinct philosophical contribution rather than a direct application of familiar evidential support to historical narratives. Malaterre comes closer than the essay acknowledges: he treats underdetermination as relative to a claim and justification network, includes information-destroying processes in that network, and explicitly extends the analysis to interconnected narratives. McGrew supplies the contrastive likelihood point. Trouillot connects silences in source production to later narrative production. The draft must explain exactly what follows from combining these ideas that none already supplies. Its strongest possible answer is a narrowly formulated bridge principle about explanatory dependence, but that principle needs a more exact trigger, scope, and justification.

## Reconstruction of the argument

The argument is best reconstructed as follows.

1. A record supports a historical difference only if some feature of the surviving evidence is more expected under one account than under a serious rival.
2. Evidence about record production can establish that a process would remove or standardize the feature that separates those accounts.
3. When that process makes the surviving record equally expected under the two accounts, the record contributes no comparative support for the disputed difference, even though it may support a coarser claim shared by both.
4. Historical explanations contain not only event claims but attitudes, causal links, and other relations. An explanation can therefore insert an unsupported difference while every coarse event claim in it remains true.
5. If such a difference does important explanatory work, responsible historical writing must either support it through evidence not neutralized by the production process, defeat the account of neutralization, or disclose its conjectural status and show the consequence of alternatives.
6. This obligation is local to the specified evidence, contrast, and explanatory use. It does not imply equal posterior probability, global scepticism, or permanent unknowability.

Steps 1–3 are largely applications of contrastive confirmation and local underdetermination. Step 4 usefully identifies the place where the problem may hide: a narrative's causal or motivational connection rather than its separately stated facts. Step 5 is the candidate contribution. The essay should foreground and defend that bridge.

## Most consequential vulnerabilities

### 1. The key bridge is asserted more than argued

The essay says that a known source limitation “must” be respected by the narrative. But why does local evidential neutrality create the specific duties listed in the essay? One answer is a general norm: a historian should proportion confidence to total evidence and should not present an unsupported explanatory link as established. If that fully explains the duty, the archive-specific principle adds diagnosis and application, not a new norm. That can still be worthwhile, but the contribution must be described modestly.

The stronger route is to argue that explanations create a special risk of evidential laundering. A record may support coarse fact C. A narrative then embeds C in an unsupported mechanism M and, because C is well supported, M inherits an appearance of support. The production history blocks that inheritance. This is more precise than saying narratives are more than lists. State the error as an invalid transfer of warrant from the shared fact to the differentiating link. Then explain why disclosure or robustness analysis is required when that link carries a central explanation: readers otherwise cannot tell which part of the explanation is evidenced.

### 2. “Removed the difference” is ambiguous

At times this means that a known copying policy would omit speeches. Elsewhere it sounds as if the historical process literally destroyed all information capable of distinguishing the accounts. Those are different claims. A clerk's instruction may make the absence of recorded dissent uninformative, yet wording, timing, order of signatures, marginalia, correlated decisions, or later behaviour may still have unequal likelihoods under the rivals. The first objection acknowledges this, but the thesis needs the restriction from the start.

Define the target as a feature set, not “the record” without qualification: given accounts H1 and H2, production model M, and surviving features E, M supports the claim that E has roughly the same likelihood under H1 and H2. The conclusion is only that E supplies little or no likelihood-ratio support between them. It does not show that the whole document, much less the whole archive, is neutral.

The essay should also separate deterministic standardization from probabilistic suppression. Real clerks may follow rules unevenly. If dissent makes leakage slightly more likely, the record is not wholly neutral. The correct response is graded: the production history weakens an inference in proportion to how much it reduces the likelihood difference.

### 3. The threshold for a “serious alternative” is too loose

The proposed criteria—fit with facts, credible behaviour, and explanatory relevance—admit many rivals. If every psychologically credible account triggers a duty, a historian may face an unmanageable set. If only rivals independently supported by evidence count, the essay should say so. The impostor example excludes a cartoon case but does not settle the hard middle.

A better trigger would combine three conditions: the rival preserves the well-supported coarse facts; it differs on a link that materially affects the explanation; and either independent evidence or established background knowledge gives it non-negligible plausibility. The duty can then scale with the role of the disputed link and the strength of the neutralization evidence. A central causal claim requires explicit treatment; a peripheral possibility may merit no mention.

### 4. The essay shifts between evidence and public writing

Sometimes the demand is epistemic: the historian may not count the register as evidence for peace. Sometimes it is communicative: the historian must tell readers that cooperation is conjectural or show what changes under a rival. These norms can come apart. A historian might privately assess the probabilities correctly but omit a qualification for reasons of space. Conversely, a caveat can be present while the underlying inference remains poor.

Distinguish two conclusions. The evidential conclusion is that the neutralized feature cannot increase the comparative support of the chosen account. The presentational conclusion arises when the disputed difference is central and a normal reader would otherwise take it as established: the historian should make its status assessable. This second claim needs a premise about what readers need in order to evaluate an explanation. The draft gestures at that premise but does not defend its threshold.

### 5. Background probabilities remain underspecified

The essay rightly says that the accounts need not be equally probable. Yet “the entry ... add[s] no support for peace over disagreement” is true only relative to the specified production model and selected features. If the fact of signing itself is more likely under voluntary agreement than coercion, then holding the signatories and decision fixed may stipulate away relevant evidence. The comparison is legitimate only if the question concerns attitudes conditional on an agreed set of facts. Say this explicitly.

This also yields a counterexample. Suppose coercion usually prevents all councillors from signing, while peaceful consensus usually produces all signatures. The copying rule deletes speeches but not signatures. The register then supports peace over coercion, although it says nothing about debate. The essay should distinguish rivals that differ only in omitted material from rivals that predict different retained outcomes.

### 6. The example risks making the conclusion too easy

The invented council case is clean because the instruction explicitly says to omit disagreement and comparison copies confirm compliance. Few cases will be so neat. The Venetian example shows suppression and recovery, but the draft does not apply its proposed test to a real contested explanatory claim. A concrete application would reveal difficult choices: which evidence is fixed, which features were suppressed, which rival is serious, and what exactly a historian must revise.

Add one compact real case, or use Van Gelder and de Vivo more analytically. Identify an inference that official calm indicates political calm; identify the production and suppression evidence that defeats it; then show how non-governmental evidence changes the conclusion. Without this step, the essay demonstrates a plausible principle but not its value in hard practice.

## Significance and originality

The topic is significant because historical explanations often depend on attitudes and causal connections not stated in records. Showing how support for a coarse event can be mistakenly transferred to a finer explanatory relation would improve both historical reasoning and reader-facing qualification.

The genuinely promising contribution is the proposed bridge from production-based neutralization to a norm for explanatory links. The surrounding components are established: McGrew gives the contrastive logic of silence and the notice/record/survival chain; Malaterre gives claim-relative local underdetermination, information-destroying processes, granularity, and the extension to connected narratives; Trouillot tracks silence through source, archive, retrieval, and significance; Van Gelder and de Vivo exemplify suppression and recovery through independent sources. The draft presently cites these as background but does not compare its bridge to Malaterre's section 6 claim that a narrative may be locally underdetermined when one constituent statement is. It must do so.

A defensible originality claim would be: the essay derives a presentational and justificatory requirement for a particular class of historical explanations from a production-based finding of evidential neutrality, and identifies warrant transfer from coarse fact to explanatory link as the error. A brief search cannot establish that no one has made this argument. John Lange's full article, the full archival-silence literature, and work on narrative explanation were not accessed. The essay should call this a proposed synthesis or refinement unless wider review supports a stronger claim.

## Source accuracy and literature engagement

The McGrew citation is accurate. His manuscript says the force of silence is contrastive, and pp. 8–12 distinguish notice, record, survival, and present awareness. The Malaterre citation is also accurate, but the discussion is too slight given how much of the conceptual structure it anticipates. Cite sections 5–7 more directly and state the difference between his justification-network result and this essay's proposed obligation.

The summary of Trouillot is substantially accurate, with one wording correction: his four moments are fact creation, fact assembly, fact retrieval, and retrospective significance. “Use in narratives” is understandable, but “retrieval” is the closer locator. The Van Gelder and de Vivo characterization is accurate: the publisher text says they use non-governmental sources to reconstruct the revolt and investigate its exclusion from government records. Their article also offers the best route to a non-toy application.

I checked the public author manuscript or publisher HTML for all four cited works. I did not access Lange's full article, the whole of Trouillot's book, the full *Silence of the Archive*, or a comprehensive body of work on narrative explanation. I therefore remain uncertain about priority and about whether the proposed obligation already has a standard formulation in historiography.

## Language review

### Opening

The opening begins: “A town council agrees to raise a tax. Its surviving register gives the decision and the names of those who signed it. Did the councillors welcome the tax, accept it reluctantly, or sign after threats?” This is immediately understandable. It gives the object, the evidence, and the disputed question before introducing theory. “A town council votes to raise a tax” may be slightly better than “agrees,” because “agrees” can already suggest voluntary unity, which the example later puts in doubt. Suggested revision: “A town council votes to raise a tax. The surviving register gives the decision and the names of those who signed it. Did the councillors support the tax, accept it reluctantly, or sign under threat?”

### Central thesis

Original: “When there is good evidence that a process of recording or preservation removed the difference between two serious accounts of the past, a historian cannot treat the resulting record as choosing between those accounts.”

The sentence is readable, but “removed the difference” suggests that the accounts themselves became identical or that every trace was erased. The argument concerns selected features of surviving evidence. Suggested revision: “Suppose we have good evidence that the way a record was made or preserved would give us the same surviving features under either of two serious accounts. Those features cannot tell us which account is better.”

### The claimed contribution

Original: “The underlying logic of missing evidence is familiar. What needs attention is the passage from understanding a record's limits to writing a historical explanation.”

“The passage” is an abstract label, and the reader still does not know what goes wrong during it. Suggested revision: “The logic of missing evidence is familiar. The harder problem is how a known limit of a record should change the explanation a historian writes from it.”

### Comparative support

Original: “Keep the final decision, signatories, and relevant clerical practice fixed. Under that practice, either meeting produces the same official entry.”

This is the essay's crucial comparison, but “keep ... fixed” arrives as unexplained model-building language. Suggested revision: “Assume that both versions end with the same decision and signatures, and that the clerks follow the same copying rule. Whether the debate was calm or angry, the official entry would then look the same.”

### Strongest objection

Original: “The question is whether an actual feature provides a reason to distinguish the competing accounts under a defensible account of how that feature arose.”

This packs the evidence, contrast, and production model into one abstract sentence. Suggested revision: “An indirect clue matters only if we can explain two things: how it entered the record, and why it would be more likely under one account than the other.”

### Narrative duty

Original: “The historian who explains this transition has made a positive contribution even if she cannot recover the lost debate.”

“This transition” has no concrete referent close enough for an unfamiliar reader. Suggested revision: “A historian contributes something valuable by showing how an omission made during record-keeping later came to look like a fact about the meeting, even if she cannot recover the lost debate.”

### Final formulation

Original: “We study not only what traces survive, but what differences those traces were made to preserve.”

This is clear and memorable, but “were made to preserve” may imply conscious design in cases of accidental preservation. Suggested revision: “We should ask both which traces survived and which differences the recording process was capable of preserving.”

Across the essay, the prose is mostly natural and unusually clear. The main recurring difficulty is not sentence length but vague nouns—“difference,” “transition,” “connection,” and “demand”—whose reference shifts. Replace them with the precise item at issue: a retained feature, an omitted attitude, an explanatory link, or a duty to disclose uncertainty.

## Prioritized revisions

1. Formulate the contribution as a bridge principle about invalid transfer of warrant from a supported coarse fact to a neutralized explanatory link, and argue for the principle rather than merely naming a demand.
2. Define the neutralization claim relative to two hypotheses, a production model, and specified surviving features. Allow graded weakening when suppression is imperfect.
3. Separate the evidential norm from the public-writing norm and give a threshold for when disclosure or robustness analysis is required.
4. Set a workable standard for “serious alternative” and clarify that the comparison holds shared facts fixed rather than ignoring evidence that differs between rivals.
5. Engage Malaterre's narrative-level discussion directly and narrow the originality claim. Add one real application, preferably using Van Gelder and de Vivo, to demonstrate the principle under less ideal conditions.
6. Revise the handful of abstract formulations identified above, especially the thesis and strongest objection, while preserving the direct opening and the essay's useful local/global distinctions.
