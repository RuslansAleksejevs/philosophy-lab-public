# What an Archive Cannot Tell Us

A town council agrees to raise a tax. Its surviving register gives the decision and the names of those who signed it. Did the councillors welcome the tax, accept it reluctantly, or sign after threats? The register says nothing about this. A historian may be tempted to read its calm language as evidence of a calm meeting.

Now suppose we learn how the register was made. Clerks copied final decisions from rough notes. They were instructed to leave out speeches and disagreements. Examples from other years show that they followed this rule even after bitter meetings. The rough notes for the year we are studying were later destroyed. The surviving page has not changed, but what it can reasonably tell us has.

We still do not know how the meeting went. Yet we now know something better than the bare fact that we have not found an answer. We can explain why this record fails to answer the question. That explanation places a demand on any history built from it. If a historian describes a peaceful agreement, the support for “peaceful” must come from somewhere the copying rule did not already neutralize.

This essay defends that demand. When there is good evidence that a process of recording or preservation removed the difference between two serious accounts of the past, a historian cannot treat the resulting record as choosing between those accounts. A narrative that nevertheless chooses must identify an additional reason that survives this objection. The demand is local: it concerns a stated difference and a particular body of evidence. It neither makes the accounts equally likely overall nor declares the past forever unknowable.

The underlying logic of missing evidence is familiar. What needs attention is the passage from understanding a record's limits to writing a historical explanation. A historian can avoid false factual sentences and still arrange a true record into a story that quietly restores distinctions the record has lost. Explaining why those distinctions disappeared is one way to prevent this mistake.

## Learning why we do not know

Consider two historians with the same council register. The first has looked for descriptions of the debate and found none. The second has also found the copying instruction, checked examples of its use, and traced the loss of the rough notes. Both withhold judgment about the meeting. Their position is nevertheless different.

The first may sensibly continue looking through similar register entries for a revealing phrase. The second has a reason to doubt that this search will help. She can explain which sort of new source would matter: a participant's letter, for example, or a surviving rough note. She can also challenge an inference from the register's polite wording to the participants' polite conduct. Her greater knowledge does not consist in a better guess about the meeting. It consists in a better account of the connection between that meeting and its surviving record.

Such knowledge is possible because the event and the recording process can leave different evidence. A rule for copying documents may survive when a particular set of originals does not. Other examples may reveal what the copyists omitted. The destruction of papers may itself be recorded. We need not know the content of a lost disagreement in order to know that disagreements were normally excluded from the official register.

This point requires care. Uniform register entries alone would not establish the copying rule. Their uniformity might reflect genuinely uniform meetings. Using the uniformity first to prove that the clerks removed disagreement, and then using the removal to explain the uniformity, would provide little independent support. In the example, the instruction and comparisons between rough notes and fair copies do the important work. A real inquiry would also have to establish that the practice continued in the year at issue.

The example is invented to isolate a common kind of problem. Historical investigation can establish the production of silence without stopping at silence. Maartje van Gelder and Filippo de Vivo reconstruct Venetian protests in 1569 using sources outside the government's own records. They also examine how official records concealed conflict. Their study shows why one archive's silence must not be confused with the limits of all surviving evidence. It is a case of successful recovery, not a warrant for despair. [Van Gelder and de Vivo, introduction and conclusion](https://academic.oup.com/past/article/258/1/44/6532405).

## What follows from a record's silence?

It helps to distinguish three claims. We may have failed to find evidence that separates two accounts. We may have good reason to think that a specified record would look the same under either account. Or we may think that no possible future inquiry could separate them. Each claim is stronger than the last in a different way. A failed search does not establish how the record was produced. Knowledge of that production does not establish the fate of every other trace.

The middle claim is the one we need. In the council example, compare a peaceful acceptance of the tax with a reluctant acceptance after an angry debate. Keep the final decision, signatories, and relevant clerical practice fixed. Under that practice, either meeting produces the same official entry. The entry can support the claim that the tax was adopted, while adding no support for peace over disagreement.

This is a comparative point. A record may be exactly what we expect after a peaceful meeting and still fail to support that account over a rival. We must also ask what we would expect after a contentious meeting. Timothy McGrew's analysis of arguments from silence makes this general requirement explicit: the evidential force of an omission depends on the competing expectations of that omission. His account also separates noticing an event, recording it, and the record surviving into present knowledge. [McGrew, author manuscript, pp. 8–13](https://timothymcgrew.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/The-Argument-from-Silence-Acta-Analytica-Tim-2013.pdf).

No new rule of probability is needed here. The philosophical issue is what happens when a historian embeds the unsupported distinction in an explanation. Consider: “The councillors accepted the tax, and the town entered a period of stable government.” Each clause might have solid evidence. Now consider: “The councillors' shared commitment to the town allowed them to accept the tax and secure stable government.” This version supplies an attitude and a causal connection. The register's support for the decision cannot simply be carried across to those additions.

A historian may have excellent independent reasons for them. But if the only defence is that the decision was signed without recorded dissent, knowledge of the copying practice defeats the defence. It does so even if peaceful cooperation remains the historian's favourite account.

The relevant demand can be put plainly. Once an account of how the record was made explains why an important difference would disappear, anyone using that record to assert the difference must answer that explanation. The answer may challenge the account of record-making, point to a feature it fails to explain, or supply further evidence. Merely making the historical story more coherent does not answer it.

## Why a narrative must preserve this limit

Why impose this demand on historical writing? A familiar answer is that historians should not claim more than their evidence supports. That is correct but leaves the practical question open: which additions exceed the evidence? A demonstrated loss of a particular distinction gives the general rule a definite target.

Suppose a history of the town says only that the register contains no recorded dissent. Taken literally, that is true. But the chapter uses this fact to explain the council's unity, and the account of unity explains the later stability. The wording has encouraged the reader to make precisely the inference that the copying practice blocks. Adding a footnote saying that all archives are incomplete would not repair the explanation. The qualification must bear on the claimed unity.

The reason is that historical explanation offers more than a list of true propositions. It asks a reader to understand why events happened. If a crucial link is merely possible, the reader should not receive it as an established link. An author need not describe every doubt. But a known limitation that undermines a central explanatory step is part of what the reader needs to assess that step.

There are several honest ways to write the council's history. The historian could leave the participants' attitudes unresolved and explain the later stability by factors that have better support. She could defend peaceful cooperation from letters or other evidence and state that the register itself cannot settle it. She could present cooperation as a conjecture, explain its appeal, and show how the account changes if the agreement was reluctant. These are different literary choices, but each makes the disputed connection available for judgment.

This is not a demand that every rival story receive equal space. An account must be a serious alternative before the obligation arises. It should fit established facts, draw on a credible way that people or institutions act, and matter to the explanation being offered. The mere possibility that the councillors were secretly replaced by impostors creates no comparable burden. Reluctant agreement matters because the historian is explaining stability through willing cooperation, and disagreement followed by a binding decision is a credible competing process.

Even a serious alternative need not be equally probable. Suppose private letters show that these councillors usually cooperated, while their institutional arrangements made coercion difficult. Those facts could favour peaceful acceptance. The point is that they must do the work openly. The official entry should not be counted again as fresh confirmation of peace when the copying practice makes that feature uninformative.

A further distinction follows. Sometimes the conclusion should be “we do not know which account is right.” Sometimes it should be “this account is better supported, but not for the reason usually given.” Establishing the limits of a record can justify either conclusion. It does not automatically settle the overall balance of evidence.

## A smaller claim is not always an adequate answer

One response is simply to say less. If we cannot establish peaceful agreement, why not report only the tax decision? Often that is exactly right. Christophe Malaterre argues that the degree of detail in a historical claim affects its justification. A broad claim can be well supported while a more detailed version remains unresolved; changes in evidence and background theories can alter that boundary. [Malaterre, sections 3–6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-024-00583-8).

But reducing detail can also hide why the missing detail matters. If the question is how the council maintained power, the difference between consent and reluctant compliance is not ornamental. An account that quietly retreats to “a decision was adopted” has established something, but it has not answered that question. The historian should either change the announced question or explain where the answer runs out.

Moreover, the reason the record lacks detail can itself enter the history. A practice of excluding disagreement tells us something about how an institution represented its actions. This does not by itself prove deceit. A register might omit debate because officials needed a clear record of binding decisions. Its limited purpose may be entirely ordinary. But if later readers take it as a record of unanimous feeling, an administrative choice has acquired a further consequence: it shapes a picture of the institution that the record was not equipped to establish.

Michel-Rolph Trouillot's account of historical silences is an important starting point here. He distinguishes silences arising in the creation of sources, their assembly into archives, their use in narratives, and the assignment of historical significance. He also insists that different silences require different investigations. [Trouillot, chapter 1, “Theorizing Ambiguity and Tracking Power”](https://nyu.manifoldapp.org/read/silencing-the-past-excerpt-trulliot/section/2f251038-7193-4e95-aba1-01953ed871c3).

The present argument concerns a particular connection between these stages. A demonstrated omission in recording should constrain a later narrative that relies on the record. If the narrative restores the omitted distinction without further support, it turns a known limit of the source into an apparent fact about the past. The historian who explains this transition has made a positive contribution even if she cannot recover the lost debate.

## Three objections

The first objection is that the proposed demand could become an excuse to stop researching. Historians repeatedly find evidence where earlier investigators saw none. A clerk who leaves out dissent may accidentally preserve it in an unusual delay, a changed name, or a difference between copies. A record need not say something directly to show it indirectly.

This objection defeats any easy claim of permanent unknowability. It does not defeat the narrower demand. The question is whether an actual feature provides a reason to distinguish the competing accounts under a defensible account of how that feature arose. If an unusual delay would be more likely after dissent, it may count. The claim that the register does not distinguish the accounts must then be narrowed or withdrawn.

Indeed, explaining the omission can improve the search. If fair copies suppress speeches but retain dates, delays become worth investigating. If both dates and speeches were standardized, other documents become more promising. Knowledge of the record's limits is a guide to asking better questions, provided it remains tied to specific features and practices.

The second objection concerns inference to the best explanation. Perhaps peaceful cooperation best explains the town's long stability, even though the register cannot distinguish peace from conflict. Historians should be allowed to infer hidden causes from a broad pattern.

They should. The proposed demand does not require an eyewitness document for every claim. Evidence of the later stability, together with a credible account of how cooperation produces it, might support the inference. But the rival needs a fair hearing at that point. Stable government might also arise from strong procedures that allow deep disagreements without institutional collapse. The historian must show why the observed stability favours one process. Repeating that the council successfully made decisions does not suffice if successful decisions were already held fixed in both accounts.

The third objection is that our account of the archive can be as doubtful as our account of the meeting. A copying instruction does not prove obedience. Comparisons from other years may not apply. Historians cannot avoid uncertainty by moving it one step away.

That is right. The claim about record-making needs its own evidence, and its strength limits the conclusion drawn from it. If we only suspect selective copying, we have grounds for caution rather than a firm finding that the register contributes nothing. The argument is not a machine for producing certainty out of ignorance. It identifies a kind of conclusion available when the relevant production history is better established than the disputed event detail.

The difference is still valuable. We often know how a form was designed without knowing how a particular person felt while completing it. There is no general reason why uncertainty about the latter must infect every claim about the former. What matters is whether the grounds actually differ, not whether both claims concern the past.

## An answer with a boundary

A historical investigation can succeed by showing why an attractive answer is not supported by the evidence usually offered for it. This is more than an admission of failure. It identifies a missing connection, explains its absence, and tells us what a better defence would require.

The result should stay as precise as the investigation permits. The council register may establish an adopted tax while leaving willing cooperation unresolved. Other evidence may settle that question later. Until then, a history that makes cooperation carry the explanation must either defend it elsewhere or show the reader that the explanation remains a proposal.

This approach gives the history of an archive a direct role in assessing the history written from it. We study not only what traces survive, but what differences those traces were made to preserve. When we learn that a difference was removed, the removal becomes a fact the narrative must respect. We may still lack the answer to how the meeting went. We need no longer mistake the register's silence for the councillors' agreement.
