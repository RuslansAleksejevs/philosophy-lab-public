# Who Gets to Tell the Tool It Is Wrong?

A manager uses a program to arrange the shifts at a small theatre. The program works well. It fills every post, observes the agreed hours, and shares unpopular shifts fairly. Before publishing a schedule, the manager can change any part of it. A large button says “Reject.”

Now suppose that all staff requests also pass through the program. It decides which messages concern scheduling and places the relevant ones before the manager. A worker writes that she cannot take the evening shift because she must accompany her father home after an operation. The program classifies the message as a personal update. The manager never sees it.

The manager could reject the schedule. She has the time, authority, and practical skill to do so. Yet none of these powers answers the problem. The information that would give her a reason to use them has been stopped by the same tool whose recommendation she is meant to assess.

I argue that retaining control over delegated decisions requires more than retaining a way to refuse them. For the kinds of reasons a person is expected to consider, there must be a usable way for those reasons to reach her even when the recommending tool fails to recognize them. This need not mean doing the tool's work unaided. It may mean keeping a conversation open, using another instrument, or asking another person. What matters is whether a failure in the recommendation can also silence the grounds for questioning it.

This is a claim about a person's continuing power to review a delegated task. It is not a complete account of autonomy, and it does not require a human veto over every machine action. Its purpose is to identify a loss of control that can remain hidden even when consent, explanation, and override are all present.

## A power that lacks its occasion

Consider two versions of the theatre. In the first, workers can send a request directly to the manager as well as through the scheduling program. In the second, the program is the only route. Everything else is the same: the manager, the schedule, the program's accuracy so far, and the reject button.

The first manager can learn that an otherwise good schedule overlooks a serious need. The second can learn this only if the program first judges the information worth showing her. Both managers can issue a contrary instruction. Only the first has a route for discovering this particular reason to issue it when the program fails.

This difference matters even before any message is actually lost. A person who checks a locked door has a different security arrangement from a person who asks the lock whether it is secure. Their houses might have equally quiet histories. The difference concerns what would happen under a relevant failure, not how often trouble has happened so far.

The comparison does not show that the second manager has no agency. She chose to use the program, chose the schedule's aims, and may still exercise judgment over costs or staffing levels. Nor does it show that every decision she makes through the program is unfree. It shows a more precise loss. With respect to needs that the program excludes, her advertised power of review has no dependable occasion for use.

A random refusal would not repair this. She could reject one schedule in ten, but this would not make her rejection responsive to the worker's need. Control through review involves being able to change course for a reason. A capacity to produce a different outcome by chance is a different capacity.

The claim also concerns more than ignorance in a single case. Anyone can overlook a message. The difficulty in the second theatre is built into the arrangement: the very mistake that creates a need for review removes the evidence that could prompt it. Asking the manager to “use her judgment” leaves that dependence untouched.

## What the existing accounts already establish

The inadequacy of a human override is not a new discovery. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven argue that meaningful human control requires systems to respond to relevant human reasons and requires an appropriate connection to human understanding and responsibility. Their account includes the wider arrangement of people and machines, not just the final operator. It also allows a system to remain under human control without tracking that operator's reasons in every circumstance. My question is narrower: which routes must remain open if this particular person is supposed to retain the task of reconsidering the recommendation? Their account supplies a reason to ask that question; it does not make a button an adequate answer. [Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018, “Tracking” and “Tracing.”](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00015/full)

Bainbridge identifies a related problem in automated work. People may lose the skills that they will later need for exceptional cases. Checking automated performance can itself exceed their abilities, and adding a display or alarm raises further questions about checking that aid. These difficulties make it unreasonable to treat the human monitor as an unlimited reserve of competence. [Bainbridge 1983, §§1.1–2.1, pp. 775–777.](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)

Work on contesting algorithmic decisions adds another piece. Freiesleben, Meding, and König distinguish changing one's circumstances to obtain a different result from challenging the result's correctness. They argue that ordinary explanations may fail to establish grounds for reversing a particular decision. Their discussion of evidence outside the original set of features is especially relevant here. [Freiesleben, Meding, and König 2026, §§1 and 5.3.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.16041v1)

These ideas rule out any broad claim that the need for informed challenge has been overlooked. The contribution proposed here is a specific test for the arrangement that carries information to a reviewer: when the recommending tool rejects a kind of consideration, can that consideration still reach the person charged with reviewing it? This test directs attention to a relation between failures. A channel may supply excellent information in normal use while failing precisely when the review is most needed.

## Why the route matters

The argument can now be stated more carefully. Suppose a person retains responsibility for reviewing recommendations in light of a certain kind of consideration. At the theatre, the manager is expected to consider serious conflicts between shifts and staff needs. Such review requires some practical capacity to learn about those conflicts. If the only way she can learn about them depends on the recommending program already treating them as relevant, she cannot review the program's failure to treat them as relevant through that arrangement. The same judgment is both the subject of review and a condition of access to the review. Her control is therefore incomplete in the very area she has supposedly retained.

The conclusion is relative to a kind of failure. It does not demand access to every possible fact. Nobody could review a schedule against the entire world. The theatre needs to decide which matters the manager must remain able to consider: urgent care needs, unequal treatment, broken promises, and so on. That decision requires reasons of its own. A provider cannot make the review adequate merely by defining its scope as “whatever our system already notices.” But neither must the scope include remote possibilities with no reasonable connection to the work.

Once that scope is fixed, a useful question is concrete. Suppose the program keeps recommending the same schedule and keeps dismissing an important care request. Could the request still reach the manager in time for her to act? A direct conversation could do this. A separate mailbox could do it, provided she can actually read it. A second tool could do it if it receives the original message and does not have to accept the first tool's classification. None guarantees success. Each prevents this one failure from automatically blocking every response to it.

A second tool is not enough merely because it has a different name. If it reads only summaries prepared by the scheduler, the missing request remains missing. Conversely, two functions within one product might provide the needed route. A fixed inbox that displays every staff message can remain useful when a nearby recommendation model misclassifies one. The relevant independence is independence from the error being checked, not a count of companies, interfaces, or devices.

The person must also be able to do something with the message. A mailbox buried under thousands of irrelevant notices is a weak route. A complaint arriving after the schedule becomes irreversible is too late. A worker who risks punishment for using the channel may reasonably stay silent. These facts do not replace the argument about access. They specify what makes access usable. A route exists for purposes of review only when ordinary people can use it, understand enough of what arrives, and respond within the relevant time.

This requirement is less demanding than retaining the ability to arrange every schedule by hand. The manager might be unable to solve a complicated staffing problem yet still understand that a proposed shift breaks an important promise. She can ask for a new proposal with that constraint included. If the program refuses, she can postpone publication or seek help. Detecting a reason to reconsider and producing the replacement are different tasks.

That distinction is important for tools that expand what people can do. Someone who relies on speech software need not demonstrate unaided speech in order to govern what she says. Someone using a calculator need not recreate all its calculations to notice that the wrong prices were entered. A demand for self-sufficiency would often take away the very agency that assistance provides. The demand defended here instead asks whether people can bring relevant doubts into their use of assistance.

## The strongest objection: all judgment depends on something

There is no wholly independent channel of knowledge. The manager trusts the worker's account, her own memory, and perhaps another program. Each may fail. Requiring a further check on every check would lead to an endless chain. Worse, tools can sometimes notice their own errors better than their users or competitors can. Why deny that such tools support human control?

The objection succeeds against a requirement of total independence. It does not defeat the more limited requirement here. The manager need not verify every source before hearing the care request. She needs a route that survives the scheduler's refusal to classify that request correctly. The worker can be mistaken or dishonest; the manager can then investigate. The first task is to keep investigation possible without requiring the disputed judgment to approve it.

There is no infinite chain because the demand is tied to a specified review task and a specified failure. A person checking whether the reported price matches a shop receipt may use her eyesight without also conducting an inquiry into vision. In a different task, where faulty vision is the suspected source of error, that same method may be inadequate. Whether one process can check another depends on what is being checked.

A tool's self-criticism can also be helpful. Suppose the scheduler independently checks whether it has dropped any staff message and shows omissions to the manager. That may meet the requirement concerning message loss. The proposed test asks what the checking process actually relies on. If it simply repeats the classification that dropped the message, it adds no route around that failure. If it compares the displayed messages with an unfiltered record, it might. A tool does not cease to support review merely because it belongs to the system under review.

This answer admits an important limit. No finite arrangement can preserve review against every correlated failure. The manager, worker, and program might share an unnoticed assumption. Control comes in degrees, and claims about it should name their scope. The criterion identifies a preventable form of closure. It does not promise freedom from all dependence.

## Could giving up review be an exercise of agency?

Perhaps the second manager understands the arrangement and deliberately accepts it. She wants to spend her time on rehearsals. The program is usually better at scheduling, and missed exceptions are an acceptable price. Would calling this a loss of control disrespect her own choice?

No. Exercising a power can result in giving some of it up. A person can freely authorize an agent to settle a matter without retaining a meaningful opportunity to reconsider each decision. The initial authorization may be fully her own. What does not follow is that every later decision remains under her active supervision because an unused reject button is still present.

The distinction matters for honest descriptions of delegation. The theatre may choose largely automatic scheduling with only limited managerial review. If that arrangement is justified, it should be defended in those terms. It should not claim comprehensive human judgment while excluding information the judgment would require. Consent to delegation and continuing control over its particular results answer different questions.

Nor does stronger review always produce better decisions. Direct complaints can favor the most confident workers. Managers can be biased, and a well-designed program might treat quiet workers more fairly. This is a serious objection to badly designed review procedures. It is not a reason to confuse the absence of a usable route with its presence. One might provide confidential assistance, give every worker the same opportunity to raise a conflict, and require the manager to explain exceptions. Whether those measures are worth their costs is a practical question. The philosophical point is that better average performance cannot by itself establish continuing human review.

Sometimes there is no need for it. A person may knowingly delegate an unimportant choice without wanting to hear exceptions. Sometimes there is no time for it, as when an automatic safety device acts faster than a person could. In such cases control may lie in choosing, maintaining, or limiting the device rather than reconsidering each output. My claim concerns the cases in which a person is supposed to retain that latter task. It sets a condition for the task being real, not a universal demand that every task must be retained.

## The division of work that needs defending

The natural temptation is to divide work by outputs: the machine proposes, the person approves. But this division leaves unanswered who determines what the person can consider. If the machine both proposes an action and controls every route by which a reason against it can become visible, a final human decision may disguise a much larger delegation.

A better division keeps the scope of review in view. What kinds of reasons must this person remain able to consider? How could each reach her if the recommendation process overlooks it? Can she understand enough to pause, question, or redirect what happens? These questions allow extensive reliance on tools. They also reveal when an apparently supportive tool has become the only judge of when its user should doubt it.

The theatre does not have to abandon its scheduler. It has to decide whether the manager really retains review of staff needs. If she does, workers need a usable way to reach her when the scheduler thinks their needs are irrelevant. The essential power is not simply to say no. It is to remain reachable by a reason for saying no.
