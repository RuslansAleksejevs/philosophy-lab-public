# Critique: When Trying a Ritual Becomes a Promise

## Strongest reconstruction

The essay's central claim is best read as a claim about the public constitution of an undertaking. It is not saying that doubt cancels ritual commitments, that religious influence is inherently suspect, or that all obligations require consent. It argues:

1. An obligation attributed to a person's undertaking requires an act that publicly counts as undertaking an identifiable duty.
2. What an act publicly counts as depends partly on the circumstances supplied by the party that solicits it, not merely on its bodily form or usual ritual convention.
3. If an authorized representative invites a newcomer to perform a rite in order to decide whether to undertake duty D, that invitation publicly represents D as still open.
4. Repeating the rite under that representation therefore does not, without a further change in the public circumstances, count as undertaking D.
5. A later change of belief does not alter what the earlier act counted as. Only a later act of undertaking or ratification can do that.

The genuine contribution is the conjunction of two points that are often considered separately: practice can rationally precede belief, and public ritual can bind despite private doubt. The essay identifies a conflict that appears when a community itself authorizes exploratory participation. Its narrow proposal is that the invitation becomes part of the public evidence, and perhaps the public conditions, of the ritual act. Pascal and James supply background for learning through practice. Rappaport supplies the stronger opposing picture of public acceptance. None of them establishes the essay's conclusion.

My verdict is that this is a lucid and potentially significant argument, but the draft does not yet establish its strongest premise. It moves too quickly from “the invitation affects reasonable understanding” to “the invitation prevents an obligation from arising.” It also slides among several different things a ritual might do: count as religious participation, accept a public norm, confer membership, promise future conduct, and create an enforceable claim in particular people. The thesis is most defensible if narrowed to solicited obligations grounded in assurance or agreement, and if it explains when an invitation defeats a ritual convention.

## Argument and premise vulnerabilities

### 1. The object called “commitment” changes

Mira speaks weekly words, yet the leaders infer a “pledge of continuing support,” sometimes described as “lifelong service.” We are never told what the words say, whether the rite is ordinarily initiatory, or whether newcomers know its conventional force. This matters. Participating in a liturgy may accept a norm as a standard for the occasion without promising to remain in the community. Membership may carry exit rules without being lifelong. A vow of one year creates a specific promissory duty. These are different normative relations.

Rappaport's claim is correspondingly more limited and more complicated than the foil suggests. On printed pp. 119–123, he says participation publicly accepts the order and its encoded norms; he does not say that any repeated prayer creates any future duty leaders later name. The draft should specify D in each case and ask whether this rite conventionally undertakes D. Otherwise the imagined leaders look plainly opportunistic, and the hard case disappears.

A stronger test case would make the ritual language itself explicit: for example, Mira is invited to “try the novitiate for a year,” but each weekly rite includes “I bind myself permanently to serve this house.” Everyone knows those words ordinarily function as a vow. Now the invitation and convention genuinely conflict. The essay must explain which governs and why.

### 2. Reasonable misunderstanding does not yet show constitutive failure

The second premise says the soliciting party “is responsible for the meaning it gives the act.” The third says it cannot gain a claim by creating a reasonable misunderstanding. Those propositions strongly support blame, estoppel, waiver, or loss of standing to enforce a duty. They do not automatically prove that no undertaking occurred.

Consider an analogous case. An organizer falsely tells a novice that raising a paddle at an auction is only a rehearsal, while the auctioneer and bidders use the ordinary convention. The organizer may be culpable and may owe compensation. Whether the novice placed a bid depends on who had authority, what the public convention is, what others reasonably took the act to mean, and what remedies the misleading assurance produces. The draft treats the inviter's framing as decisive without stating a rule for resolving these competing public facts.

The distinction between two conclusions should be explicit:

- **No undertaking:** the invitation makes the ritual act infelicitous as a performance of D.
- **No enforceable claim by this community:** D may arise under the convention, but the community cannot fairly demand performance because its authorized assurance induced reliance.

The essay's title and central limit suggest the first, while much of the reasoning establishes the second. The author should choose one or defend the route from the second to the first. The second is narrower but already substantial.

### 3. The account needs conditions for when framing defeats convention

“Authorized invitation” carries nearly all the work but remains underspecified. Authorization can mean power to welcome visitors, apparent authority to explain a rite, or institutional power to alter the rite's status. Those are not equivalent. A priest may be authorized to instruct a seeker but unable, within the tradition, to turn ordination into a trial. Yet the seeker may still reasonably rely on the priest's assurance.

The essay should offer criteria such as: the representative was reasonably presented as competent to explain the rite; the participant did not know that the assurance conflicted with the rite; the undertaking would not have been made absent the assurance; the claimed duty is one whose basis is the participant's voluntary undertaking; and no later, unambiguous act superseded the invitation. It should also say what happens when the words expressly contradict the assurance. “Try it” before “I promise lifelong service” may not leave the latter open if the speaker understands both sentences.

### 4. Counterexamples put pressure on the temporal rule

The claim that belief alone does not “travel backward” is sound. But the draft sometimes speaks as if nothing later can validate an initially defective undertaking. It later allows retrospective acceptance, settlement, and forgiveness, which is correct. The clean principle is: a psychological change alone is not ratification; an informed act can be. State what makes ratification valid and whether it can create duties only prospectively or also accept responsibility for the earlier period.

There is also an accumulation case. Suppose no single rehearsal is a vow, but after months Mira knowingly takes on roles, accepts confidential access reserved for members, tells others to rely on her continued service, and corrects people who call her a visitor. The draft briefly allows conduct to acquire clear meaning, but its phrase “neither repetition ... supplies the missing basis” risks excluding a pattern whose public meaning emerges cumulatively. Replace “repetition” with “mere repetition without a public change in meaning.”

### 5. The strongest objection deserves a less easy opponent

The “surrender” objection is the strongest because some practices may be intelligible only as commitments from the outset. The response says an honest invitation should disclose this. That is right but incomplete. The harder reply is that the community may accurately say: “You cannot try this particular act without doing it; you can only try the surrounding discipline. Saying these words is itself the vow.” If Mira then proceeds while understanding this, her earlier wish to explore does not reserve an exit. The thesis survives only if the actual invitation still presents the vow itself as undecided.

This distinction would also prevent overreach. A trial marriage ceremony is not a marriage merely because the movements match; but someone cannot turn a valid marriage ceremony into rehearsal through a private experimental attitude. The relevant question is whether the parties share, or have reasonable grounds to share, a public description of the act.

## Significance, originality, literature, and source accuracy

**Significance.** The problem matters beyond religion. It concerns recruitment, onboarding, initiations, therapeutic or political communities, and any practice in which participation both teaches a role and appears to accept it. The religious case adds a real complication because practice may produce the belief that later makes the practice seem retrospectively appropriate. The essay should state this wider significance briefly while keeping its conclusion confined to claimed religious undertakings.

**Originality.** The draft is appropriately cautious, but its search is too thin for even a narrow originality judgment. Manzon's 2018 abstract already criticizes Rappaport by requiring personal engagement for successful ritual enactment. More directly, the open 2026 article “Off the hook? Toward an improved account of liturgical participation” asks how conventions and intentions determine ritual significance, treats faithless but open seekers, and discusses covert religious framing. That article does not obviously make the draft's exact diachronic claim about an inviter's assurance and later belief. The likely contribution is therefore not “context matters to ritual meaning,” but a narrower anti-retroactivity and inviter-responsibility argument about obligations. The final essay should cite and distinguish this article, and avoid claiming priority without a broader search.

**Engagement with literature.** Pascal and James motivate practice before conviction but do little argumentative work after the opening. Rappaport is the only sustained interlocutor. The essay would be stronger if it connected its key premise to speech-act felicity conditions and to one theory of promissory obligation. A conventional account, an assurance account, and an estoppel-like account yield different verdicts in Mira's case. The draft need not survey all theories, but it should say which kind of reason grounds the community's alleged claim. It should also engage the 2026 ritual-participation discussion because it is unusually close and current.

**Source accuracy.** I verified the cited primary passages. Pascal fragment 233 does advise practice as a route to belief. James §§IX–X supports both self-fulfilling trust and the cost of skeptical delay. Rappaport printed pp. 119–123 distinguishes inward belief from public acceptance and treats liturgical performance as establishing obligation; printed pp. 446–447 explicitly denies binding force to coerced acceptance. The summaries are accurate. The main issue is interpretive scope: Rappaport's acceptance of a public order should not be equated without argument with a promise of indefinite membership or service.

**What I could not establish.** I could access only Manzon's abstract, not the full article. I did not perform a systematic literature review or read Austin and Searle in full. I used public reference summaries to locate the speech-act issue. My retrieval record is in `sources/critic/retrieval-notes.md`.

## Language review

### Opening

The opening begins: “A person can join a prayer before she knows whether anyone hears it. She may hope that praying with others will help her understand what their faith means.” This is immediately understandable. It introduces the person, action, and uncertainty without jargon. The next move—“After several months she wants to stop. The community answers that she has already pledged herself”—also makes the conflict plain. Keep it.

One small improvement would make the disputed duty concrete. Replace “she has already pledged herself” with: “The community answers that, by repeating the rite, she promised to remain and serve it.” The present wording postpones what the alleged pledge requires.

### Four passages needing revision

1. **Central thesis:** “When a community authorizes participation as an attempt to discover whether to commit, it cannot treat that same participation as sufficient grounds for the commitment under investigation.” The sentence is precise but has an abstract chain—“participation,” “attempt,” “grounds,” “commitment under investigation”—at the point where the reader most needs a simple thesis.

   Simpler rewrite: “If a community invites someone to take part so that she can decide whether to commit, it cannot later say that taking part was itself the commitment. It needs some further reason to show that she said yes.”

2. **Core premise:** “An invitation can qualify a convention; at the least, it can defeat the inviter's claim that the convention alone makes the participant's meaning clear.” “Qualify a convention” and “defeat the inviter's claim” are compressed legal-philosophical phrases. The two possible conclusions are also hidden.

   Simpler rewrite: “A community's explanation can change how its usual ritual should be understood in this case. Even if it does not change the ritual, the community can no longer point to custom alone and say the newcomer's meaning was clear.”

3. **Historical argument:** “This distinction prevents a troubling argument: the community helped produce a loyal believer; therefore it was entitled to treat her as bound while doing so.” This is already clear. The optional improvement is to name the invalid step more directly.

   Simpler rewrite: “The fact that the practice later produced belief does not show that the community had a right to treat Mira as committed before she believed. Success at changing her mind cannot prove that she had already promised.”

4. **Strongest objection:** “A person who reserves judgment about every act cannot experience wholehearted devotion. Insisting on an open exit may therefore destroy the very opportunity she seeks.” The thought is clear, but “reserves judgment about every act” suggests constant inner monitoring, which the essay does not require. It risks making the objection easier to answer than it should be.

   Simpler rewrite: “Some forms of worship may work only when a person commits herself fully at the start. If she treats the central act as a trial she can undo, she may never experience the form of devotion she hoped to understand.”

5. **Concluding formulation:** “A later yes can begin something profound. It cannot, merely by arriving, prove that an earlier yes was given.” This is memorable, but “a later yes” is ambiguous because the essay's example says there was no earlier yes. It also blurs belief, promise, and ratification.

   Simpler rewrite: “Mira's later belief may lead her to make a real commitment. Her belief alone does not show that she made that commitment earlier.”

6. **Dense conceptual sentence:** “The problem is not that an outlook has causes. It is the substitution of a caused outlook for an undertaking that has not been shown to occur.” “Caused outlook” sounds mechanical, and “substitution” asks the reader to unpack the argument.

   Simpler rewrite: “There is nothing wrong with beliefs being shaped by practice and other people. The mistake is to treat Mira's new belief as proof that she previously made a promise.”

Across the manuscript, “commitment,” “acceptance,” “undertaking,” “duty,” and “obligation” sometimes appear interchangeable. They should either be defined or replaced with the exact act at issue: accepting a norm, becoming a member, promising a year of help, or promising lifelong service. This is the most important language revision because it is also the most important argumentative revision.

## Prioritized revision plan

1. Specify the exact duty D and rebuild Mira as a genuinely hard case in which explicit ritual convention conflicts with an authorized experimental invitation.
2. Decide whether the conclusion is that no undertaking occurs or that the misleading community lacks standing to enforce it. Defend the relevant bridge principle.
3. Give conditions under which an authorized explanation defeats, modifies, or merely creates an excuse against a ritual convention; address explicit contradictory words and cumulative conduct.
4. Narrow the anti-retroactivity claim: later belief alone is insufficient, while informed ratification or later public conduct may create or assume obligations.
5. Engage the recent “Off the hook?” article and a suitable speech-act or promising framework; distinguish ritual significance from promissory obligation.
6. Apply the language rewrites throughout by replacing generic normative nouns with the concrete promise, person, act, and duration at issue.
