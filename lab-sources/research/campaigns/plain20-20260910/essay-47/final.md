# When Trying a Ritual Becomes a Promise

A person can join a prayer before she knows whether anyone hears it. She may hope that praying with others will help her understand their faith. Suppose a community welcomes her on those terms. After a year she wants to stop. The community answers that, by repeating the rite, she promised to remain and serve it for life.

Her lack of belief does not settle the dispute. People can make promises while doubting the ideas that give those promises their point. Yet her spoken words do not settle it either. The community helped establish what those words meant when it invited her to take part.

I defend a limit on the claim this community may make. If it invites someone to perform a rite so that she can decide whether to promise lifelong service, it cannot later rely on that performance alone as proof that she made the promise. It needs a further reason to show that she said yes. The invitation may prevent the promise from being made at all. Even where the rite has an institutional force that the inviter lacked power to change, the community may lose its right to demand performance from the person it misled. Which result follows depends on the rite and the inviter's authority. The practical limit is the same: the community cannot gain an enforceable claim from the conflict it created.

This thesis concerns duties said to arise from a person's voluntary act. It does not decide whether God exists, whether a sacrament is valid, or whether some duties bind everyone without consent. Its question is narrower: when may a community fairly say, “You promised this to us, so you owe it”?

## Learning by doing

The thought that practice can lead to faith has a familiar source. In fragment 233 of the *Pensées*, Pascal advises a person unable to believe to begin with practices such as holy water and masses. Acting like believers is presented as a route toward becoming one. The advice addresses a real difficulty: someone cannot simply decide that a disputed claim now seems true. She can decide what to do next. [Pascal, fragment 233](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/pascal/pensees.all.html).

There need be nothing dishonest about that decision. A visitor can sincerely hope for an answer while remaining uncertain that there is anyone to answer. She can attend carefully to a ceremony without pretending that she already understands it. She can also know that repeated participation may change her feelings. Wanting to change is not automatically being deceived.

Nor is advance certainty a reasonable condition for every serious undertaking. James argues that trust can help bring about the relationship whose possibility one is trying to assess. Waiting for proof can sometimes prevent the good from arising. His discussion of religion extends the challenge: suspension can carry risks of its own. One need not accept his whole defense of faith to see the point. [James, “The Will to Believe,” §§IX–X](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659-images.html).

An invitation to participate may therefore be a serious offer of understanding. Some things become clear only through sustained attention and shared work. A person learning to sing cannot first hear exactly what it is like to sing well. Likewise, the meaning of communal prayer may emerge through learning when to speak, how to listen, and whom to remember.

But agreeing to rehearse for a month does not amount to agreeing to sing for the group every year. The rehearsal may change my taste, friendships, and plans. Permission to help shape my outlook and authority to demand my future conduct remain different matters.

Religious practice brings them especially close. The same words may help a newcomer explore a life while established members use them to renew their allegiance. Recent work on liturgical participation likewise stresses that words, intentions, conventions, and context can pull in different directions. “Off the hook?” discusses seekers and covert framing, but asks mainly what makes participation religiously successful. My question concerns a narrower result: a claimed promissory duty owed to the community, especially after the practice changes the seeker's belief. [“Off the hook?”, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11153-026-10018-4).

## A hard case for public commitment

Rappaport gives a strong reason to resist making ritual depend on inward certainty. He distinguishes private belief from public acceptance. Ritual participation can establish obligations even when the performer privately doubts or rejects what the ritual expresses. A public order cannot rest on everyone's continuously sharing the same convictions. He also denies that coerced acceptance creates a moral obligation. [Rappaport, pp. 119–123, 446–447](https://isnuponorogo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Ritual_and_Religion_in_the_Making_of_Humanity__Cambridge_Studies_in_Social_and_Cultural_Anthropology_.pdf).

The distinction between belief and acceptance is right. Suppose Daniel understands a vow to support a religious house for one year. He freely takes it because he wants to give its way of life a fair chance. He doubts its teaching, but promises the year of service. His doubt does not cancel the promise. If others arrange their work around it, he cannot fairly reply that they should have inspected his soul.

Now consider a harder version of Mira's case. A leader whom the community presents as responsible for newcomers tells her: “Try the novitiate for a year. At the end you can decide whether to remain and serve for life.” Each week, however, novices join a rite containing the words, “I bind myself permanently to serve this house.” Established members treat those words as a lifelong vow. Mira is told that, for someone in her trial year, speaking them is part of learning the practice and does not settle the later choice. She reasonably relies on that explanation. At year's end she leaves. The house points to the words and demands lifelong service.

This is not a conflict between public words and a secret reservation. The invitation, the representative's role, the customary words, and the later demand are all public facts. Nor can we assume that the leader had power to alter the rite. Perhaps the tradition holds that no officer can turn this vow into a rehearsal. The question is what claim the house has against a novice whom its own representative induced to speak on contrary terms.

Rappaport's account does not by itself answer that question. Acceptance of an order is not automatically a promise of every future duty that leaders might name. We must identify the alleged act and duty: here, promising lifelong service to the house. We must then ask both whether the rite performed that act and whether this house may enforce the result against Mira.

## Two ways an invitation can matter

Words perform acts only under suitable conditions. The same signature can authorize payment, acknowledge delivery, or provide a handwriting sample. Its shape does not choose among these acts. The parties' roles, shared conventions, and explanation of the occasion help determine what is done. This familiar point from work on speech acts applies to ritual words as well. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Pragmatics,” §2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatics/).

An invitation can matter in two ways. First, it may become part of the conditions that determine which act occurred. If the people with authority over the rite jointly present the novice's performance as rehearsal, then she may not make the institutional vow at all. The movements and words match a vow, but the accepted setting does not. This resembles actors speaking a wedding formula on stage.

Second, the invitation may leave the institutional act intact but block the community's claim against this participant. Suppose the welcoming leader had apparent authority to explain the rite but no actual power to alter it. The tradition may count Mira's words as a vow. Still, a duty grounded in assurance or voluntary undertaking cannot fairly be enforced by the party whose authorized representative caused her reasonable mistake about what she was undertaking. The house asked her to rely on its explanation, obtained her participation through that reliance, and now seeks an advantage from the contradiction. At minimum, it must release her from the demand it induced her not to choose.

Theories of promising explain such duties in different ways. Some emphasize convention, some the assurance given to another person, and some the other's reliance. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Promises,” §§5.3–6](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2011/entries/promises/). Mira's case need not decide among them. If convention controls the institutional status of the vow, the misleading assurance still undermines the house's standing to enforce it. If assurance or justified reliance helps constitute the promise, the conflict may prevent the promise itself. The draft's mistake would be to force both routes into one conclusion.

Several conditions limit this protection. The representative must reasonably appear competent to explain the rite. Mira must not understand that the explanation is false. The promise must be one whose basis is her voluntary undertaking, rather than a duty claimed to bind everyone. She must rely on the explanation in participating. And no later clear act may replace it.

These conditions also show when an exploratory wish does not help. A friend in the congregation cannot privately rewrite the rite for Mira. Nor can Mira turn a vow into a trial by silently hoping to reconsider it. If the community accurately tells her, “You may try the surrounding discipline, but these particular words are the lifelong vow; you cannot try saying them without making it,” and she understands and speaks them, her uncertainty about belief does not reserve an exit. The hard case exists only when the community presents the very promise later claimed as still undecided.

## Later belief and later action

Suppose the practice eventually convinces Mira. She now believes the teachings and wants to stay. Does that show that the house's earlier demand was justified?

No. Her new belief answers a question about her present outlook. It does not answer the historical question of what she promised during the trial year. The fact that the practice produced belief does not show that the community had a right to treat her as committed beforehand. Success at changing her mind cannot prove that she had already promised.

There is nothing wrong with beliefs being shaped by practice and other people. Religious education, family life, and friendship all influence what seems important. The mistake is to treat Mira's new belief as proof that she previously made a promise. Becoming convinced, promising service, and forgiving a misleading invitation are different acts.

Later action can matter. With adequate understanding, Mira may now take the lifelong vow. She may explicitly accept responsibility for part of the trial year, settle the dispute, or forgive the house. A pattern of conduct can also become clear enough to count: she might knowingly accept a role reserved for permanent members, ask others to rely on her continuing service, and correct people who call her a visitor. Mere repetition of the original rite, under the original explanation, adds no new basis. Conduct whose public meaning has changed can.

Ratification therefore requires more than a psychological change. Mira must understand the disputed act and freely do something that adopts it. What that act reaches also needs interpretation. A new promise normally creates duties from now on. An explicit settlement might assume responsibility for an earlier period. Belief alone does neither.

## Objections

The first objection is that this gives participants an easy escape. Anyone could enjoy a community's benefits and later call the experience a trial.

The reply is that a private description is insufficient. Mira needs a public basis for her understanding: an explanation from someone reasonably presented as responsible, her reliance on it, and no later clear correction. Daniel cannot escape his understood one-year promise by later calling it research. The difference is that the community itself left Mira's lifelong promise open.

A second objection appeals to other members' reliance. They taught Mira, shared confidential matters, and counted on her continued presence. Surely she owes them something.

She may. She can owe notice about work she agreed to do, care with confidences, return of property, or compensation for reliance she knowingly invited. Those duties must be identified and defended. They do not prove the different claim that she promised lifelong service. Teaching offered while someone is deciding does not acquire a hidden price after she decides to leave.

A third objection says that some forms of worship work only when a person commits fully at the start. If she treats the central act as a trial she can undo, she may never experience the devotion she hoped to understand.

That may be true. A community need not offer every rite experimentally. It can say that a seeker may observe or try the surrounding discipline but that a particular vow binds whoever knowingly takes it. Some seekers will then decline. The community may lose their participation, but this is not a reason to promise them a later choice that the rite denies.

A fourth objection says that the sacred rite has a meaning no officer can modify. This is the strongest case for separating institutional validity from an enforceable claim. The tradition may hold that the vow occurred. My argument does not settle its supernatural or internal status. It says that the house cannot cite its own misleading invitation as a route to a moral demand against Mira. Claims about what God has done and claims about what a visitor voluntarily promised to other people require different support.

Finally, one may object that this is ordinary consent applied to religion. Much of the argument does use ordinary ideas about promises. The special problem comes from combining two truths: practice may properly precede conviction, and public commitment may survive doubt. Without attention to the invitation, these truths can make exploratory action look binding before the person decides. The further danger is temporal. If practice succeeds in producing faith, that success can make the original conflict disappear from view.

## Making room for serious beginnings

A community that welcomes doubters needs an intelligible account of what their participation does. It can distinguish observing, joining a limited practice, and taking an enduring vow. It can allow the same words to serve different roles where those roles are publicly understood. An exploratory period can still demand discipline, inconvenience, and duties freely accepted for its duration.

The conclusion is limited but demanding. When a community invites someone to use a rite to decide whether to promise lifelong service, it cannot rely on that performance alone to demand the service. If its representatives can set the rite's force, no vow may occur. If they cannot, their assurance may still deprive the community of a fair claim against the person they misled. Clear later conduct can create or ratify duties; mere repetition and later belief cannot.

The community must therefore honor the kind of beginning it offers. Mira's later belief may lead her to make a real promise. Her belief alone does not show that she made that promise earlier.
