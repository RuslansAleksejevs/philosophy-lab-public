## Substantive changes

I accepted the critic's central objection. The draft did not adequately distinguish the claim that no vow occurred from the claim that a community may not enforce a vow after misleading the participant. The final now gives both routes. A representative with power over the rite may make the performance a rehearsal; a representative with only apparent explanatory authority may leave its institutional force intact while defeating the community's moral claim against the person who relied on the explanation.

I made Mira's alleged duty exact: lifelong service to a religious house. The rite now expressly says, “I bind myself permanently to serve this house,” while the authorized invitation says the trial-year participant can decide later. This creates a real conflict between convention and framing instead of letting the leaders invent an unspecified duty after the fact.

I added limiting conditions: apparent competence to explain the rite, reasonable ignorance of the conflict, reliance, a duty grounded in voluntary undertaking, and no later unambiguous superseding act. I also accepted the critic's hard counterexample. An exploratory private attitude cannot undo a vow when the community clearly explains that the central act itself cannot be tried without being performed.

I narrowed the temporal claim. Mere repetition under unchanged terms and later belief are insufficient, but informed ratification or a cumulative pattern with a new public meaning may create or assume duties. The final distinguishes prospective promises from explicit settlements concerning an earlier period.

I clarified Rappaport's scope, separating acceptance of an order from a promise of indefinite service. I added the closely related 2026 “Off the hook?” article and distinguished its concern with successful liturgical participation from this essay's narrower concern with promissory claims after an exploratory invitation. I also added limited speech-act and promise-theory framing based on the critic's recorded sources. I do not claim priority or a systematic literature review.

I rejected only the suggestion that the essay must choose a single general theory of promises. The argument is stronger as a conditional result across plausible theories: convention may preserve institutional validity, while assurance and reliance still bear on the community's standing. The final states this modestly rather than claiming that all theories give the same account.

## Language changes

I kept the concrete opening but named the alleged promise immediately. Throughout the essay I replaced vague uses of “commitment,” “acceptance,” and “obligation” with the exact act or duty: joining a rite, accepting an order, promising one year, or promising lifelong service. The central thesis now says directly what the community invited Mira to do and what it later demanded.

I separated dense sentences that combined institutional force, reasonable understanding, and enforceability. The sections on later belief and wholehearted devotion now use the critic's simpler formulations in substance. I replaced the ambiguous closing image of “a later yes” with a direct distinction between later belief and an earlier promise. I reviewed the remaining essay for the same problems, especially the transition from ritual meaning to a claim held by particular people.

## Remaining limitations

The argument does not settle sacramental validity, duties independent of voluntary undertaking, or every conflict between actual and apparent institutional authority. Its literature search remains limited. The Manzon article was available only by abstract, the recent liturgy article was read only through the critic's recorded public retrieval, and Austin and Searle were not read in full. The account gives conditions for the focal case rather than a complete theory of promising or ritual action.
