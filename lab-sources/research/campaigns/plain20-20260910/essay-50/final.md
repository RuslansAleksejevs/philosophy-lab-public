# When Thinking Again Is Not Enough

You decide to rent a flat. Before signing, you spend an evening checking what could go wrong. You picture the noise from the street, the awkward kitchen, and the long walk from the station in the rain. By the end of the evening, another flat seems better. You have thought harder about the choice. Does that mean your new judgment is better?

Perhaps. You may have found a serious fault. You may also have understood an old fact in a better way. But suppose you learned no new fact, corrected no mistake, and gained no better sense of how much any concern matters. You simply made one flat's drawbacks vivid while leaving the other flat in the distance. Your doubt is real. It does not follow that it is a better guide than the judgment it replaced.

This essay defends a narrow rule about changing plans. The rule concerns an overall judgment that one option is better than another, given concerns the person still accepts. Suppose a focused review changes that judgment. If the person knows that the review gave unequal attention to the options, and has no reason to think this unequal attention tracked what matters, the change itself is not evidence that the new judgment is better. The known mismatch defeats any extra credit the judgment might seem to earn from coming after more thought.

This rule does not prove that the earlier judgment was right. Nor does it cover every result of a review. A check may reveal a decisive fault, show that an option falls below an acceptable threshold, or support action under risk without ranking all options. The rule applies when a person treats the wider feeling caused by a narrow check as a better overall comparison.

The point matters because careful people have a route to unstable choice. They investigate the option they selected, discover how unattractive any real option can look under close scrutiny, and flee to an option still protected by distance. More care can then produce another reversal. They did not necessarily ask a bad question. They let the answer to one question settle another.

## Two questions inside a review

Mara must choose between two flats, Cedar and Birch. She has compared their costs, travel times, space, and noise. Neither is better in every respect. She chooses Cedar. No deposit has been paid, and nobody else relies on her decision. We can therefore set aside promises and the cost of switching.

Before signing, Mara asks what would be difficult about living at Cedar. This is a reasonable precaution. She imagines carrying groceries home on wet evenings. She studies the kitchen. She thinks through a day when the street is especially noisy.

The check can have at least three results. First, Mara might discover that a fixed appointment makes Cedar's journey impossible. Second, she might decide that Cedar is not acceptable even without judging Birch better overall. Third, she might learn nothing of either kind but come to judge Birch better because Cedar's familiar costs now feel more pressing.

The first result is a discovery. The second uses a threshold: Cedar fails a condition that any chosen flat must meet. Either can rationally change what Mara does. A safety engineer may inspect only the selected design because that design alone still needs approval. If it fails the test, using another design can be rational even without a full new ranking.

The third result is different. Mara now compares Cedar as seen through a focused search for burdens with Birch as seen through her earlier balanced review. The check was suited to the local question, “Does Cedar have a problem?” It was not, just by being a good check, suited to the broader question, “Which flat is better overall?”

A one-sided inquiry is therefore not necessarily a bad inquiry. If the roof may leak, examine the roof. It would be absurd to spend equal time admiring the windows. The mistake is to say: “I was right to check Cedar for problems. That check made Birch feel better. So I must now have good reason to judge Birch better overall.” The first claim does not establish the last.

## What exactly is being judged?

A review can change several things. It can change a passing feeling, a desire, a belief about an option, or an overall comparison. These changes should not be treated as one event.

My rule is about an all-things-considered comparative judgment. This is Mara's view about which flat better serves the concerns she continues to endorse: cost, travel, space, noise, and anything else she takes to matter. Such a judgment can be more or less well supported by the way she considered those concerns.

A passing loss of attraction is not yet that judgment. A newly created desire raises a different issue. Repeatedly imagining Cedar's commute might make Mara hate the thought of it. The new aversion may itself affect what living there would be like. In that case, she faces two questions. Did the exercise reveal Cedar's importance under her existing concerns? And did it change her in a way that creates a new practical reason? My argument answers only the first.

This limit is important. A lasting preference is not automatically authoritative. Advertising, prejudice, or compulsive rehearsal can create lasting preferences too. Yet persistence may matter to what Mara will experience in the flat. Deciding which created preferences should guide action requires a wider account of agency and well-being. The rule defended here leaves that question open.

## What earlier work explains

The idea that rational agency sometimes requires resistance to changing one's mind is familiar. Holton connects weakness of will with revising intentions too readily. He does not treat all revision as failure. He asks whether the particular reconsideration shows reasonable tendencies and includes the conditions for clear thought among the relevant factors (Holton, 1999, author manuscript pp. 5–7). His account already makes the process of reconsideration central.

Holton also discusses real changes in valuation and the way desire directs attention. He defends habits that prevent repeated reconsideration while preserving our ability to respond to change (Holton, “Inverse Akrasia and Weakness of Will,” manuscript pp. 5–9). This discussion helps show why a change in desire cannot simply be treated as a faulty belief.

Nor must deliberation add a new fact to be useful. Hoek and Bradley show how a question can improve thought by bringing existing information into focus. They also show how questions can steer choice by directing attention. Attention can improve a judgment or mislead it; the absence of new information settles neither possibility (Hoek and Bradley, 2024, introduction and discussion of collective decision making).

These accounts leave room for a more exact distinction. A person may have good reason to review a plan but poor reason to accept the new overall ranking that the review happens to produce. The useful review can create a mismatch between its local purpose and the wider use of its effects.

The proposed result is best understood as a precise extension of process-sensitive views, not as a new general theory of intention. A limited search does not establish that no one has stated the same rule. Its value must rest on the distinction and the argument, not on an unsupported claim of novelty.

## A defeater for the later judgment

Three steps support the rule.

First, how a judgment was reached can affect whether we should trust it. If I compare the annual cost of one flat with the monthly cost of another, my confidence does not repair the comparison. Evidence about the procedure can weaken my reason to rely on its output. This point does not assume that every practical choice has one exact numerical answer.

Second, a focused review can alter salience without improving the overall comparison. Salience is simply how strongly a consideration occupies attention. Imagine that Mara notices a pattern: she prefers whichever flat she has not just spent an hour criticizing. She still accepts the same facts and concerns. The pattern gives her a specific reason to think that the latest shift records what is vivid rather than what matters more.

Third, a judgment is not better merely because it came later. A later judgment deserves more trust when it uses better information, corrects an inference, or gives the agent better understanding. Its date alone adds nothing.

Together these steps yield a defeater rule:

> When a focused review causes a new overall ranking, known unequal attention defeats treating the change itself as evidence of improvement, unless the agent has reason to think the focus tracked the importance of the concerns or corrected a limitation in the earlier comparison.

This is a defeater, not a demand that Mara prove her later judgment from the ground up. A considered judgment normally has some standing. But once Mara has specific evidence that the local task mismatched the global ranking, she cannot use “I thought more and then changed my mind” as an answer to that concern.

The rule is also symmetric. Suppose Mara first chose Cedar after imagining its pleasures in detail while barely considering Birch. Her earlier choice then faces the same problem. The rule gives it no protection merely because it became a plan. If both comparisons are flawed, suspension may be the honest result.

## How a focused review can answer the defeater

Unequal attention can be the right way to learn. Mara may know Cedar's commute time yet never have considered its lived shape. She may be uncertain about this feature while understanding Birch well. Concentrating on Cedar can then improve her acquaintance with the relevant burden.

Mara need not give a polished explanation before she may revise. Practical understanding can be tacit. Still, there may be evidence that her attention tracked importance. The imagined commute may connect with similar journeys she has actually made. Her reaction may remain after she places both flats under a modestly matched prompt. She may notice that her first comparison pictured travel only after easy days although most days will be tiring. Or she may find that the same concern produces a similar response when she considers Birch.

None of these signs proves that the later judgment is correct. They show how it can answer the specific worry raised by the one-sided process. By contrast, a preference that flips whenever Mara changes the target of criticism leaves the worry in place.

Repair therefore need not mean equal minutes for each option. Some risks deserve more attention, and some options are harder to understand. Mara needs a comparison shaped by the importance and uncertainty of the concerns, rather than by the accidental order in which she pictured them.

One repair is to examine Birch through the same relevant concerns that guided the check of Cedar. Another is to place Cedar's burdens back beside its benefits. A third is to ask what changed: a fact, an inference, an understanding of a burden, an endorsed concern, or only a feeling of vividness. The answer helps identify which question the review actually settled.

Sometimes Mara cannot reconstruct the earlier comparison before a deadline. Then the justification of her ranking may remain unsettled even though she must act. She might choose Cedar because the original comparison was adequate and the later doubt has a known source. She might choose Birch because the harm of being wrong is lower. She might pay to preserve an option or postpone the decision. Risk, deadlines, and the value of keeping options open can justify an act without making one flat better overall.

## Objections

The first objection is that the rule asks Mara to act against her current best judgment. Once she judges Birch better, how could choosing Cedar be rational?

Mara's immediate impression may favor Birch. But she also knows how that impression was produced. After she includes this fact, her considered view may be that the ranking is unsettled or that the earlier comparison remains more reliable. She is not required to act against a settled judgment. The point is that the first ranking produced during reconsideration need not be her fully considered judgment.

If Mara addresses the process concern and still reasonably judges Birch better, this argument gives her no reason to stay with Cedar. A plan does not become immune to defeat merely because it is already a plan.

A second objection says that all practical thought directs attention. If every result requires another inquiry into its source, no decision can end.

The rule does not require general suspicion. It applies when the person has specific evidence of a mismatch: for example, she knows that she searched only for Cedar's faults and notices that her preference follows the latest target of criticism. A brief check can answer the concern, and the importance of the choice limits how much further inquiry is reasonable. The bare possibility of bias triggers no endless review.

A third objection comes from the safety engineer. Inspecting only the chosen design is rational. If the inspection makes the engineer unwilling to use it, why demand a balanced comparison?

No balanced comparison is required. The design may reveal a decisive defect or fail a safety threshold. Either result can settle what to do. The rule concerns a different inference: moving from the discomfort caused by a local inspection to a claim that the other design is better overall. Distinguishing a veto from a ranking explains why the same one-sided procedure can be fit for one conclusion and unfit for another.

A fourth objection returns to a desire created by thought. Suppose advertising makes Birch feel like home, and the effect lasts. It may now shape Mara's future experience. Does its suspect origin make it irrelevant?

The rule alone cannot answer. It can show that the feeling is not, by itself, evidence that Birch better served Mara's earlier concerns. Whether the new desire should count as a concern is another question. Its persistence is relevant to future experience but is neither necessary nor sufficient for authority. This boundary is a limitation of the account, not a problem that a simple test can remove.

Finally, the rule may sound like familiar advice to avoid bias. Its sharper claim concerns the use of a justified inquiry. A local defect check may be exactly what a rational agent should perform. Even so, the warrant for asking that local question does not automatically support an overall ranking caused by the check. The defect lies in the move between two questions, not simply in being careless or unfair.

## Doubt without surrender

A plan should help us act while remaining answerable to reasons. That requires more than deciding when to reopen it. We must also decide what the results of a review establish.

A person can responsibly ask what is wrong with a plan, answer that question well, and then mistake the resulting loss of attraction for a better overall comparison. Known unequal attention gives that person a reason to pause. It does not prove that the old plan was right. It blocks one tempting argument for thinking the new ranking is better.

Rational persistence in such a case need not involve refusing to think. It can consist in thinking accurately about what one's additional thought has and has not established. A doubt deserves attention without always settling what to do next.
