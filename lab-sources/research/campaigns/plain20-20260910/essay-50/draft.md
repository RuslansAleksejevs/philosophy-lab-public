# When Thinking Again Is Not Enough

You decide to rent a flat. Before signing, you spend an evening checking what could go wrong. You picture the noise from the street, the awkward kitchen, and the long walk from the station in the rain. By the end of the evening, another flat seems better. You have thought harder about your choice. Have you therefore improved it?

Perhaps. You may have found a serious fault. But suppose you learned no new facts, uncovered no missed consequence, and identified no mistake in your earlier comparison. You spent the evening making one flat's familiar drawbacks vivid. You did not do the same for the other flat. Your doubt is real. It does not follow that it is a better guide than the judgment it displaced.

This essay defends a limited claim about changing plans. A good reason to examine a plan does not automatically give us a good reason to trust every comparative judgment that the examination produces. When we know that a change of mind came from a one-sided task, and cannot identify a relevant improvement in our grounds, we should not give the later judgment priority merely because it resulted from more thought. We should first correct the comparison, if doing so is worth the cost. Sometimes that means keeping the plan for now. Sometimes it means suspending both judgments.

The point matters because careful people have a distinctive route to unstable choice. They investigate the option they have selected, discover how unattractive any real option can look under concentrated scrutiny, and flee to an option still protected by distance. More care can then produce another reversal. The problem is not simply that they think too much. They ask a sensible question and then let its answer settle a different question.

## Two questions inside a review

Consider Mara, who must choose between two flats, Cedar and Birch. She has compared their costs, travel times, space, and noise. Neither dominates the other: each has an advantage that matters to her. She chooses Cedar. No deposit has been paid and nobody else is relying on her decision. We can therefore set aside switching costs and promises.

Before signing, Mara asks: what would be difficult about living at Cedar? This is a reasonable precaution. She needs to make sure the choice is workable. She imagines carrying groceries home on wet evenings. She studies the kitchen layout. She thinks through a day when the street is especially noisy.

There are at least two possible results. Mara might notice that a fixed appointment would make the journey impossible. Or she might simply feel much more strongly about a journey she had already allowed for. These results need different treatment.

In the first case, her inquiry answers the question it was meant to answer. It identifies a problem in the plan. Depending on its importance, the problem may also settle the comparison with Birch. In the second case, the inquiry has changed how the options strike her without yet showing why that change deserves authority. Cedar is now pictured through its burdens. Birch remains pictured through the balanced description Mara used earlier. Her present comparison combines the outputs of two different kinds of examination.

A one-sided inquiry is not necessarily a bad inquiry. If the roof may leak, examine the roof. It would be absurd to require an equal amount of time admiring the windows. But a useful inquiry into one option's defects need not produce a reliable ranking of all the options. This distinction allows us to criticize Mara's inference without criticizing her decision to inspect Cedar.

The error would be to reason: “Checking Cedar was sensible; checking made me prefer Birch; so the preference for Birch inherits the justification of the check.” The conclusion does not follow. Permission to run a procedure is not a guarantee that every effect of running it is a warranted answer to every question.

## What existing accounts already explain

The idea that rational agency requires some resistance to changing one's mind is familiar. Holton connects weakness of will with revising intentions too readily. Crucially, his account does not treat all changes as failures. He asks whether the particular reconsideration displays reasonable tendencies, and includes the quality of the circumstances for thought among the relevant factors. He also distinguishes justified reassessment from a failure of nerve that would occur whether a risky plan was sound or unsound. The quality of a process, rather than change alone, is already central to his account (Holton, 1999, author manuscript pp. 5–7).

Nor is the influence of attention a new discovery. In a separate discussion, Holton considers how desire directs attention and can alter valuation. He defends the role of habits that prevent repeated reconsideration while leaving us able to respond to changes. His defense distinguishes not reopening a decision from retaining it after reconsideration when one's reasons oppose it (Holton, “Inverse Akrasia and Weakness of Will,” manuscript pp. 6, 8–9).

There is also a strong reason not to make new information the price of rational revision. Hoek and Bradley show how a question can improve deliberation by bringing existing information into focus. Their discussion also shows how directing attention can steer another person's choice. The same general route can help or mislead: its value cannot be read off the fact that no new information arrived (Hoek and Bradley, 2024, introduction and discussion of collective decision making).

The present proposal adds a narrower point at the boundary between review and revision. Even when beginning a review was sensible, accepting its resulting ranking may require a further justification. The relevant defect need not be temptation, poor conditions for thinking, or an unreasonable habit of reopening decisions. It can arise from carrying out an appropriate, focused check very well. Its local success is precisely what creates a lopsided basis for a broader judgment.

This is an extension of process-sensitive approaches to intention, not a rival general theory of intention. Its proposed contribution is to distinguish the standards for asking a question from the standards for using its psychological effects to answer another one.

## Why later need not mean better

Three premises support the claim.

First, a judgment's authority depends partly on how it was reached. If I know that I compared the annual cost of one flat with the monthly cost of another, my confidence does not make the comparison sound. Evidence about the procedure can weaken my entitlement to rely on its output. This is compatible with many views about value. It does not assume that there is one exact numerical answer to every practical question.

Second, thinking can change a judgment without removing a defect in its grounds. This is a claim about possibility, not an estimate of how often it happens. Imagine Mara notices that she reliably prefers whichever flat she has not just spent an hour criticizing. She still accepts her original factual estimates and priorities. No overlooked difficulty emerges. She has good reason to suspect that the shift records what is currently vivid rather than a better comparison.

Third, later judgments have no automatic priority that defeats such evidence. Being later may correlate with having more information, understanding consequences better, or having more experience. Those are reasons to defer to a later judgment. Mere temporal order is not another reason of equal strength. If the very process connecting the two judgments explains the change without explaining an improvement, “I have now reconsidered” cannot close the case.

Together these premises give a conditional conclusion. Where the agent has credible evidence that a shift arose from a mismatched comparison, and no identified improvement that answers this concern, she should not treat the shift alone as a sufficient reason to abandon the plan. The word “alone” matters. A modest doubt may justify another check. It may combine with a deadline or a high risk of harm to justify a precaution. What it cannot do is certify itself as the wiser judgment simply by being the product of additional deliberation.

Notice the symmetry. Suppose Mara initially selected Cedar after imagining its pleasures in great detail while barely considering Birch. Her original choice then has the same defect. The proposal gives it no special protection. If neither comparison is trustworthy, she should be uncertain. The earlier plan earns provisional authority only when there is some reason to regard its basis as better, or when acting on it is justified despite unresolved uncertainty. Priority belongs to a better-supported procedure, not to the earlier self.

## How to repair the comparison

The repair is not to give every option an equal number of minutes. Some options are more complex. Some risks deserve more attention. An equal division of time could preserve a serious imbalance.

Mara needs to return to the question the decision actually requires: how would each flat serve the concerns that matter here? One repair is to examine Birch's difficulties using the same relevant concerns that guided her examination of Cedar. Another is to place Cedar's difficulties back beside its benefits, keeping their estimated importance visible. A third is to ask exactly what the check changed: a fact, an inference, an estimate of importance, or only the ease with which a disadvantage comes to mind.

These are fallible ways to improve a comparison. They do not offer a mechanical test for correct value. In particular, Mara cannot determine whether she learned something simply by checking whether she can state a new proposition. She might finally understand how exhausting a familiar commute would be. That improvement in understanding can justify revision even if her travel-time estimate is unchanged. But she should be able to explain why this is a better appreciation of the burden, rather than assume that stronger feeling proves it.

An explanation need not be lengthy. She might realize that she had imagined travelling only after an easy day, although most days will be tiring. That gives a reason to prefer the new assessment. Or she might recognize that she would regard the same journey as a burden at Birch but had excused it at Cedar. That gives another. The relevant question is whether the change corrects something that bears on the choice.

Sometimes no useful repair is available before action. We should then distinguish the best decision from the best account of why we are making it. Mara may reasonably proceed with Cedar because the earlier review was adequate and the later doubt has a known source of distortion. She may choose Birch because its downside is less dangerous if she is wrong. She may postpone signing. None of these responses requires pretending that the mere arrival of a new preference settled the matter.

## Objections

The first objection is that this asks Mara to disregard her current best judgment. Once she judges Birch better, is it not irrational to choose Cedar?

The answer depends on what counts as her current best judgment. Mara's immediate impression is that Birch is better. But she also knows how that impression was produced. If she takes that knowledge seriously, her considered position may instead be that the ranking is unsettled, or that her earlier comparison is still more reliable. There is no requirement here to act against a settled, fully considered judgment while stubbornly refusing to revise it. The claim is that the first comparative conclusion reached during reconsideration need not be that fully considered judgment.

If Mara has included the evidence about the review, addressed it adequately, and still reasonably judges Birch better, this argument gives her no reason to stay with Cedar. Plans do not acquire immunity from defeat through an appeal to their own stability.

A second objection says that all practical thought directs attention. If every result must survive a further investigation into its source, no decision can be complete.

The proposal does not require universal suspicion. It concerns cases in which the person has a specific reason to doubt the fit between the inquiry and its use. Mara knows that she examined only Cedar's burdens. That is a concrete defect to consider. We routinely distinguish a known limitation from the bare possibility of error. A short repair can be enough, and the importance of the choice limits what further effort is reasonable. A demand to scrutinize every thought would recreate the instability the proposal seeks to explain.

A third objection challenges the separation between changed judgment and changed value. Perhaps imagining the commute changes what Mara wants. If she now dislikes it more, the choice situation itself has changed. Why insist on finding an improvement in her assessment?

This is a serious limit. Deliberation can shape the person who must live with the result. If thought produces a durable aversion, that aversion may affect how good Cedar would be for her even if it began through a lopsided exercise. Its origin does not erase its practical consequences. My claim therefore concerns reliance on a changed ranking as a better assessment, not a blanket refusal to accommodate desires caused by thinking.

Mara must distinguish “I now understand this burden better” from “this exercise has made the burden worse for me.” The second claim can justify changing plans. It requires some basis for expecting the aversion to matter beyond the present exercise. In the case where her preference reverses with each round of criticism, treating every shift as an enduring change in her interests would be especially poorly supported. In other cases a new desire may last, and the proposal permits her to respond to it.

A fourth objection says that the argument amounts to familiar advice to be balanced. The advice is familiar, but it leaves a question open: when may a reasonable review transmit its justification to a revision? The case shows that a review need not be balanced to be reasonable. A focused defect check can be exactly what the agent should do. The mistake lies in taking the comparative feeling it causes as though it were the answer of a balanced comparison. Telling Mara simply to deliberate fairly misses why her unfair comparison can grow out of a fair use of her attention.

Finally, one might worry that this proposal equips stubborn people with an excuse. Any unwelcome consideration can be dismissed as too vivid. That would violate the proposal's symmetry and its demand for a specific explanation. A newly noticed serious defect must be addressed on its merits. The fact that it emerged during a one-sided search does not discredit it. What is challenged is an unsupported shift in ranking, not everything discovered along the way.

## A place for doubt without surrender

A plan should help us act while remaining answerable to reasons. That requires more than deciding how often to reopen it. We also need to decide what weight to give the results of a review once it has begun.

The flat case identifies one failure at this second stage. A person can responsibly ask what is wrong with a plan, answer that question carefully, and then mistakenly treat the resulting loss of attraction as a verdict on whether another plan is better. The inquiry was justified. The transfer of authority was not.

Rational persistence in such a case need not involve refusing to think. It can consist in thinking accurately about what one's additional thought has and has not established. A doubt deserves attention without always deserving control of the next action.
