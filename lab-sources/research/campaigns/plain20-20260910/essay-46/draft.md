# Teaching Students to Question the Lesson

A student can reach her own answer and still be kept from thinking independently. Her teacher need not dictate a conclusion, punish disagreement, or reward flattery. He can give her interesting problems, insist on evidence, and welcome every objection she raises. He can also decide which evidence she will ever have reason to look for.

This is a problem for an attractive educational aim: helping students become able to judge for themselves. We often look for success in what students do with a problem. Can they explain their answer? Can they test it? Can they defend it without asking what the teacher thinks? Those are important achievements. Yet they leave a further question unanswered. Can students investigate how the problem, and the evidence available for solving it, came to be put before them?

I argue that education for intellectual independence must make that further investigation possible. When a teacher's selection materially affects what students can conclude, students need a usable way to question the selection. This does not require access to everything, equal time for every opinion, or escape from all teachers. It requires that growing competence bring increasing access to the choices that shape inquiry, including some means of checking those choices beyond the selector's own assurance.

The familiar worry about manipulation is the starting point. The more specific claim is about the evidence of educational success. Good reasoning within a lesson cannot, by itself, establish that the lesson has made its students independent of its designer. A teacher who undertakes to develop that independence therefore has a duty to create occasions on which control of the lesson can itself be examined.

## A successful lesson with something missing

Consider a course about how a town should use its public spaces. Students care about the town. They interview residents, compare costs, and revise proposals in response to criticism. Their teacher never announces a preferred answer. Students disagree with him, and he gives excellent marks to well-supported disagreements.

But the teacher has quietly restricted their starting materials to spaces already approved for commercial development. The documents describe several genuine choices about those spaces. No document suggests asking why other spaces were excluded, and the course's research portal contains only the selected files. Students can ask for further documents, but only by naming a project or an issue. They do not know that a large publicly owned garden has been left out. Nothing in the available material gives them a reason to suspect it exists.

The students reason well. Some of their proposals may be right. The problem is not that their thinking is fake. It is that their ability to discover a major limit on their inquiry still depends on the teacher deciding to reveal it. He can alter the range of plausible conclusions without confronting their critical skills. He changes what those skills encounter.

Now alter the course. The teacher explains that the initial selection covers only proposed commercial developments. Students can inspect a simple list of excluded sites and the stated reasons for exclusion. They can ask a librarian or planning officer for records through a route the teacher does not control. They need not study every site. Most may reasonably keep working on the original assignment. Their position has nevertheless changed. They can recognize the boundary and investigate whether it matters.

These courses could produce equally impressive final reports. They could even produce the same reports. Yet the second gives the students a form of control over inquiry that the first withholds. The difference lies in what they can do if the framing deserves challenge, rather than in whether they happen to disagree now.

The example concerns a selective inquiry, not an ordinary exercise clearly limited to a stipulated problem. A worksheet asking students to calculate three given areas makes no promise to survey the town. Selection becomes troubling when students are invited to treat their inquiry as supporting a wider judgment while being unable to examine a restriction that could change that judgment. The scope of the educational claim matters.

## Why active learning is not enough

The tension is old. In Book II of *Emile*, Rousseau distinguishes dependence on things from dependence on people. He recommends arranging a child's encounters so that experience, rather than direct commands, guides conduct. In a nearby passage, however, he describes a tutor retaining control over the child's environment while the child experiences himself as choosing freely. The passage shows how personal control can work through apparent encounters with things. It does not establish that Rousseau's whole educational project fails: later in Book III, the pupil can put difficult questions to the tutor. My concern is the limited independence of the earlier arrangement. [Rousseau, *Emile*, Books II–III](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5427/pg5427-images.html).

Dewey offers a different and powerful account of learning. In chapter XII of *Democracy and Education*, students need a problem that engages them, material with which to address it, and opportunities to develop and test their own suggestions. Teaching can involve active participation by the teacher; it need not mean handing over finished answers or leaving students alone. This explains why the first town-planning course can accomplish real education. The students care, inquire, and test. It also gives us resources to improve the course: the arrangement of its materials can become a further problem for inquiry. [Dewey, chapter XII](https://www.johndeweyphilosophy.com/books/democracy_and_education/Thinking_in_Education.html).

Contemporary discussion already recognizes the tension between a learner's activity and a mentor's influence. Ania Atkinson's proposal for mentoring new teachers draws on Rousseau while acknowledging the problems of hidden guidance. It defends choice, personal experience, and a supporting mentor. My argument shares the rejection of education as mere delivery of answers. It adds a demand concerning the mentor's position: support should include a route for investigating the limits of what the mentor supplies. This is a proposed clarification of an established difficulty, not the discovery that teachers influence students. [Atkinson, “Not just Émile”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopedu/qhaf095).

The distinction needed here is simple. A person may be able to judge the evidence she has without being able to judge how that evidence was selected. Both abilities admit degrees. Neither requires complete mastery. A student who understands why one sample is biased has taken a step toward the second ability. A student who can compare two arguments but cannot find out that the teacher has omitted the strongest rival has mainly exercised the first.

Why call the second ability part of intellectual independence? Because selection can guide belief while leaving the person's reasoning untouched. If independence concerns having a meaningful role in directing one's beliefs, it must cover an important way in which those beliefs can be directed. Otherwise, a teacher can meet the standard by replacing visible instructions with invisible restrictions.

This is not an argument that influence makes thought unfree. Every question has a history, and much of what we know comes from other people. The issue is whether one person retains avoidable control over important limits on another's inquiry while the second has no usable means of recognizing or questioning that control. Reliance on a teacher can be reasonable. Reliance that the teacher makes impossible to examine is a different relationship.

## What teachers owe

The duty follows from three claims. First, teaching for independence aims to improve students' capacity to direct and correct their own inquiry. Second, a teacher's selection can systematically shape conclusions while evading the reasoning skills that students display within the selection. Third, where an educator can reasonably help students recognize and assess such a source of direction, leaving it wholly beyond assessment leaves the educational aim partly unmet.

The conclusion is limited but substantial. Teachers who claim to educate for independence should provide proportionate opportunities to examine consequential choices in the construction of lessons. They need not surrender all control. They must help students acquire some control over whether continued reliance is warranted.

Three practical features make this duty concrete.

Students need to know where a consequential choice has been made. In the town course, saying that the files cover only a certain kind of site is already useful. In history, a teacher might explain that the week's documents all come from officials. In science, students might be told that an exercise removes measurement noise to isolate one relationship. Such statements make a limit visible without burying learners under a history of every editorial decision.

Students also need a manageable way to investigate whether the choice matters. A catalogue of a million documents will not help a beginner who cannot search it. A short index, a worked example of an omission, access to another informed person, or time to request a different source may. The relevant test is whether students with the skills the course has actually taught can use the opportunity. A formal invitation to object is weak evidence if the grounds of possible objection remain hidden.

Finally, their investigation must be allowed to affect the inquiry. If an excluded site matters, the teacher might widen the assignment, narrow what the class claims to have shown, or explain why the exclusion remains justified. He need not accept the student's preferred conclusion. He does need to answer the relevant challenge as a challenge about evidence and scope, rather than treating the act of making it as a failure to follow the lesson.

Together these features allow students to do something more precise than “question everything.” They can ask what this inquiry covers, find a relevant way to check, and seek an appropriate correction. That ability can strengthen trust. A student who learns why the simplification is harmless has a better basis for relying on it.

## Must the checking escape the teacher?

A teacher might meet all three demands on paper while remaining the sole judge of whether they have been met. He explains his selection, provides a second packet he has also selected, and answers objections with further materials of his choosing. Is that enough?

Sometimes it is a valuable beginning. Yet it cannot be the entire mature arrangement if the concern is dependence on that particular teacher. His assurance that he has not excluded anything important leaves the student reliant on the very selection being assessed. It may be true and responsibly accepted. It does not supply an independent check on his power to select.

The further requirement is modest. Some checks should reach materials, people, or procedures whose relevant answers the teacher cannot simply determine. An outside archive, a different teacher, a source the student finds, or observation outside the prepared exercise may provide this. None is infallible. None stands outside society. Their point is to make one person's control incomplete.

There is no endless demand to find an authority free of every influence. The target is a particular dependence. A second source can reduce that dependence even though it creates another. Students must still judge whether the second source is relevant and reliable, perhaps with help. Intellectual independence can grow through better organized reliance on others.

Nor does an outside website automatically help. Ten sites repeating the same selected report may preserve the restriction. What matters is whether the checking route has some capacity to reveal a relevant omission or mistake that the original selector would prefer, or simply fail, to disclose. This is a functional requirement, not a demand for a certain number of sources.

## The strongest reasons to keep control

The most serious objection is educational. Beginners cannot yet judge which questions are worth pursuing or which sources deserve attention. They need selection precisely because they lack the capacity this argument asks them to exercise. Requiring them to inspect every limit would replace learning with confusion.

This objection defeats a requirement of immediate, complete control. It does not defeat gradual opportunities to examine control. Learning to assess a selected source can itself be taught through a selected example. The teacher might first reveal one harmless omission and work through its effects. Later, students can investigate a less guided case. At some point, a check must be possible beyond cases designed to make the teacher's judgment look good. But it need not occur at the beginning of every lesson.

Development also limits blame. A course in basic reading may contribute to later independence without being capable of completing it. The wrong inference would be that successful reading exercises already show independence in deciding what to read or whom to believe. My claim concerns what the achievement establishes and what a broader educational commitment still requires.

A second objection concerns sound knowledge. Should a biology teacher really make space for every rejected view, or a mathematics teacher let the class vote on proof standards? No. The ability to challenge an exclusion does not entail a right to have the excluded material taught as credible. Teachers can explain that a view lacks relevant evidence, teach students how such failures are assessed, and retain the curriculum. A serious challenge may show that a source has been misclassified. It may also fail. The duty concerns a route of assessment, not a guaranteed change of verdict.

A third objection is that all this takes time from the subject. That is true. The duty should track the importance of the hidden choice and the educational claim being made. A minor simplification that students can already identify may need only a sentence. A course claiming to prepare citizens to judge public policy needs more. It cannot reasonably count polished arguments on preapproved questions as complete evidence that its graduates can direct public inquiry.

A final objection is deeper. Suppose the first town teacher is exceptionally wise. His selected materials lead to correct conclusions, and his students acquire transferable reasoning skills. Perhaps allowing outside inquiry now would expose them to persuasive falsehoods. Why sacrifice reliable learning for an opportunity to make mistakes?

The answer is not that mistakes are always worth the cost. Temporary restrictions can be justified, especially for young learners. But two judgments must remain separate: whether the restriction is justified, and whether students have acquired independence in the restricted area. A justified dependence is still a dependence. Calling it independence conceals what the teacher has retained and makes it harder to ask when that control should end.

Where the aim includes eventual independence, justified temporary control should come with a path toward its reduction. Otherwise the justification can renew itself forever: students are denied practice because they lack judgment, then denied control because they lack practice. The teacher must sometimes accept a bounded risk of an inferior answer so that the student can learn to assess a question the teacher has not fully settled for her.

## What educational success would show

An examination can establish that a student reasons well from supplied evidence. It cannot, without further evidence, establish that she can assess the supply. This matters because a school can look open-minded while retaining decisive control over what counts as an occasion for thought.

A fuller assessment would sometimes ask students to identify a limit in an assignment, find a relevant source beyond its starting materials, and explain whether that limit changes what can be concluded. They may defend the original assignment. Independence should not be measured by the frequency of disagreement. It should be measured in part by the student's ability to discover when agreement rests on a choice worth checking.

Even this performance is evidence, not a guarantee. A rehearsed exercise can teach students to produce the approved objection. The educational task is to extend the capacity into less prepared settings, where a challenge may surprise the teacher and require a real response.

Teachers must choose what students first encounter. They should also teach students how to investigate those choices. Education begins with a world arranged for the learner. An important part of its success is that the arrangement eventually becomes something the learner can question.
