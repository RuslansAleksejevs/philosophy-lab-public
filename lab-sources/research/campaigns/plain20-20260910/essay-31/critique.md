# Critique of What a Mathematical Explanation Leaves Open

## Reconstruction and verdict

The central result is sound and clearly exhibited: two connected boards have the same black/white totals but different tiling answers, so those totals cannot determine tiling throughout that family. The essay argues that learning this limit improves one's understanding of the colour argument as a reusable method, even without improving its proof of the original impossibility. The rectangle restriction shows why such limits depend on the family being studied.

This is a promising, modest philosophical clarification, not yet a well-established original contribution. The mathematical reasoning succeeds. The chief weakness is that the essay slides between understanding a theorem's explanation, understanding an inference, and understanding the problem-solving power of a representation. Its explicit qualifications help, but the distinction should become the argument's centre. Revise rather than set aside.

## Argument and strongest objections

1. **The two-reader comparison is too easy.** The first reader makes a converse error; almost any account will count the second as understanding more. The stronger comparison is between two careful readers who both know the proof is one-way. One suspends judgment about whether totals could suffice by another argument; the other can prove they cannot suffice. Explain what additional ability the latter gains. For example, the latter can reject every proposed complete decision procedure based only on those totals without inspecting it, and can give a reason to acquire adjacency information. This would support the distinctive claim without relying on a logically careless foil. The draft already gestures at this comparison; develop it rather than spending equal space on the obvious mistake.

2. **What exactly is better understood?** Someone can fully understand why unequal totals prevent covering, yet be uninterested in complete classification. The pair adds understanding of the information supplied by a method, not obviously a deeper understanding of why the original board cannot be covered. The draft admits this, then repeatedly calls it a deepening of “an explanation.” State a clean distinction: understanding the explanation of the original result may stay fixed, while understanding its value for a further task increases. Defend the latter as a philosophically worthwhile object in its own right. Otherwise the conclusion risks becoming true only because “understanding an explanation” has silently expanded to include all knowledge about its use.

3. **The information is chosen by the analyst.** The original proof does not literally contain only two numbers: it also uses the rule that each legal domino connects opposite colours, and hence structural facts about adjacency. Your abstraction can legitimately retain totals and hold the domino rule fixed, but say so explicitly. A critic can otherwise object that richer pairing arguments develop information already visible in the proof. The impossibility result concerns decision procedures whose case-specific input consists solely of totals; it does not prove that the proof idea, “counting,” or the original reason has reached every limit. Retain the third objection, but sharpen this distinction before it is needed.

4. **A proved global limit is not silence on every balanced input.** The pair proves non-decision for the whole family and ambiguity at totals 3/3. It does not establish that totals answer no balanced case. For example, every connected two-cell board is tileable, so totals 1/1 together with connectedness settle that case. The manuscript usually makes the weaker, correct claim, but its broad phrases about where “it must stop” can suggest the stronger one. Add this example or a brief explicit scope sentence. This also improves the family discussion: background knowledge matters even within the same broad family, not only after switching to rectangles.

5. **Why mathematical explanation rather than ordinary information loss?** Any coarse description can group together cases with different answers. That general fact applies equally to non-explanatory algorithms and lists of measurements. Acknowledge that the pair test is not a criterion distinguishing explanatory proofs from other tools. Its interest is a norm governing the use of explanations after they are adopted. Argue for the explanatory application without presenting an elementary information-loss observation as itself a discovery about explanation's nature.

## Significance and originality

The distinction between an unproved converse and a proved inability of selected data to decide is the strongest feature. It yields a useful practical consequence: one can know that looking for a more ingenious formula in the same inputs cannot solve the stated complete task. That is more precise than advice to check hypotheses.

The significance remains limited by the absence of an opponent who counts only successful extensions as understanding. The draft's conditional criticism is fair, but it should not imply the surveyed theories actually make that mistake. A strong final version can offer a sharply specified addition within an ability account, without claiming to overturn established theories.

Originality is unresolved. Avigad explicitly includes counterexamples showing what happens when hypotheses are removed among abilities relevant to understanding proofs (preprint pp. 9–10). That is a much closer predecessor than a general reference to Part I §2 suggests. Explain why your test is not merely his ability instantiated: it rules out all complete classifiers on fixed case-specific data, rather than only refuting a theorem with a deleted hypothesis. Conversely, concede that this distinction might be a useful elaboration rather than a new philosophical position. A limited search cannot settle priority.

## Sources and scholarship

The inspected sources broadly support the manuscript's summaries. Steiner's printed pp. 143–144 explicitly make characterizing properties relative to a family and relate explanation to changing objects and theorems. Baron, Colyvan and Ripley's preprint p. 4 sets out fixed facts, alteration and propagation of consequences. Neither source was shown to deny the gain you identify; preserve that restraint.

The functional account needs more care. Inglis and Mejía-Ramos §3 describes explaining with respect to a proposition and improvement in understanding that proposition. Your extra pair may improve understanding of a method while leaving understanding of the original proposition unchanged. Therefore present this as a question about choosing the object of understanding, not a straightforward extra kind of gain their account must already count. They also distinguish their approach from some ability-based accounts. Avoid implying that all four sources form a single programme.

Exact sources checked and locators: [Avigad](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/avigad/Papers/understanding.pdf), introduction, Part I §2 and preprint pp. 9–10; [Steiner](https://paperzz.com/doc/9454464/mathematical-explanation), printed pp. 143–144, imperfect OCR; [Baron et al.](https://api.research-repository.uwa.edu.au/ws/files/65657562/intramath18.pdf), preprint pp. 3–4; [Inglis and Mejía-Ramos](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-019-02234-5), §§2–3. A publisher lead for “Revisiting the mutilated chessboard or the many roles of a picture” failed to open; no substantive claim about it is warranted. I did not independently read all four full works. Retrieval notes preserve these limits.

## Concrete language review

The opening and mathematical examples are readable without specialist background. The abstract prose becomes harder precisely where the philosophical claim needs most help. The following review includes the central thesis and strongest objection.

**Opening:** “Remove two opposite corner squares from an ordinary chessboard. Can the remaining board be covered with dominoes?” The problem is immediately understandable. “Ordinary” assumes board size, and the next sentence could explain domino size more directly. Optional rewrite: “Take an eight-by-eight chessboard and remove two diagonally opposite corner squares. Can dominoes cover what remains? Each domino covers exactly two squares that share an edge.” The rest of the rules can follow as written. No dramatic scene-setting is needed.

**Central thesis:** “I will argue that understanding a mathematical explanation as a reusable reason includes understanding what its chosen information leaves open.” “Reusable reason” and “chosen information” arrive before the reader knows what choosing information means. Rewrite: “To use this explanation on other boards, we need to know what the colour counts can tell us—and what they cannot. Learning that limit can improve our understanding of the method, even when we already understand the original proof.” This also helps resolve the philosophical ambiguity.

**General form:** “Then find two permitted cases that agree on that information but differ on the further answer being sought.” This is accurate but impersonal. Rewrite: “Next, find two cases with the same recorded information but different answers to the new question.” Keep the preceding requirement to state the family; “permitted” need not carry it again.

**Family section:** “A limit of an explanation's information is therefore relative to what else is held fixed.” The noun chain hides the simple thought. Rewrite: “What the colour counts can tell us depends on what else we know about the board.” Follow with the rectangle example, then generalize if necessary.

**Literature section:** “Successful adaptation is one achievement; showing that a retained description is too coarse for a further task is another.” “Retained description” and “too coarse” demand translation. Rewrite: “It is one thing to make a proof work on a new case. It is another to show that the facts it uses cannot answer a new question.” Qualify “facts it uses” as case-specific inputs elsewhere, as recommended above.

**Strongest objection:** “The first objection is that this is only the elementary distinction between a sufficient condition and a necessary condition.” The jargon is immediately illustrated but need not lead. Rewrite: “Is this just the familiar warning that an argument does not prove its converse? Unequal colour counts rule out a covering. That does not mean every impossible board has unequal counts.” Then state why two cases do more than warn against reversing an implication.

**Conclusion:** “Sometimes it grows because we can prove exactly why, at this point, it must stop.” The referents of “it” shift between understanding and explanation, and “exactly” promises more than one ambiguous input proves. Rewrite: “We also learn when we can prove that the same colour counts occur on boards with different answers. Then we know that those counts cannot solve the whole problem.” This ends on the demonstrated result instead of a wider slogan.

## Revision priorities

First, replace the careless-reader comparison with a developed comparison between two cautious readers. Second, separate understanding the original explanation from understanding the method's limits, and state the case-specific input restriction. Third, bound the result to non-decision over the family, with balanced cases such as the two-cell board acknowledged. Fourth, engage Avigad's counterexample passage and the proposition-specific functional account. Finally, carry the concrete language changes across the abstract sections while preserving the mathematical example's direct style. No manuscript edits were made by this critic.
