# When Imagining Otherwise Should Change Your Mind

You are reading a faded date on a letter. At first, the last digit looks like a 3. You picture how a 5 would fit the surviving marks. Now it looks like a 5. You picture the 3 again, and the mark looks like a 3 once more. Does this give you a reason to doubt your first reading, or have you only found a way to distort a good view?

The difference matters. Advising someone to imagine an alternative is a familiar way to loosen a hasty judgment. Yet imagination can also create doubt where the evidence was good. A person who can picture a different answer has not thereby found a reason to believe it. Nor has a person who can alter an impression necessarily exposed a defect in the original impression.

Trying to picture another answer can show why the marks seem to support the answer we have in mind. I defend a limited lesson from this discovery. When either imagined answer produces a convincing look, and we have no separate reason to trust one way of looking more, the feeling of confirmation does not by itself favor either answer. That need not remove information already supplied by the marks. To lower confidence in the first reading, we need something further: a trustworthy check that challenges a feature on which that confidence depended. The hardest part is making sure our check has revealed a weakness rather than created one.

## What the exercise can show

Consider a more detailed version of the letter case. Mara has good ordinary eyesight. She knows that the date ends in either 3 or 5, because the writer used one of two dates established by other records. The paper is damaged. Two curves survive clearly; a short mark at the upper left is faint. Initially, Mara says that the digit is 3. Asked why, she points to what looks like a continuous upper curve.

Mara then tries to see how a 5 would fit. The faint mark looks like the beginning of its top stroke. Returning to the 3, she finds that the same mark appears to belong to a curve. She alternates several times. She does not discover a further stroke or move to better light. Her sense that the mark decisively belongs to one digit follows the digit she pictures.

This is a proposed case, not a report of an experiment. It describes something weaker than making black look white by deciding that it should. The visible marks continue to constrain Mara: she cannot make the date look like a clear 8. What changes is the apparent connection among unclear parts. Whether that change occurs within vision, in visual imagination alongside vision, or in Mara's immediate interpretation is left open. In all three versions, she is tempted to use the resulting appearance in the same way: as confirmation of which digit the writer put down.

Her exercise supplies two sorts of information. There is information about the letter: the marks she can presently make out fit more than one reconstruction. There is also information about her own response: an appearance she took to settle the choice can be obtained by first picturing either answer. The second finding matters even though Mara does not learn which date is correct.

Suppose a measuring device has a setting labelled 3 or 5. When we select 3, it displays 3; when we select 5, it displays 5. We cannot yet conclude that it has no contact with the object being measured. Perhaps one setting improves its sensitivity while the other makes it fail. But unless we have some reason to prefer one setting, getting the selected result is no independent check on our selection. Repeating the measurement does not repair this problem.

Mara faces the same limited question. Does the convincing look favor the answer, or does it reflect which answer she selected? A result can do both. Perhaps the shape influences how readily each reading emerges, even though both are possible. The exercise has not measured all the information in Mara's experience. What it challenges is a particular inference: because the selected reading now feels visually decisive, it has received a fresh check that favors it over the rival. If either selection can yield that feeling, Mara needs another reason to regard one setting as more trustworthy.

This is different from the familiar warning against counting the same evidence twice. Repeatedly reading a clear 3 may add little new information, while the original evidence remains excellent. In Mara's case, she has also learned something new about the feeling she was treating as confirmation: she can obtain it for incompatible readings. This discovery calls into question the value of that feeling as a way to choose between them. It does not show that the entire appearance has become worthless, or even that it contains no new information.

There are therefore three claims to keep apart. A repeated look may duplicate evidence. A discovered dependence may undermine one supposed sign of correctness in a current look. A later check may challenge evidence supporting the first look. None of these claims entails the next. Mara's alternating exercise supports the middle claim only insofar as she was treating the feeling of decisiveness as a sign that favored one digit. Whether it also supports the last claim remains to be established.

Mara need not assign equal probabilities to the dates. Other records might favor one, and clearly surviving marks may do so too. Even the uncertain connection might retain some value. What she should withdraw for now is the claim that its becoming convincing under the selected reading is, by itself, a further reason to prefer that reading.

## An old problem and a narrower question

The general danger of thought helping to produce its own apparent evidence is already central to work on perception. Teng argues that an experience can lose evidential force when imagination helps fabricate the content it presents. She also distinguishes this from helpful interactions between imagination and perception (2016, §§4–5). The mere involvement of imagination is therefore not the discovery at issue here.

My question concerns a person's response to an imaginative check she has actually performed. What can she reasonably infer from discovering that her impression changes? Answering this question need not settle whether the experience had less force before she made that discovery. It concerns her reasons after it.

There is also no need to settle the disputed claim that thought changes perception itself. Brogaard and Gatzia stress the need to distinguish changes in perception from other processes that can explain apparent influence by thought (2017, §§1–3). The letter case leaves this distinction open. If Mara changes only her interpretation, she still has reason to reconsider an argument that mistakes her interpretation for fresh visual confirmation.

The kind of reconsideration needed is familiar in discussions of defeaters. A defeater is a reason that takes away some support another reason seemed to provide. It can supply evidence against a conclusion, or it can weaken the connection between the original evidence and that conclusion. Glüer explains this difference in her discussion of perceptual reasons (2018, §4). Mara's discovery primarily does the latter. It tells against the force of her apparent confirmation, without telling her that the date is 5.

The contribution proposed here is a distinction within this familiar picture. We must separate evidence that our current way of checking is compromised from evidence that our first judgment was compromised. An imaginative exercise can supply the former without supplying the latter. A useful account should say what connects them when both conclusions are warranted.

## Why the first impression is a separate matter

Imagine that Leo reads an undamaged digit in daylight. It clearly looks like a 3. He then closes one eye, stares for a long time, and works hard to picture a 5. Eventually he manages a peculiar 5-like appearance. When he relaxes, the clear 3 returns. Leo has discovered that effort can distort his experience. It would be a mistake to conclude, on that basis alone, that his original reading was doubtful.

This example blocks a tempting argument: if an experience can be changed without changing its object, it was never a good reason for belief. Good sources of evidence can be damaged or misused. The possibility of damaging them is not evidence that they were damaged earlier.

The same point applies even when the later exercise produces equally convincing appearances. Perhaps deliberate imagining starts a process that was absent during the first look. If so, the later changes need not diagnose the first look at all. A person cannot fairly treat an experimentally induced difficulty as proof of a pre-existing one.

This problem persists even if the first and later looks concern exactly the same feature. Suppose Mara initially sees a genuinely continuous faint curve. Ordinary vision picks it out correctly. Learning to impose a 5 then leaves her unable to inspect it without competing imagery. She can no longer trace the curve reliably. Its disappearance from her present view does not show that it was absent from her original evidence. Naming the same feature at both times is not enough to connect their reliability.

Now change what happens after the exercise. Mara puts aside both proposed digits and uses a magnified view under better light. She checks individual areas of ink, rather than asking which complete digit she sees. The upper connection contains a patch where the surface has flaked away. In the improved view, this gap remains visible whether she has just pictured 3 or 5. She has no sign of fatigue or reduced acuity, and can make out nearby marks more clearly than before. The damaged digit remains unresolved, but the paper does not preserve the continuous ink trace she thought settled it.

This check gives her a reason the alternating exercise alone did not. It improves access to the feature while reducing the relevance of the contested choice. Seeing the gap does not depend on completing the figure as one digit. These facts give her a defeasible reason to trust the check over her earlier impression of an unbroken trace. The paper has not changed in the interval. She should therefore withdraw the added confidence she placed in the claim that the trace was visibly complete.

The contrast is between two outcomes of checking the same feature. In the first, persistent imagery leaves Mara with worse access and no good reason to prefer the later view. In the second, a better view supplies a stable detail that challenges her earlier account of the ink. This does not prove that the first experience was wholly unreliable. A damaged 3 can still be a 3, and her first impression may have responded to its overall shape. It shows why a particular reason for being confident no longer holds up.

There is a further limit. When asked why she read 3, Mara might sincerely cite the curve without giving the full basis of her perception. Spacing, shape, and context may have helped without her noticing their role. Losing her verbal explanation would not automatically discredit those contributions. The conclusion concerns the extra confidence she placed in an allegedly decisive trace. It becomes stronger only if she has reason to think that this trace really carried most of her confidence. An unexplained first impression can retain weight even after one explanation of it fails.

If no better check is available, Mara should distinguish her inability to defend the first reading now from evidence that it was poorly supported then. She may reasonably remain unsure how much weight to give it. She should not turn this uncertainty into a claim that the imaginative exercise has diagnosed the original experience.

## Discovery and interference

One might object that the account assumes its answer. How can Mara know whether she has exposed ambiguity or created it? She can see the letter only by using the very capacities she is checking.

Mara still has to use her eyes to check what happened. That does not make every comparison useless. The improved view supplies a reason to prefer one check; merely struggling longer with competing images does not. Other comparisons can matter too. Does the alternative require prolonged effort, or can she reach either reading with a brief suggestion? Does one reading reveal a stable mark that the other leaves unexplained? Does a better view preserve the same uncertainty, or resolve it? Does the problem concern the feature that originally did the work, or a new way of looking that she deliberately introduced?

None of these questions is a conclusive test by itself. Equal ease does not guarantee equal reliability. A familiar wrong reading may be easier than an unfamiliar correct one. Stable appearances can also be false. What these questions provide is a way to look for reasons that favor one account of the change over another. They make the exercise answerable to more than the bare fact that an alternative became imaginable.

This matters because an account of rational doubt need not be an infallible method of detecting error. Mara can have a good reason to doubt a particular appearance without knowing exactly how the appearance was produced. We routinely accept this possibility when an unreliable witness gives a report. Learning that the witness repeats suggestions is relevant even before we learn whether this particular report was suggested.

There is nonetheless an important restriction. If Mara already knows that she can freely replace what she sees with convincing imagery, the fact that she has done so again supplies little new information. She must assess the original reading using what she knows about the circumstances in which that reading arose. The imaginative exercise earns its force by revealing a relevant dependence she had not already allowed for, not by the drama of a changed experience.

An objection in the opposite direction says that this is too cautious. Once Mara can produce both readings, surely she must suspend judgment entirely. But that treats one weakened source as though it were her whole evidence. The letter's contents might mention an event that happened only on one of the possible dates. The visual exercise does not remove that reason. Even within vision, a clear lower stroke may favor one reading while the upper connection remains uncertain. A responsible response reduces the contribution of the challenged feature; it need not level all remaining differences.

## Imagination can also help us see

Suppose a colleague says, “Try a 5, and look for its straight top edge.” Mara then notices a surviving horizontal line she had missed. She looks away, returns, and can trace it without maintaining the imagined 5. A magnified view confirms that it is ink rather than a tear. Here the suggestion has helped her obtain evidence.

The origin of the search does not discredit its result. An imagined answer can direct attention to a feature whose existence does not depend on accepting that answer. The mistake would be to identify all guidance by imagination with the production of false confirmation.

The difference from the original case is that the result can now be checked without relying on the same choice that prompted the search. This does not mean that perception must be independent of all background thought. Reading requires learned capacities, and noticing a straight edge involves some understanding of what to look for. The question is whether the mark supports 5 for a reason beyond Mara's effort to picture 5.

Even this independence admits degrees. A person may continue to need the suggestion to notice a real feature. An expert may see a structure that a novice cannot isolate. Failure to make the feature equally clear under every imaginative setting is not enough to discredit it. There may be separate evidence that the expert's method reliably identifies the relevant structures. In that case, the preferred setting has earned its preference.

This is why a requirement to “look the same under every imagined alternative” would be wrong. Reliable detection often depends on asking the right question. The problem arises when the apparent success of a question is our only reason for thinking it was the right question, and incompatible questions can manufacture the same kind of success.

## How far the lesson travels

The letter case is deliberately narrow. It involves two known candidates, a stable object, and a specific disputed feature. Interpreting a person's expression is harder. Imagining that someone is angry may make an expression seem hostile; imagining exhaustion may make it seem weary. But anger and exhaustion can coexist, faces move, and the interpretation may guide attention toward genuinely different features. The simple fact of a change therefore supports less than it does in the controlled letter case.

Still, the distinction between current confirmation and the first impression remains useful. A person who can make a face seem to confirm whichever story she rehearses should hesitate before counting the next confirming look as additional evidence. Whether she should also revise her original judgment depends on what supported it: a clear action, a remembered remark, a particular facial movement, or only the same story-shaped impression. The approach gives no general verdict that appearances of emotion are untrustworthy.

Nor does it make imagination an obligation before every perceptual belief. We could waste our lives trying to disrupt clear perceptions. A reason to test a reading is not supplied merely by the abstract possibility of error. Damage, conflicting readings, an unclear feature doing too much work, or unusually strong prior expectations can make a test worth conducting. The present argument separates the conclusions a changed impression might support, and the extra checks each conclusion needs.

Finally, this account does not establish how often people accurately notice these dependencies. That would require empirical work. People might misremember their first impression, mistake changes of attention for changes in the same feature, or comply with what they think a questioner wants. These possibilities limit the practical reliability of the exercise. They do not remove the distinction between what a well-founded discovery about current checking shows and what it shows about an earlier judgment.

Imagining another answer does not itself give that answer the standing of something we saw. It can instead expose how easily a selected reading acquires a feeling of confirmation. That discovery matters, but it does not tell us how much information remains in the marks or how good the first view was. Mara should not count each convincing reading as a fresh vote. To revise the confidence she placed in the first reading, she needs a check worth trusting. If better light shows the gap she had taken for continuous ink, she has such a check. If she has only learned to interfere with her view, she does not.
