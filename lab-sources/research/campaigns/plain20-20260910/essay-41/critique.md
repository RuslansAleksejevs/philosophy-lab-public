# Critique of essay 41

## Central argument and verdict

The essay distinguishes two conclusions from making an ambiguous digit look like either a3 or a5: the resulting looks should not be counted as independent confirmation; and the first look may deserve less trust. The second needs an additional connection to what supported that first look. This is a sensible and potentially useful distinction. The strongest reading is a claim about what a person should believe after discovering a dependence, not a theory of whether her original experience was justified all along.

Keep this core and revise. The main unresolved issue is that the essay moves among three different claims: no additional evidence from repetition, less support from the current appearance, and less support from the original appearance. Only the third is clearly distinguished from the others. The central Mara case also assumes more about the reliability of her new inspection than the essay's interference objection allows. These are repairable weaknesses, but they concern the proposed contribution itself.

## Argument: highest-priority revisions

1. **Separate duplicate evidence from weakened evidence.** Repeatedly reading a perfectly clear3 may add no independent support after the first reliable reading. That does not weaken any evidence already possessed. Conversely, an unreliable but partially informative test might still give some new evidence. Thus “stop treating each new appearance as fresh support” does not establish “lower the weight of a particular appearance.” The measuring-device analogy makes the gap visible: a device that follows the selected answer most of the time may still respond differently depending on the real digit. Observing agreement with both selected settings on one object does not establish complete unresponsiveness to that object.

A precise repair is to say that the exercise defeats a particular inference: that the felt decisiveness of each selected reading is an independent reason to favor it over the rival. Do not claim that all information in the resulting experience disappears. The essay already preserves other visible marks; extend that care to the apparently unstable feature itself. Explain separately when evidence of instability lowers the earlier estimate of how diagnostic that feature was. One short paragraph distinguishing repetition from newly discovered unreliability would materially strengthen the paper.

2. **Strengthen the bridge back to the first look.** Mara first cites a continuous curve; later she cannot trace it consistently. But a reliable experience need not be something a person can reproduce or trace after a disturbing intervention. Consider Mara looking at a genuinely faint continuous curve that ordinary vision initially integrates correctly. Once she learns to impose the5, that learned ability persists. She can no longer inspect the curve without competing imagery. The physical object and named feature remain the same, yet the exercise has damaged access to good evidence. This counterexample meets the current text's “same feature” bridge while retaining the essay's own reason against backward defeat.

The later list of checks is helpful but needs to do actual work in Mara's case. Add explicit, defeasible reasons to think the later inspection remains informative about the original mark: she stops picturing either digit, checks what ink survives, uses an improved view if available, and finds no continuous trace under conditions at least as good as before. Alternatively, openly narrow the claim to doubt about her present ability to defend her initial claim, without equating that with weaker support from the initial experience. The current text partly takes each route and needs to choose or distinguish them.

3. **Do not equate a stated explanation with the full basis of a perceptual judgment.** Asked why she saw3, Mara may give a sincere but incomplete reconstruction. Her initial perception may also respond to spacing, curvature, or context she cannot name. The draft acknowledges that not every reason is erased, but should explain why a failed verbal explanation challenges the experience at all. A clean answer: it undermines only the extra confidence Mara explicitly placed in this allegedly decisive visible trace. The paper then concerns correcting confidence supported by a claimed feature, rather than generally diagnosing the reliability of first impressions. This narrower result is defensible and still useful.

4. **State the role of lack of a preferred setting.** The exception for reliable expertise is good. Put its condition into the early principle, not only into the later qualification: when neither imagined reading has an independently supported advantage, their ability to produce a convincing appearance does not by itself favor either answer. Otherwise the early “once we have evidence of the second relation” appears to imply that any discovered cognitive influence should cause a downgrade. A reader should not have to retract this inference several sections later.

## Strongest objection and significance

The strongest objection is induced interference, not the demand that Mara suspend every belief. The essay recognizes this and should spend its revision effort there. It cannot simply repeat that she has found a “relevant dependence”: relevance is what the objection contests. The suggested concrete bridge provides an answer that can be disputed and assessed.

The practical significance is modest but real: people often treat a newly vivid interpretation as new corroboration, then move too quickly from manipulability to distrust of an initial perception. A clear explanation of the different inferences can improve reasoning about damaged records and ambiguous expressions. Claims about how often this exercise works remain empirical and appropriately unestablished. The face example is carefully limited; retain that restraint.

## Originality and scholarship

The general thought-to-apparent-evidence problem is established background. Teng's section4 expressly separates lack of original prima facie force from later defeat through awareness of an experience's source. The essay correctly refuses to claim the general downgrade idea as new. Its narrower candidate contribution is an account of what an actual imaginative intervention warrants about later and earlier appearances. At present, the bridge condition risks being only the truism that later evidence defeats an earlier reason when relevant to it. A worked paired case, holding the named feature fixed while changing whether later inspection remains reliable, would make the contribution more informative.

My limited independent search found Siegel's *The Rationality of Perception*, chapter4, especially the listed section4.5 on whether hijacked experiences generate a defeater. Only the publisher abstract and contents were available in the returned result; I did not read the full chapter and cannot assert anticipation. It is a sensible further comparison if accessible. No novelty certification is justified.

## Concrete language review

### Opening

“You are reading a faded date on a letter. At first, the last digit looks like a 3. You picture how a 5 would fit the surviving marks. Now it looks like a 5. You picture the 3 again, and the 3 returns. You have learned something, but what? That the date is not what you first thought? That your eyes cannot be trusted? Or only that you can interfere with an otherwise good view?”

The problem is immediately understandable. The example has a purpose and does not feel decorative. The series of three questions is slightly more theatrical than necessary, and “the 3 returns” can briefly sound as though the ink changes. Optional rewrite: “You picture the3 again, and the mark looks like a3 once more. Does this give you a reason to doubt your first reading, or have you only found a way to distort a good view?” Preserve the concrete opening.

### Thesis, opening section

“An imaginative exercise can give us evidence about how a seeming confirmation is being produced.”

“Seeming confirmation” asks an unfamiliar reader to decode the argument before hearing it. Rewrite: “Trying to picture another answer can show why the marks seem to support the answer we have in mind.” Then state the two conclusions plainly: “This can show that the next look adds no independent support. To doubt the first look as well, we need a reason to think its support had the same weakness.” This last sentence must be adjusted to the repaired distinction between repetition and lost support.

### What the exercise can show

“But once we have evidence of the second relation, we cannot assume the first has the strength it seemed to have before.”

The reader must look backward to identify two unnamed relations; the qualification also overstates the argument before the expertise exception. Rewrite: “If the display follows our choice, its agreement with that choice may tell us little about the digit. We need a reason to think this setting is better than the other.” This is easier to follow and identifies the missing condition.

### Why the first impression is a separate matter

“The relevant connection is therefore evidential, not merely temporal.”

This is a compact technical label after an otherwise concrete section. Rewrite: “It is not enough that both looks occurred at different times while Mara viewed the same letter. The later look must give her a reason to doubt something that supported the first.” Keep the next explanation only if it adds something; avoid restating this three times.

### Strongest objection, discovery and interference

“She cannot escape those capacities altogether. But she need not obtain a view of herself from outside her mind.”

This shifts a good concrete objection toward an unnecessarily grand problem of access to one's own mind. Rewrite: “Mara still has to use her eyes to check what happened. That does not make every comparison useless.” Then give two contrasting checks and explain why they matter. The original objection—“How can Mara know whether she has exposed ambiguity or created it?”—is already clear and should remain.

### Imagination can also help us see

“The required independence is narrower: the evidence should not get its apparent force merely from temporarily adopting the very answer it is supposed to support.”

This is precise but packs several abstract expressions into one sentence. Rewrite: “The question is whether the mark supports5 for a reason beyond Mara's effort to picture5.” Follow with the expert exception so the simpler formulation does not imply that all thought-guided noticing is suspect.

### Conclusion

“To change our assessment of the first impression as well, we need the further connection: a reason to think that what we have now called into question was part of what made that first impression worth trusting.”

The sentence is grammatical but burdens the reader with two “what” clauses and vague backward references. Rewrite: “We should also lower our confidence in the first look if the exercise gives us reason to doubt the feature we relied on then.” Add “and gives us reason to trust this new check” if that is the chosen repair. A concrete final return to Mara would be stronger than another abstract formulation.

## Revision order

First distinguish no new confirmation from loss of support. Second repair the backward bridge with the induced-interference counterexample and clarify the role of Mara's stated basis. Third make the limited contribution explicit against the established literature. Finally simplify the quoted passages and check the entire manuscript for repeated abstract summaries. Do not add length unless the missing argument needs it.
