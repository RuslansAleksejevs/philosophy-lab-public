# When an Answer Becomes the Group's Result

A team needs a meeting room that all its visitors can enter, including a wheelchair user. They agree that access after six is a requirement. The person booking the room asks a caretaker, “Which entrance is open after six?” The caretaker names the east entrance. The organizer says, “Good. We have what we need.” But the east entrance has stairs, and nobody has yet checked for another way inside.

The caretaker may have given a true answer to the question asked. The organizer's conclusion nevertheless gives the team a false impression of its progress. It presents information about opening hours as if it established that the agreed access requirement was met. The mistake occurs when the reply becomes the group's result.

This essay defends a narrow rule about that transition. When someone adopts an answer as completing a shared inquiry, that person needs adequate grounds for the completion they invite others to rely on. They must judge it by the standards the group agreed to use. If they report success under a changed standard, they must make that change clear where others would otherwise reasonably infer success under the old one. Disclosure does not itself authorize the change.

This is a rule about warranted reporting, not a general theory of who controls conversation. A chair can have authority to end discussion without having evidence that its information needs have been met. Nor must an inquiry achieve the practical outcome that motivated it. Learning that no suitable room exists can settle the inquiry perfectly well. The important question is what the report gives people reason to think has been established.

## What has been completed?

Three achievements can come apart. Someone can answer the sentence asked. The answer can resolve the group's information need. And the group can secure the arrangement it wanted. Learning that the east entrance is open may achieve the first without the second. Learning that no accessible entrance is open achieves the second without the third. Booking a suitable room would be a further practical achievement.

The distinction prevents an easy but serious mistake. Suppose the caretaker checks every entrance and establishes that none is accessible after six. The organizer can truthfully say, “That settles it: this room will not work.” No requirement has been dropped. The answer has resolved whether the room meets it. An unmet practical requirement therefore does not, by itself, show that an inquiry remains unanswered.

The opening goes wrong for a different reason. The information leaves the relevant issue open, but the organizer presents it as enough to proceed. “We have what we need” would ordinarily tell this team that its agreed information needs have been met. The evidence supplied concerns only part of them.

There is another necessary limit. A group may agree to balance price, speed, binding, and quality when choosing a printing shop. All four aims count, but they need not be four conditions every acceptable shop must meet. Choosing a cheap shop without binding may faithfully carry out the original plan if that plan allowed the tradeoff. Nobody has quietly abandoned binding merely by choosing against it.

Contrast a group that explicitly requires both poster printing and binding. Finding a shop that prints posters does not establish that this stricter search has succeeded. The difference lies in what the group agreed to do with its aims: insist on them, weigh them, or merely prefer them. A sound report must preserve that difference. It must not turn a requirement into an optional preference, or an optional preference into a veto.

## Questions, plans, and their public record

The importance of purpose is familiar in work on questions. Roberts connects the acceptance of a question with a shared commitment to answer it. Her account tracks questions still under discussion and removes them when answered or found unanswerable. This describes progress in inquiry; it does not imply that an answer fulfills every practical hope that led people to ask. [Roberts 2012, §1.3, (17), pp. 22–23](https://semprag.org/index.php/sp/article/download/sp.5.6/pdf/5868).

Benz relates useful answers to the decisions they enable. His model assumes aligned preferences between the person asking and the expert replying, while allowing their information to differ. It helps explain why a reply need not state everything to be useful. It is not an account of authority to revise a group's aims. [Benz 2004, §§1–2 and pp. 12–14](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/sub/index.php/sub/article/download/748/680/1437). Macagno likewise discusses the different functions questions can perform, including seeking information and guiding decisions. [Macagno 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11217-023-09892-6).

A particularly close account is Habgood-Coote's discussion of group inquiry. It links accepting questions with plans to answer them and uses conversational progress to explain a division of intellectual work. It also recognizes that an answer may resolve a question by supplying enough information for the speakers' practical and epistemic interests. The connection between questions, group plans, and sufficient answers is therefore not a new proposal here. [Habgood-Coote 2022, §3 and n. 36](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-020-00232-5).

My concern is narrower: what happens when one participant presents a limited answer as the result on which the others may now proceed? Groups divide work partly so that each person need not repeat every check. A report of completion lets them stop treating an issue as something they still need to investigate. A mistaken report can therefore remove a question from their practical attention even though the evidence has not resolved it.

This gives the speaker's role a special importance. The caretaker was asked about opening hours. If unaware of the team's wider search, the caretaker need not certify access merely by naming an entrance. The organizer knows what the team agreed to check and speaks as the person bringing that search to a conclusion. In that role, the organizer cannot transfer responsibility for the broader conclusion to the caretaker's narrower answer.

## How a true answer becomes misleading

Consider a team whose members have separate pieces of the story. Before the search, they agree that a room must have evening access for all visitors. Mira will ask about hours. Jonah will prepare directions once access is confirmed. The chair will gather the checks and announce when the room is ready to book.

Mira reports only, “The east entrance is open after six.” She has not inspected it. The chair knows that the entrance has stairs, but has no information about a ramp or lift. Jonah is absent for Mira's report and later receives the chair's message: “Access is confirmed; go ahead with the directions.”

The problem is not that Mira's sentence has acquired a new literal meaning. The chair has made a further claim by using it as the completed result. Given the division of work, Jonah has a reason to think that the relevant checks were made, perhaps by someone other than Mira. He does not need to infer from opening hours alone that the entrance is accessible. He relies on the chair's report that the team's investigation has reached the agreed point.

This is why the chair owes a qualification that Mira's original answer may not have required. The chair invites reliance across the separate tasks. The message should instead say, “Opening hours are confirmed. We still need to check step-free access.” That tells Jonah what can be taken for granted and what cannot.

The case supports the rule in three steps. First, a division of inquiry gives participants reason to rely on reports about work they did not personally observe. Without some such reliance, dividing the work would save little effort. Second, a completion report represents that the reported part has reached the standard that governs it. Otherwise it could not guide the next person's task. Third, a speaker who issues that report must have adequate grounds for what it represents, or clearly limit it. Evidence for one check cannot by itself warrant reporting a larger collection of checks as complete.

The last step is an ordinary norm of responsible assertion applied to a specific conversational role. That is a limit on the proposal's novelty, but it does not make the diagnosis empty. It identifies which utterance carries the unsupported conclusion and which participant is responsible for it. The true answer need not be retracted. The misleading adoption of it needs correction.

A useful test is to ask what someone can reasonably do after hearing the report. Would Jonah be entitled to stop investigating access and prepare directions? If the answer is yes, the report has conveyed more than the fact that a door is open. If the chair knows that this broader inference is unsupported, repeating the caretaker's true words does not excuse leaving the inference in place.

The test concerns reasonable reliance, not every inference anyone might make. A listener who knows the chair means only that hours are confirmed cannot treat the message as proof of a ramp. Nor does every casual “great, thanks” certify the whole inquiry. The group's words, agreed roles, and established practice determine the reasonable scope of the report.

## Evidence, accuracy, and authority

An accurate report and a justified report are different. Suppose a trusted caretaker explicitly confirms step-free evening access, but is mistaken about a broken lift. The chair may have adequate grounds to report access confirmed, even though the report is inaccurate. This is not the same failure as knowingly treating information about hours as if it established access.

The rule does not demand certainty. What counts as adequate evidence depends on the checks the inquiry reasonably calls for and on reasons to doubt them. A speaker must not ignore a known gap relevant to the conclusion being offered. They need not discover every hidden defect before speaking. A complete account of evidence standards would require more than this essay; the central examples use gaps obvious to the person reporting.

Authority introduces a separate distinction. Suppose the chair is allowed to choose a venue and end the discussion. The chair may say, “I am ending the search. We have not confirmed access.” That report can be fully warranted even if the resulting decision is irresponsible. Conversely, the chair may lack authority to drop the requirement while being entirely clear about doing so. Accurate notice would not make the change legitimate.

If an authorized revision allows a narrower search, its completion can be reported relative to that new standard. The chair might say, “We are now checking opening hours only; that check is complete.” This does not establish that the original access inquiry succeeded. Whether the revised project is permissible remains a separate question.

The argument does not depend on a theory under which everyone must consent before a joint commitment can end. Gilbert's account gives a central place to joint commitment and, in its basic case, to each party's open readiness to cease being jointly committed. [Gilbert 2023, §3](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.12971). The reporting rule leaves that dispute open. Even if a chair may unilaterally change the plan, the change cannot supply missing evidence about what the old plan established.

Costs work in the same way. Further inquiry may be too expensive. A group can justifiably stop with an unanswered question. The warranted conclusion is then that continuing is not worthwhile, not that the missing information has somehow been obtained. This leaves room for practical judgment without disguising its price.

## Which standards govern?

The clearest standards are explicit: “Check both opening hours and step-free access before booking.” A requirement also may be understood from what people have done, but institutional roles alone do not settle every case. Being responsible for directions does not automatically give someone authority to add any condition they prefer.

A requirement belongs to the search when what participants have said or done gives the others good reason to think it was included. To determine how it belongs, we must also ask whether it was treated as compulsory, negotiable, or desirable when feasible. The reporting rule works most securely where these facts are clear. Where they are contested, the group may need to clarify its earlier agreement before anyone can confidently report completion.

This is not a device for making every private interest binding. A passerby cannot reopen the printing search by demanding a shop that sells rare ink. Nor can a participant silently raise the standard after a satisfactory answer arrives. A challenge must identify something the report reasonably represents as settled and explain why the evidence does not settle it under the agreed standard.

Groups can also agree on unjust standards. They may openly exclude a visitor's access needs. This essay does not vindicate such an agreement. It identifies a further problem that arises even when a need has already been included: a later report can quietly erase its unresolved status. Preventing that erasure does not replace the moral argument for including the need in the first place.

## Is the real question simply different?

A natural objection says that the team really asked, “Can our visitors get in after six?” Once we represent that question correctly, the chair's mistake is obvious. A reply about opening hours plainly does not answer it.

That is often the right representation. Yet the group must still keep track of why this broader question governs its progress. The chair cannot defend the completion report by pointing only to the sentence Mira asked the caretaker. That sentence was one step within an agreed inquiry. Its answer does not replace the inquiry merely because it is the most recent exchange.

The history of the task and the speaker's reporting role therefore matter together. The history identifies what was to be checked. The role explains why others take the chair's words as a report on that work rather than another partial observation. This combination locates a responsibility that disappears if we consider only isolated question-and-answer pairs.

The strongest remaining objection is that this is simply a case of saying something misleading. It is. The claim is not that inquiry requires a wholly new kind of honesty. It is that truthful answers can support misleading conclusions about shared progress through a further act of adoption, and responsibility for that act need not belong to the original respondent. Recognizing this helps a group repair the right part of its conversation.

If the caretaker's hours are correct, keep them. If access is unknown, leave it unknown. If the chair decides to stop, record the decision as a decision. If the group discovers that no entrance will work, report a completed inquiry with an unwelcome answer. None of these outcomes needs to be presented as another.

## What the next person may rely on

A report of completion is useful because it lets someone else proceed without repeating the work. That usefulness gives it an evidential burden. The person reporting must have grounds for the result that the next person is being invited to take for granted.

The relevant standard is the one the group actually adopted, including its permitted tradeoffs and the possibility of a negative answer. A revision must be identified where silence would misleadingly preserve the old standard; its authorization must be assessed separately. A justified mistake remains possible, and an authorized stop can leave an inquiry unfinished.

In the opening example, the repair is small: “The east entrance is open. We have not yet established that all our visitors can use it.” The sentence preserves the true answer while refusing to promote it into an unsupported group conclusion. That is what responsible closure requires: an honest account of what the others may now rely on.
