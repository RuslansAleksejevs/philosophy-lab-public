# Critique — essay 39

## Judgment and reconstruction

Revise. The draft defends a plausible, useful constraint on describing collective progress, but its central formulation conflates answering a question, finding a successful means, and achieving a purpose. Its originality is limited and needs comparison with a particularly close source identified below. The strongest feature is the distinction between a legitimate decision to stop and an accurate report that the original task succeeded. Preserve that distinction and the refusal to give every participant a veto.

The argument, charitably reconstructed, is: (1) jointly accepting an inquiry can establish public requirements for its success; (2) a true or useful answer can leave some of those requirements unmet; (3) an unqualified success report normally invites reliance on the publicly established requirements; therefore (4) a speaker who knows some requirements remain unmet must not imply that they have been met, even if entitled to stop or revise the inquiry. This is sound in the explicit conjunction cases described. It is not yet a general account of whose purposes determine successful inquiry.

## Main philosophical revisions

1. **A negative answer can settle an inquiry without fulfilling its practical purposes.** Suppose the caretaker establishes that no entrance is accessible after six. The group now knows the answer to the relevant practical information question. It can truthfully say, “That settles it: this room will not work,” without changing its requirements or achieving the purpose of finding suitable access. The question is answerable and answered; it is the desired arrangement that is unavailable. The closing thesis currently says successful settlement requires “meeting those purposes or making the change in the undertaking clear.” That excludes this ordinary case unless “meeting” is stretched to mean informing a decision—which would undermine the inference from a frustrated purpose to an unsettled question.

The phrase “or establishing the relevant alternative” in the first premise gestures at this but does not explain it. Add the negative-answer case and distinguish successful inquiry from successful procurement. Formulate the constraint around what a declaration reasonably conveys: one must not represent accepted requirements as satisfied merely because discussion stops. Some inquiries seek to establish whether requirements can be satisfied; a negative finding can complete them. This is the most important repair.

2. **Accepted purposes are not always jointly necessary conditions.** A group can openly seek a shop that balances price, speed, binding, and print quality, accepting all four purposes without making each a hard requirement. Choosing a cheap shop without binding may complete precisely the original search if the agreement was to choose the best feasible compromise. No purpose has been silently removed. The draft moves from several accepted purposes to an implicitly conjunctive standard; its examples stipulate that standard rather than justify the move. The paragraph about preferred means helps but does not answer this objection. Distinguish requirements, aspirations, and agreed tradeoffs. State that purposes govern in the way the group accepted them; do not demand that every recognized purpose be achieved. Add one case where sacrificing a recognized aim is faithful to the original agreement.

3. **The hardest objection remains the familiar honesty norm.** The essay candidly admits it applies a general rule about faithfully describing undertakings. But “the defect belongs to the transition” identifies a location, not yet a distinctive principle. In the clearest examples, “We found what we need” is simply false or misleading. To improve significance, develop the reliance test already present: why can an answer that is literally true create a misleading public record only when the chair adopts it as the group's result? Describe the change in what participants are entitled to assume, and explain why the chair rather than the caretaker then owes clarification. A carefully worked case of distributed knowledge would provide more substance than repeating the poster example.

4. **Separate misleading description from legitimate amendment.** Making a change clear does not itself authorize it, as the draft correctly observes elsewhere. The final formula's “or” nevertheless makes notice sound sufficient for successful closure. A chair who says “I have secretly dropped your requirement; the search succeeded” has supplied notice but still has not shown success under the old task. Say instead: report completion relative to the revised standard, and separately state whether amendment was authorized. A disclosure can make a report intelligible without making the underlying action permissible.

5. **Specify the epistemic standard without demanding certainty.** The text alternates between requirements actually being met and participants having grounds to say they are met. A reasonable but mistaken success report need not involve the quiet exclusion described in the opening. The claim about accuracy can be factive, but a claim about entitlement must refer to adequate grounds. Distinguish an inaccurate but justified report from a knowingly misleading or unsupported one. The title asks who gets to say it; the argument largely answers what one must have reason to say.

## Scholarship, accuracy, originality, and significance

I independently accessed the cited primary works and checked the passages relevant to the draft, rather than verifying every derivation.

Roberts's §1.3, (17), article pp. 22–23 (PDF indices 21–22), does link acceptance to commitment and removal to answer or unanswerability. The account also discusses practical unanswerability earlier, and removal of entailed questions. The draft's summary is broadly fair, but the locator should include (17), and it should not imply that the work treats answering as fulfillment of every practical goal. [Primary PDF](https://semprag.org/index.php/sp/article/download/sp.5.6/pdf/5868).

Benz's abstract, introduction, and discussion around PDF pp. 5, 10–12 support the claims about aligned payoffs and different informational perspectives. This is a model of utility, not a theory of authorized goal revision. The draft avoids an unfair criticism of it. [Primary PDF](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/sub/index.php/sub/article/download/748/680/1437).

Macagno's introduction and discussion of linguistic/educational approaches support the modest claim about different question functions. The citation is accurate but largely background; its contribution could be stated in a sentence without implying it supplies the proposed norm. [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11217-023-09892-6).

A closer primary source is Joshua Habgood-Coote, “Group Inquiry,” published online 2020, *Erkenntnis* 87 (2022), 1099–1123. Section 3 connects accepted questions, plans, conversational progress, and success; footnote 36 allows a resolving answer sufficient for speakers' practical and epistemic interests. Section 4 connects this to collective plans. This already brings several elements of the draft together. The author's specific concern about misleading reports after goal revision may remain distinct, but the manuscript should explain that narrower difference rather than relying on a gap between pragmatics and groups. [Article, §3 and n.36](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-020-00232-5).

A second relevant lead is Margaret Gilbert's own 2023 précis of *Rights and Demands*, §3: joint commitments ground participants' standing to demand conformity, and ending such commitments ordinarily involves the parties. It matters because the draft invokes shared acceptance and entitlement while declining unanimous control over stopping. It should clarify that its descriptive norm does not depend on adopting Gilbert's account of release. I read the available primary précis, not the book. [Précis, §3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/phpr.12971).

This brief search cannot establish novelty or exhaustion of the literature. No source contradicts the narrow honesty constraint. The main significance would come from explaining how accurate answers become misleading collective conclusions through public uptake; the current draft states that possibility more fully than it explains it.

## Concrete language review

### Opening

> “A team is choosing a room for a public meeting. One person will book it. Another will tell visitors how to get inside. They ask a caretaker, ‘Which entrance is open after six?’”

The people and activity are immediately understandable. The problem only appears once the next paragraph adds that accessibility was already agreed. That late stipulation can make the first paragraph look like an innocent misunderstanding retroactively turned into wrongdoing. Put the shared requirement before the answer. Suggested opening: “A team needs a meeting room that all its visitors can enter, including a wheelchair user. They ask which entrance is open after six. The caretaker names an entrance with stairs. The person booking the room says, ‘Good. We have what we need.’ But the colleague preparing directions still needs to know how everyone will get inside.” This preserves a useful example rather than adding drama.

### Thesis

> “If a group has accepted a question as a means of serving several purposes, it cannot correctly announce that the shared task has succeeded merely because the answer serves one of them.”

“Accepted a question as a means of serving” is abstract, and “serves” leaves the logical relationship obscure. Suggested rewrite, incorporating the substantive repair: “Suppose a group agrees that its search must meet two requirements. Finding an answer that meets only one does not show that the search has succeeded.” Follow immediately with the limits about tradeoffs and negative answers, rather than loading them into this sentence.

### Background section

> “Its target is a change that can occur even when the words of a question and the facts supplied in reply remain fixed.”

“Target,” “change,” and “remain fixed” ask readers to hold empty placeholders before learning what happened. Rewrite: “The group can quietly drop a requirement without changing either the question's words or the caretaker's answer.” Then explain how adopting the answer as final does this. This makes the intended mechanism visible.

### Argument section

> “Together these claims support a constraint: a declaration that a shared practical question has been settled must answer to the purposes included in its acceptance.”

“Constraint,” “declaration,” and “purposes included in its acceptance” obscure a simple obligation. Rewrite: “Someone who says ‘We have what we need’ must judge the result by the requirements the group agreed to use.” This also avoids the negative-answer ambiguity of “settled.”

### Whose purposes count?

> “A purpose counts here when the participants have given one another reasonable grounds to treat it as part of the undertaking.”

This is not bad prose, but “counts” and “undertaking” make the criterion feel circular. Rewrite: “A requirement belongs to the search when what people have said or done gives the others good reason to think they agreed to it.” Retain explicit agreement as the clearest case; do not make institutional roles automatically sufficient.

### Strongest objection and response

> “Its contribution is to make the justification for closure answerable to the history of acceptance.”

This is the most compressed, academic sentence in the central reply. It names the claimed achievement without showing it. Rewrite: “To judge whether the search is finished, we must look back at what the group agreed to find. A speaker cannot remove a requirement just by later describing the original question more narrowly.” This is easier to follow but still needs the amendment and tradeoff qualifications.

### Conclusion

> “Otherwise the person most ready to act can make readiness stand in for shared completion.”

The point is intelligible, but the repeated abstract nouns make it sound polished at the expense of directness. Rewrite: “Otherwise, the person who has enough information to act can wrongly imply that everyone else's agreed needs have been met.” Keep “agreed,” since removing it would broaden the claim incorrectly.

## Revision priorities

First fix the negative-answer case and the distinction between accepted aims and necessary requirements. Next make the norm about success reports precise, including evidence, notice, and authorization. Address Habgood-Coote and acknowledge the limited novelty. Finally revise the opening and abstract formulations throughout, reducing repeated statements of stopping versus success to make room for the harder cases. The result should remain a full essay, but need not grow substantially.
