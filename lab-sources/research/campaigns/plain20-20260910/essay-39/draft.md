# Who Gets to Say the Question Is Settled?

A team is choosing a room for a public meeting. One person will book it. Another will tell visitors how to get inside. They ask a caretaker, “Which entrance is open after six?” The caretaker says, “The east entrance.” It has stairs. The person making the booking says, “Good. That answers our question.” The person preparing directions says, “But can everyone we invited use it?”

The caretaker may have spoken truly. The answer may also be enough for the person booking the room. Yet something has gone wrong if the group treats the second person as introducing an unrelated concern. Suppose everyone already knew that the search was meant to find an entrance suitable for the invited visitors, including a wheelchair user. The answer has not completed that shared task. One participant's need has quietly become the measure of success for everyone.

This essay concerns that small but consequential move. When people seek an answer together, what entitles someone to declare the matter settled? My claim is limited. If a group has accepted a question as a means of serving several purposes, it cannot correctly announce that the shared task has succeeded merely because the answer serves one of them. It must either meet the remaining purposes or make clear that it has narrowed or abandoned the task. A participant can have authority to stop discussion without having authority to turn an unfinished task into a finished one.

The point is not that every answer must satisfy every interest anyone has. It concerns purposes already included in the undertaking. Nor is the claim a general demand for unanimous decisions. It concerns the description of what the conversation has achieved.

## An answer can do several jobs

A question has words, but asking it also has a place in an activity. “Which entrance is open?” might ask for a complete list. It might ask for one usable entrance. It might test whether someone read a notice. We should therefore distinguish an answer to the sentence, an answer useful to a person, and an answer that completes the task for which a group raised the question.

Existing work already makes much of these differences. Roberts treats accepted questions as shared goals and models their place among the questions still under discussion. In her formal account, a question leaves that set when it has been answered or found unanswerable. This gives a clear picture of how answering moves a conversation forward. It also makes acceptance, rather than the bare act of asking, central to the group's commitment. [Roberts 2012, §1.3, pp. 21–25](https://semprag.org/index.php/sp/article/download/sp.5.6/pdf/5868).

Benz examines why an answer that supplies only part of the available information can still be useful. His model relates answers to the decisions they enable. It assumes coordinated preferences between the person asking and the expert answering, while allowing their information to differ. The model thus takes both participants seriously; it does not simply ignore the hearer or the expert. [Benz 2004, §§1–2 and pp. 12–14](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/sub/index.php/sub/article/download/748/680/1437).

Macagno's discussion of the different functions of questions also warns against treating every request for a response as the same kind of act. Questions can seek information, examine a student, guide a decision, or perform other roles in a dialogue. [Macagno 2023, discussion of linguistic and educational approaches](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11217-023-09892-6).

The present proposal builds on these distinctions. Its target is a change that can occur even when the words of a question and the facts supplied in reply remain fixed. A group starts with several acknowledged purposes. It then evaluates the answer using only some of them. The issue is not whether practical purposes matter. It is what licenses the removal of a purpose from the standard by which the conversation judges its own success.

Consider a less emotionally charged example. Two colleagues need a printer. One needs it for a poster and the other for a bound handout. They agree to find a shop that can handle both, and ask, “Where can we get the printing done today?” Someone names a shop that prints posters but cannot bind anything. Finding it advances the search. It does not complete the agreed search. The conclusion remains the same even if posters matter much more to the organization than handouts. That priority might justify changing the plan. It does not show that the original plan has already been carried out.

This example matters because the point cannot be reduced to a special duty concerning accessibility. It arises wherever the purposes used to judge an answer differ from those under which the question was accepted.

## Why stopping does not establish success

The argument rests on three claims.

First, accepting a practical question together can establish a standard of success. The standard need not be stated in technical language. “We need a shop that does both jobs” is enough. Accepting that undertaking means that finding such a shop, or establishing the relevant alternative, is what the present search is for. People may have further private hopes, but those hopes are not automatically part of this standard.

Second, the truth and usefulness of a reply do not by themselves show that it meets that standard. The printing example demonstrates this directly. “The shop on Elm Street prints posters today” may be true and immediately useful. Neither property entails that the shop can produce the handout. Even perfect certainty about the poster service leaves the second part unsettled.

Third, reporting success normally carries the claim that the accepted standard has been met. If the organizer says, “We have found what we need,” participants are entitled to understand “what we need” by reference to the undertaking they accepted. Otherwise they must continually ask whether every earlier commitment still counts. Shared inquiry would cease to provide a dependable record of its own progress.

Together these claims support a constraint: a declaration that a shared practical question has been settled must answer to the purposes included in its acceptance. If it does not, the speaker must qualify the declaration or acknowledge a change of task.

This is more modest than a rule that a conversation must continue until everyone is content. The organizer may say, “We have to stop searching. We will use this shop for the posters and postpone the handout.” That is a decision. It may be wise or unwise, authorized or unauthorized. Whatever its merits, it does not claim that the shop met both requirements. Participants can now reason from an accurate description of where matters stand.

The difference is easy to overlook because everyday expressions are flexible. “That's settled” can mean that a decision has been made, that a factual doubt has disappeared, or simply that no more time will be spent on the topic. The proposed constraint does not prohibit these uses. It prohibits relying on their flexibility to make stopping the discussion function as evidence that its accepted purpose was fulfilled.

One useful test is to imagine someone acting on the declaration. After hearing “We found what we need,” would the colleague reasonably send the handout to the shop without asking about binding? If so, the declaration conveys more than a decision to stop. It represents the result as adequate for a use the group had already included. The missing qualification has practical content.

## Whose purposes count?

The restriction to accepted purposes does real work. A passerby who hears the printing discussion and wants a shop selling rare ink cannot keep the question open merely by mentioning that desire. The group did not undertake to serve it. Likewise, a participant cannot indefinitely add conditions and then accuse others of failing to complete the original task.

A purpose counts here when the participants have given one another reasonable grounds to treat it as part of the undertaking. Explicit agreement is the clearest case. Established roles can also supply evidence. If a meeting organizer asks a colleague responsible for all visitors' directions to help find the entrance, that role may make visitor access an acknowledged part of the search. But roles do not settle every borderline case. The central argument is strongest when the purpose has been openly stated and accepted.

This restriction leaves an obvious difficulty. A group can refuse to recognize an important purpose in the first place. It can decide, wrongly, that wheelchair access does not matter. My proposal does not make such a decision just. It isolates a further wrong: treating an already accepted concern as satisfied when it has instead been dropped. The first wrong calls for an account of which interests a group ought to recognize. The second can be identified from the group's own undertaking, without settling that broader dispute.

There is also a difference between a purpose and one person's preferred means of serving it. The colleague preparing the handout might prefer binding, though stapling would meet every requirement the group accepted. That colleague cannot block a truthful declaration of completion just by insisting on binding. A challenge to closure must identify an unmet accepted requirement, not merely an unfulfilled personal preference.

The group may disagree about whether stapling really is enough. Then it should examine the relevant requirement. The point is not that the challenger wins. The point is that “We are finished” cannot settle the disagreement merely by being uttered. It needs support from the relation between the result and the accepted purpose.

## Three ways to repair the conversation

The entrance conversation can go right in several ways. The caretaker might add, “The west entrance has a ramp and stays open until seven.” If that information meets the agreed need, the practical question is answered. No dispute about authority remains.

Alternatively, the organizer might say, “We have answered which entrance is open, but not which one our visitors can use. We need to ask about a ramp.” This separates a completed factual subtask from an unfinished practical one. It preserves the value of the true answer without exaggerating it.

Finally, the organizer might say, “We cannot resolve access tonight. We will suspend the booking.” Or the organizer might decide to proceed while openly acknowledging the missing information. Stopping can be permissible even when success is unavailable. The remaining practical and moral assessment depends on the case.

These repairs differ in what they achieve. More information can complete the task. A clarification can correct its description. A decision can end or change it. Treating all three as versions of “the question is answered” conceals which of these events occurred.

This distinction also limits the demand for explicitness. People do not need to recite a list of purposes after every exchange. If everyone plainly agrees to postpone the handout, “The printing is sorted” may be harmless in context. An explicit qualification becomes necessary when the broader declaration would otherwise give participants reasonable grounds to rely on a purpose having been met. The requirement is adequate notice of the change, not a ritual form of words.

## Is this just a familiar rule against changing the subject?

One objection is that the argument adds nothing beyond a ban on changing the question. The original question was really “Which usable entrance is open?” The reply concerned only an open entrance. Once the intended question is properly represented, the failure is obvious.

That representation is often helpful. It does not remove the problem. Someone still has to determine which purposes enter the intended question and whether they continue to count after an answer arrives. The same speaker who says that the question is settled may insist that accessibility was always a separate matter. We need a reason to reject this retrospective narrowing. The earlier shared undertaking supplies that reason.

The proposed constraint can therefore be expressed inside a richer account of the question under discussion. It need not compete with such an account. Its contribution is to make the justification for closure answerable to the history of acceptance. A model containing the right question will represent the failure; the present argument explains why this question, rather than the later convenient substitute, governs the claim of success.

A second objection appeals to authority. Chairs, teachers, and managers often have special powers to determine what a conversation will address. Surely a manager may decide that printing the poster is sufficient.

Yes, if the relevant authority permits that decision. But a power to change requirements differs from evidence that the old requirements were met. A manager can cancel the handout. The manager cannot make a poster-only shop have supplied both services by declaring the search complete. Even legitimate control over the agenda leaves this distinction intact. Where an authorized change is obvious to everyone, little explanation is needed. Where it is concealed, authority does not make the misleading description accurate.

A third objection points to cost. Further inquiry may be wasteful, especially when a low-priority purpose demands enormous effort. Does the argument force a group to spend without limit?

It does not. Costs can justify ending inquiry with an unmet purpose. They can also justify revising what the group requires. But “further inquiry is not worth it” and “we achieved what we were looking for” remain different conclusions. A group gains nothing by confusing them except an undeserved appearance of success. The cost objection establishes the importance of permissible withdrawal, which the proposal preserves.

Finally, perhaps all this is merely honesty about plans, with nothing distinctive to say about questions. There is truth in the objection. The constraint draws on a general requirement to describe shared undertakings faithfully. Its philosophical interest lies in how conversational progress can hide a breach of that requirement. A reply can be true. It can be relevant. It can let the person who asked move forward. Yet the move that treats it as completing the common question can still misdescribe what the group has achieved. The defect belongs to the transition from answer to closure, not necessarily to the answer itself.

## Keeping the result visible

A group needs to know not only what facts it has learned, but which of its purposes those facts now serve. Otherwise the person most ready to act can make readiness stand in for shared completion. The others lose a clear way to show that the accepted undertaking remains unfinished.

This essay has not supplied a full theory of questions or a general rule for distributing conversational authority. It has defended one constraint on announcing success. When a practical question was accepted for several purposes, closing it as successfully settled requires meeting those purposes or making the change in the undertaking clear. A true answer may advance the inquiry without earning that declaration. A justified decision may end the inquiry without earning it either.

In the meeting example, the crucial reply is simple: “We know which entrance is open. We still do not know whether it works for our visitors.” That sentence protects both achievements and omissions. It lets the group move on, if it must, while knowing what it is leaving unresolved.
