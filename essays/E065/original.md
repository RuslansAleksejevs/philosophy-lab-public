# What Counts as an Eye? Coverage, Discrimination, and Use in Hooke and Cavendish

## Abstract

Robert Hooke counts nearly fourteen thousand “pearls or hemispheres” on a grey drone-fly. Margaret Cavendish asks whether these are eyes at all and whether so many eyes could be useful if the fly fails to see a spider until it is close. Hooke's account is more complex than either “many eyes, more sight” or “many eyes, no better sight.” He says the facets face every visible quarter, yet only a few local images are distinct at a time and an observing faculty attends to an object. He also says that a fly can turn its head to direct an optical line toward an object and thereby has a visual faculty “as compleat” as that of animals that move their eyes. I argue that this passage distinguishes, but does not fully explain, four dimensions: directional coverage, distinct discrimination, attentional selection, and successful behavior. Cavendish's usefulness objection is a serious challenge to what the count is meant to explain, but her examples alone do not decide whether the facets have a local optical role. The contribution is a bounded clarification of the evidence and tension in this exchange, not a claim that either writer missed the distinction or that the interpretation is unprecedented.

## 1. What does the count explain?

In “Of the Eyes of Flies,” Cavendish questions whether a grey drone-fly really has about fourteen thousand eyes. The microscope might show shining, transparent protuberances that reflect images without being eyes. She then asks why a fly with so many eyes does not see a spider until it is close, or can seem blind in cold weather. If two eyes can do more than a thousand, she argues, the numerous parts appear useless; nature, unlike art, does not commonly make useless things (*Observations*, 59–60).

Hooke's thirty-ninth observation in *Micrographia* complicates this challenge. He reports inspecting a selected large grey drone-fly while changing illumination and position. He describes aligned hemispheres, reflections, differently sized facets, and an approximate count obtained by counting and extrapolating rows. He then dissects the cluster and compares its layers with those of familiar eyes. He also reports that removing the movable eye knobs of crabs and lobsters affects their avoidance of obstacles. That experiment is about crustaceans, not the individual facets of this fly. Hooke uses it, along with structural resemblance, to support an analogy between their organs and the fly's clusters (Observation XXXIX).

The dispute is not settled by the number alone. A count can bear on at least three kinds of claim: what structures are visible, what role those structures play, and what the animal can do with them. The last kind itself divides into several dimensions that should not be treated as synonyms: the directions from which an object can be represented; whether any representation is distinct enough to discriminate the object; which representation receives attention; and whether the animal responds successfully. These are analytic distinctions for reading the passage, not a taxonomy I attribute to Hooke or Cavendish.

## 2. Hooke's account: many directions, few distinct images

Hooke's description supports a claim about directional coverage. He says the facets are arranged so that no quarter visible from the fly's head lacks a hemisphere directed toward it. He therefore calls the fly “circumspect” and says it has “an eye every way.” His optical account adds that an individual hemisphere might represent a whole hemisphere of the surrounding scene, in the same broad sense that a human eye receives a picture spanning a large angle. In this sense, a many-faceted cluster may receive light from many directions at once.

Hooke does not equate that broad coverage with many clear perceptions. He proposes that each hemisphere refracts light toward a possible retinal surface. Because rays arriving far from the hemisphere's optical axis would be less accurately focused, only parts of an object near that axis would be distinctly sensed. Many facets may therefore produce many pictures while only one or a few are distinct. Hooke then conjectures that an “observing faculty” attends to one object of concern at a time. The nature of that faculty, he admits, calls for deeper speculation (Observation XXXIX).

These claims separate coverage from discrimination and attention. A scene may fall within the directions represented by the cluster without every object being distinctly resolved. Even a distinct local image need not be the object currently selected by the animal's observing faculty. Hooke's model thus gives him a reason to reject the idea that a count of facets means fourteen thousand simultaneous acts of clear, attentive seeing.

Yet Hooke's conclusion is not simply that the fly sees no more or less than other animals in every respect. He says first that the multitude does not give it “the faculty of seeing more” than another creature, because a fly moves its head little while a two-eyed animal can quickly direct its eyes. He then says that an aerial crustacean can move its head enough to direct “some optick line or other” toward any object and so has a visual faculty “as compleat as any Animal that can move its eyes.”

The most coherent reading is that the fly's wide directional coverage and its ability to aim at a particular object can provide access to the directions that a mobile-eyed animal reaches by moving its eyes. That would make the systems comparable in directed access without making the fly superior in distinct perception or attention. But this is a reconstruction, not a result Hooke demonstrates. The word “compleat” sounds stronger than mere access to a direction, and the account does not show how a broadly represented but mostly indistinct scene supports a successful response. Hooke's text therefore contains a tension as well as a distinction: it combines a denial of greater seeing, broad coverage, and a claim of completeness, without giving behavioral evidence that resolves how these fit together.

## 3. Cavendish's challenge is about use as well as appearance

Cavendish questions both the microscope's presentation and the inference from visible globules to eyes. She does not say that every glass must always present a false picture: she writes that magnifying and multiplying glasses “may, and do oftentimes” present objects falsely. Her examples concern changes caused by instrument form, reflection, refraction, light, and position (*Observations*, 49–52). In the fly passage she proposes that the globules might be reflective protuberances rather than eyes (*Observations*, 59–60). Hooke's own earlier remarks about the changing appearance of flies under different illumination give her a point of contact with his account; Clucas discusses this use of visual variability in the supplied chapter extract (Clucas, 2022).

Her second move is not reducible to a demand that more parts produce greater total vision. Cavendish asks what use the alleged eyes have. If the spider approaches nearly unchecked, or cold weather leaves a fly apparently unable to respond, the count seems to promise an advantage that the animal does not display. She even considers the answer that each of many eyes might be weak, then replies that if two eyes are stronger than a thousand, nature has given a small creature a wasteful number. This is a real functional challenge: an account of the structures should say what their number contributes.

Hooke offers a possible answer. He says the arrangement covers many directions and that head movement can direct an optical line toward an object. Those proposals give the facets a possible use beyond simply being visible bumps. They do not establish that the count improves avoidance or any other behavior. Cavendish's spider example is therefore relevant to the adequacy of Hooke's functional story, especially if that story promises effective awareness across directions. Her cold-weather example also counts as the evidence she offers; its cause and conditions are not established in the passage available here, so I draw no further conclusion from it.

The examples nevertheless underdetermine the local optical claim. A failure to avoid a spider does not by itself tell us whether the fly lacks image-forming facets, whether its images are indistinct, whether an image receives attention, or whether a visual signal produces an effective response. Those possibilities are not claims about what flies actually do; they mark what Cavendish's observations do not isolate. She need not supply a rival mechanism to make her objection. Her examples press Hooke to connect proposed parts with useful performance, but they do not alone identify which step in his optical account fails.

## 4. What the evidence supports

The surface claim is that the fly's head has many regularly arranged, smooth hemispheres and that their appearance varies with illumination and viewpoint. Hooke reports changing light and position, moving reflected images, rows, and a count based on extrapolation. Cavendish's alternative—that some globules might reflect light without being eyes—shows why surface description does not settle the part's role. Still, variation in appearance does not itself disprove the structures or Hooke's count; it makes the inference from image to anatomy something to defend.

The local-function claim is that the hemispheres are optical parts. Hooke supports it with the cluster's dissected layers, comparison with other eyes, and a conjectural account of refraction. His crustacean removal observation strengthens the broader comparison between related clusters and sight, but it does not directly test the grey drone-fly's facets one by one. The inference that the fly's cluster is an organ of sight is plausible. The further claim that each hemisphere forms its own image in the living fly remains a model, not a facet-by-facet demonstration. Cavendish's alternative and the observed variation keep that model open to challenge; her behavioral examples do not refute it on their own.

The animal-level claims must be assessed separately. Hooke's “eye every way” supports directional coverage; his axis account restricts distinct discrimination; his “observing faculty” limits attentive selection; and his comments on the head's movement propose how an object can be brought onto an optical line. Cavendish's spider and cold-weather examples concern behavior. The four dimensions matter because a result about one does not automatically establish another. A broad field does not entail clear recognition of every object; a local image does not entail attention to it; and neither by itself guarantees a timely escape. Conversely, poor observed performance can count against a promise of practical advantage without showing that the local optical parts are not functioning.

This reading grants Cavendish's usefulness objection more than the claim that a high number must yield a superior animal. Her examples put pressure on Hooke's description of a “circumspect” and complete visual faculty: if that description is meant to imply robust detection and effective response, the spider example is a challenge. Hooke's optical explanation answers part of the question by distinguishing coverage from distinctness and attention, but it leaves the behavioral link open. It does not show how much coverage the fly uses, what response its images support, or why the examples Cavendish gives occur. The dispute remains underdetermined on the evidence reviewed here.

## 5. Contribution and limits

The key distinctions are not discoveries attributed to this essay. Hooke himself distinguishes a hemisphere-wide representation from the small axial region he says is distinct, and he separately speculates about attention. Cavendish asks whether the count and alleged function are useful. The essay's contribution is narrower: it puts Hooke's broad coverage and “complete” faculty beside his limit on distinct, attentive vision, then asks how Cavendish's performance-based challenge bears on each claim. This identifies an interpretive tension that a summary of the passage as either “many eyes, more sight” or “many eyes, no more sight” would miss. It does not establish that Hooke's phrases are fully reconciled.

The literature reviewed here limits any novelty claim. Clucas has already analyzed Cavendish's use of variable microscopic appearances and her response to Hooke's fly-eye discussion. Purcell directly summarizes the 14,000-eye argument and spider example (Purcell, 2025, 56–57). I claim no priority for the distinctions and no general new theory of parts or perception. The offered clarification is bounded to this textual exchange and to the excerpts actually read; it is not an exhaustive search of Cavendish scholarship.

This is also not a claim about modern insect vision. No contemporary entomology was checked. The historical evidence is a source-based reconstruction of Hooke's model and Cavendish's objection, not a test of either against current biology.

## Conclusion

Hooke's fly has, in his account, facets pointed toward every visible quarter; it does not thereby have many distinct attended perceptions at once. He also says that head movement can direct an optical line toward an object and describes the resulting faculty as complete. These claims can be made partly compatible by distinguishing directional access from clarity and attention, but Hooke does not demonstrate that this account explains successful behavior. Cavendish's question about usefulness therefore remains live: her spider and cold-weather examples challenge what the large count is supposed to contribute. They do not settle whether the facets are eyes, yet Hooke's proposed local optics do not settle their practical value either. The count is evidence to interpret, not an explanation of coverage, discrimination, attention, or performance by itself.
