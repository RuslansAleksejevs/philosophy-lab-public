# Ignorance by Design: Deliberative Jurisdiction and the Modal Structure of Fair Judgment

*Abstract.* Institutions routinely withhold true and relevant information from competent decision-makers. I argue that the two familiar explanations of this practice — bias reduction and the management of appearances — are each partly right and jointly insufficient. Behind them lies a claim about what the relevant judgments are judgments *of*, and, more fundamentally, a modal entitlement: those subject to institutional judgment are entitled not merely that the judgment fail to rest on certain grounds, but that the person judging not be *in a position* to rest it on those grounds. Designed ignorance is how that entitlement is made good. The account reconceives Raz's exclusionary reasons as facts about deliberative jurisdiction rather than about individual psychology.

## 1. Two questions about withheld evidence

A surprising amount of institutional ingenuity is spent on preventing competent people from learning things that are true and relevant to what they must decide. Examination scripts are anonymized before they are graded. Journals strip author names from submissions. Bids are sealed. Orchestras audition players behind a screen. A federal judge in the United States "shall disqualify himself in any proceeding in which his impartiality might reasonably be questioned" (28 U.S.C. § 455(a)), whatever his own confidence in his impartiality may be. American juries are generally not told that the defendant has done this kind of thing before: "Evidence of a person's character or character trait is not admissible to prove that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character or trait" (Fed. R. Evid. 404(a)(1)). And in political philosophy's most famous device, the parties choosing principles of justice are placed behind a veil of ignorance: they "do not know how the various alternatives will affect their own particular case and they are obliged to evaluate principles solely on the basis of general considerations" (Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, rev. edn, p. 118).

These practices raise two questions that are usually run together and should be kept apart.

The first is why anyone should be deprived of information bearing on the question before them. The suppressed material is frequently probative. That a defendant has three prior convictions for the same offence does raise the probability that he committed this one. The drafters of the Federal Rules were candid about this. The character rule, they wrote, is "so deeply imbedded in our jurisprudence as to assume almost constitutional proportions and to override doubts of the basic relevancy of the evidence" (Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 404, subdivision (a)). A rule that overrides doubts about relevance is not a rule that excludes the irrelevant.

The second question is why, when a consideration is not to be acted on, institutions so often make it *unavailable* rather than merely forbidding its use. We could tell graders the names and instruct them to ignore them. We could tell the jury about the priors and instruct them to disregard. We could tell the judge about her brother-in-law's shareholding and trust her to set it aside. Sometimes we do exactly that. But in the cases we regard as most serious we do not; we build the ignorance into the procedure. Why is removal of the information, rather than a norm governing its use, the preferred instrument?

Call the phenomenon **designed ignorance**: the deliberate arrangement of a decision procedure so that certain true propositions are unavailable to the decision-maker. My aim is to explain it. I shall argue that the two standard explanations — that designed ignorance improves accuracy by neutralizing bias, and that it protects the appearance of impartiality — are each partly right and jointly insufficient; that behind them lies a claim about the kind of act a verdict or a grade is; and that the decisive consideration is modal.

The argument has a consequence for a well-known dispute in practical philosophy. Joseph Raz held that rules function as exclusionary reasons: reasons not to act for certain other reasons, which exclude by kind rather than by weight. Critics have replied that exclusion collapses into weighing. I shall suggest that the critics are right about exclusion conceived as an episode in an individual's deliberation, and wrong about the phenomenon Raz was tracking. Exclusion is real, but it is a property of deliberative architecture rather than of deliberative psychology. Designed ignorance is exclusion externalized — and it is, I think, the best evidence we have that exclusion is anything at all.

## 2. Bias and appearance

### 2.1 The instrumental answer

The first and most familiar explanation is instrumental. Human assessors are unreliable in known ways. Knowing an author's reputation, a candidate's background, a defendant's record, they give these facts more weight than the evidence warrants. Removing the information therefore improves the expected accuracy of the judgment. This explanation sits comfortably inside a broader account of how agents and institutions economize on deliberation. Sunstein and Ullmann-Margalit characterize second-order decisions as strategies adopted "in order to avoid getting into an ordinary decision-making situation in the first instance," selected so as to minimize "the burdens of, and risk of error in, first-order decisions" (*Second-Order Decisions*, working paper, pp. 2–3). Blinding fits the template exactly: a choice made in advance about what will be available later, justified by its effects on cost and error. A closely related instrumentalism is at work in Enoch, Spectre and Fisher's treatment of the law's suspicion of statistical evidence; having developed an epistemological account in terms of sensitivity, they argue that "such epistemological niceties as whether a finding is sensitive are not something the law should care about," and offer instead "an instrumental, incentive-based argument" (*Statistical Evidence, Sensitivity, and the Legal Value of Knowledge*, p. 200).

The instrumental answer is true as far as it goes, and in some domains it goes the whole way. Blind data analysis in the natural and social sciences is a clear case: results are hidden from analysts while analytic choices are made, precisely because confirmation bias degrades accuracy. But the instrumental answer cannot be the whole story about adjudicative and allocative decisions, for two reasons.

**The problem of probative exclusion.** If the objection to character evidence were only that jurors overweight it, the remedy should be sensitive to how much they overweight it and to how probative it is in the instance. Where the propensity inference is strong and the risk of inflation small, the evidence should come in. Instead the prohibition is categorical, and the drafters acknowledged that it overrides doubts about relevance. The rationale the Advisory Committee reproduces is revealing in a different way. Quoting the California Law Revision Commission, it says that character evidence "tends to distract the trier of fact from the main question of what actually happened on the particular occasion," and that it "subtly permits the trier of fact to reward the good man to punish the bad man because of their respective characters despite what the evidence in the case shows actually happened." That is not a complaint about noise. It is a complaint that the tribunal would be answering a different question — a question about the defendant rather than about the charge.

**The problem of the immune assessor.** Suppose an examiner — call her Astra — has been studied exhaustively over many thousands of scripts and shown to be entirely unmoved by candidates' identities. She is not merely unbiased on average; her assessments are invariant under substitution of names. On the instrumental rationale, anonymization for Astra is pointless overhead, and we ought to exempt her, or at least to feel no loss when we do. Yet a candidate told that her script alone was graded by name has a complaint, and the complaint does not evaporate when she is shown the data on Astra's reliability. It is not obvious, from the instrumental standpoint, what that complaint could be.

### 2.2 The appearance answer

The second standard explanation is that designed ignorance protects appearances. Justice must be seen to be done. The canonical statement is Lord Hewart's in *R v Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy*. A motorist was convicted by magistrates whose acting clerk was a partner in the firm suing him over the same collision. The clerk retired with the magistrates. The court accepted the magistrates' sworn account that they reached their decision without consulting him and that he "scrupulously abstained from referring to the case in any way." It quashed the conviction anyway. "[I]t is not merely of some importance but is of fundamental importance that justice should not only be done, but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done," Hewart wrote; "[t]he answer to that question depends not upon what actually was done but upon what might appear to be done" ([1924] 1 KB 256, at 259). Section 455(a) codifies the same thought: disqualification is required wherever impartiality "might reasonably be questioned," not only where it is in fact absent.

The appearance answer explains what the instrumental answer cannot: why the immune assessor is not exempted, and why the Sussex conviction fell despite the stipulated absence of influence. But taken as fundamental it is indefensible, for a simple reason.

**The concealment problem.** If what matters is how things look, then a conflict of interest that is perfectly and permanently concealed is unobjectionable; and disclosing it, where disclosure is what generates the bad appearance, is the wrong. Both consequences are false. A judge who secretly holds shares in the defendant company wrongs the plaintiff whether or not anyone ever learns of it, and she is not exculpated by her success in hiding it. Appearance theories invert the order of explanation. That impartiality should be manifest presupposes that there is something to be manifested; publicity is a way of securing a good, not the good itself.

It is worth noticing that Hewart's own reasoning contains a second strand, which is not about appearances at all. The clerk, he says, was "so related to the case in its civil aspect as to be unfit to act as clerk to the justices in the criminal matter"; "his one position was such that he could not, if he had been required to do so, discharge the duties which his other position involved. His twofold position was a manifest contradiction." That is a claim about capacity, not about perception. It says that the man was not in a position to do what his office required. I shall argue that this second strand identifies what is really at stake.

## 3. Deliberative jurisdiction

Start with the first of our two questions: why exclude the probative?

The answer, I suggest, is that a verdict, a grade, an appointment and an award are not merely estimates. They are exercises of an office, and an office comes with a specification of the considerations it is competent to act upon. Call this its **deliberative jurisdiction**. A judgment reached on grounds outside the jurisdiction of the office is not a worse instance of the same kind of act; it is, to that extent, an act of a different kind.

Consider a referee who awards a penalty partly because the home crowd is roaring. Suppose, generously, that crowd noise is statistically informative: in this league, roars correlate with fouls. The award is not thereby a better-supported decision. It is a corrupt one — and the corruption consists precisely in the fact that a consideration outside the referee's jurisdiction entered the basis of the call. The referee's office is defined by an authorization to rule on what happened on the pitch, and by nothing else. Similarly, a mark that partly tracks the tutor's affection for the student is not a noisy measurement of the essay's quality. It is a favour wearing the costume of a mark. And a conviction that partly rests on the defendant's record is not a less accurate adjudication of the charge; it is, in the Law Revision Commission's phrase, a decision to "reward the good man" or "punish the bad man" — a judgment about a person rather than an adjudication of an act.

This is the familiar thought that the basis of an act matters to its character, applied to institutional roles. The thought is most visible in what Rawls calls pure procedural justice, where there is no independent criterion at all: "there is a correct or fair procedure such that the outcome is likewise correct or fair, whatever it is, provided that the procedure has been properly followed" (*A Theory of Justice*, rev. edn, pp. 74–75). Rawls contrasts this with the criminal trial, his example of *imperfect* procedural justice, where "there is an independent criterion for the correct outcome" but no procedure sure to reach it (p. 74). My claim occupies the space between. Even where there is an independent criterion — the defendant did or did not do it; the essay is or is not good — the normative status conferred by the institution is not identical with a report on that criterion. A conviction is not the proposition *he did it*. It is an authoritative determination, made in a particular office, that the charge has been made out on the materials that office may consider. Its identity conditions therefore include facts about its grounds.

One might object that this proves too much. Surely the point of a trial is to find out what happened, and the point of marking is to find out how good the essay is; grounds matter only as means. But notice how we actually talk when the grounds go wrong. We do not say that the verdict was probably right but poorly supported. We say it was not a real trial. We do not say the appointment went to the better candidate on a flawed basis. We say it was not a genuine competition. These are not hyperbole; they mark the fact that the status in question has not been conferred, whatever the outcome's correspondence with the independent criterion. That is why the loss involved in probative exclusion is a loss the institution can rationally accept: it buys the kind of act it is in the business of performing.

So much for the first question. The doctrine of deliberative jurisdiction tells us why probative material may be excluded: not because it is misleading, but because it is *none of the office's business*. What it does not yet explain is why the material must be made unavailable rather than merely ruled out of consideration.

## 4. Why unavailability: the argument from evidential non-domination

If all that matters is that the decision not be *based* on the excluded consideration, an upright decision-maker could deliver that by self-discipline. She could hear the fact and decline to reason from it — as the Sussex magistrates, by hypothesis, did. So why remove it?

One answer is epistemic and familiar: the basing relation is not luminous. We are poor judges of which of our inclinations trace to which of our beliefs, and an assessor who sincerely reports that a name made no difference may be wrong. This is true and it matters, but it cannot be fundamental, for it makes designed ignorance a concession to human frailty, to be withdrawn from anyone — Astra, the Sussex magistrates — whose discipline is demonstrated. The law does not withdraw it, and I think the law is right.

The deeper answer is modal. I propose:

> **Evidential Non-Domination (EN).** Where an institution confers a normative status on S through a judgment, and S is entitled not to be judged on grounds C, S's entitlement is not satisfied merely by the judgment's not in fact resting on C. It is satisfied only if the decision-maker is not in a position to rest it on C — that is, only if the independence of the judgment from C is robust across variation in the decision-maker's will.

The structure here is borrowed, deliberately, from republican political thought. Pettit's conception of freedom holds that a person is free to the extent that no one else has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (quoted in the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on republicanism, §1.2, citing Pettit 1999, p. 165). The point of the conception is that domination is a structural relation, not a pattern of events. The slave of a kindly master is unfree though unmolested, because "whether his master happens to exercise that power is neither here nor there." My suggestion is that the same distinction applies inside the practice of judging, with the decision-maker's access to information playing the role that the master's power plays in the political case.

Three considerations support EN.

**(i) It predicts the legal data.** *Sussex Justices* is otherwise a very strange decision. The court believed the magistrates. It accepted that the clerk said nothing, that the justices consulted him not at all, and that they were "not in any way biased." On an actualist reading of the entitlement — the verdict must not in fact rest on the improper connection — the conviction should have stood. It did not. The appearance theory can accommodate this, but at the price already described. EN accommodates it directly and without that price: the defect was that the man whose firm was suing McCarthy was in the room with the capacity to shape the decision, and the applicant was entitled that no one should be. Hewart's own second formulation — that the clerk's "one position was such that he could not" discharge the duties of the other — is a statement about capacity, and it is that statement, not the remark about appearances, that does the work.

**(ii) The Two Panels case.** Imagine two hiring panels. Panel A receives full dossiers, names included; every member is, by stipulation and by common knowledge, incorruptible, and their assessments are invariant under substitution of names. Panel B receives anonymized dossiers and is otherwise identical in competence. Candidates are told the difference. Most would prefer B, and — more to the point — a candidate assigned to A has a complaint she can articulate: *it was up to you whether my name counted*. This complaint survives the stipulation of incorruptibility, because the stipulation concerns what the panel does, not what is up to it.

Here is the natural rejoinder. If incorruptibility is a stable disposition, then it is *not* up to them; their forbearance is as robust as an institutional design. I think this rejoinder fails twice over.

First, a disposition of the will is exercised, not merely instantiated. Its persistence is the agent's own achievement, and it is revisable by her. A candidate who must rely on it holds her entitlement at another's pleasure: she is in the position of one who is *granted* what she is entitled to *claim*. That is precisely the asymmetry the republican tradition identifies in the case of the benevolent master, and there is no reason it should be confined to the political case.

Second, and independently, the two arrangements differ in *who answers for the outcome*. Where independence from C is secured by design, the design is public, contestable, auditable, and amendable by those subject to it; complaints about it are complaints about an institution. Where independence is secured by discipline, the only available object of complaint is an individual's character, which the complainant has no standing to legislate and no means to inspect. EN therefore underwrites a demand for contestability that the discipline model cannot meet.

**(iii) It identifies the right currency: authority, not accuracy.** The deepest point is not predictive but jurisdictional. Under the discipline model, it is the decision-maker who determines whether C counts, since it is her forbearance that keeps it out. Under designed ignorance, that determination has been made elsewhere — by the institution, in public, in advance. Designed ignorance, on this account, is not primarily an epistemic technique at all. It is an allocation of normative authority: it removes from the officeholder the power to decide what her office may consider, and vests that power in a publicly answerable design. This is why the practice clusters so tightly around decisions that confer status on identified persons, and why it is comparatively rare in decisions that merely predict.

Notice, finally, that EN specifies a restricted modal range. Independence must be robust across variations in the decision-maker's will — her care, her mood, her interests, her integrity — while holding the institutional design fixed. It is not independence across all contingencies whatever, which no procedure could deliver. This is the same restriction the republican makes when he says that the free person is secured against others' choices, not against the weather.

## 5. Exclusionary reasons, externalized

Raz distinguished first-order reasons to act from second-order reasons to act, or to refrain from acting, for a reason; exclusionary reasons are the negative second-order kind. Rules, on his account, are both first-order reasons and exclusionary reasons; they "exclude those conflicting reasons by kind, not by weight," so that a rule may require action contrary to the balance of first-order reasons (see Wang, *Are Rules Exclusionary Reasons in Legal Reasoning?*, pp. 37–39, citing *Practical Reason and Norms*, pp. 36, 40). Authoritative directives pre-empt rather than add to the balance, and the arrangement is justified by the normal justification thesis: one does better by the reasons that already apply to one by following the directives than by trying to follow those reasons directly (pp. 39–40, citing *The Morality of Freedom*, pp. 46, 53). Against this, Perry and others have urged that exclusion collapses into weighing — that an "excluded" reason is just a reason assigned zero weight by a second-order consideration.

I think the collapse objection is powerful against exclusion conceived psychologically, and I want to say why, because the diagnosis then points somewhere else.

Within a single mind that possesses the consideration C, "excluding C" and "assigning C zero weight" have the same deliberative upshot. The agent surveys her reasons, C among them, and acts as though C were not there. Whether we describe this as exclusion or as extreme discounting is a question about the vocabulary of the model, not about the agent. Worse, the exclusion has to be *managed*: the agent must identify which of her inclinations trace to C in order to discount them, and so C must remain in view in a supervisory role. The excluded reason is not absent; it is present and being handled. That is exactly the kind of difference-without-a-difference that invites the collapse charge.

Designed ignorance changes this, and it is the only thing I know of that changes it cleanly. Where C is unavailable, C is not assigned zero weight; there is nothing there to weigh, and — crucially — the agent lacks the capacity to weigh it. No supervisory presence is required, because there is nothing to supervise. The distinction between exclusion and discounting, invisible inside the head, becomes visible and operative in the architecture of the procedure.

So my proposal is that Raz was tracking something real and locating it in the wrong place. Exclusionary reasons are best understood not as items in an individual's practical reasoning but as jurisdictional facts: specifications of what an office may consider, which institutions implement by controlling what reaches the officeholder. Two corollaries follow, both of which cut against Raz's own framing.

First, the justification of exclusion cannot in general be the service conception. The normal justification thesis vindicates exclusion by showing that the subject conforms *better* to the underlying reasons by excluding. But designed ignorance often makes the decision-maker conform *worse* to the balance of first-order considerations bearing on the question before her; that is the content of the probative-exclusion problem. A justification of exclusion that is independent of reliability is therefore needed, and EN supplies one: exclusion can be warranted because the subject of the decision holds a claim about what may count, quite apart from whether honouring that claim improves the answer.

Second, the appropriate remedy for the reasoner who *does* possess an excluded consideration is not better discipline but recusal or reconstitution — the standard legal response. If exclusion were psychological, discipline would suffice. That it does not is a datum in favour of the architectural reading.

## 6. Costs, conditions, and a test case

Designed ignorance is not free. Suppressing probative information lowers expected accuracy, and accuracy is not a minor good when what is being allocated is a prison sentence or a kidney. EN is therefore not lexically prior to everything else; it is one consideration among others, and its application needs conditions. I suggest four.

1. *Status conferral.* The decision must confer a normative status on identified persons — liability, desert, entitlement, office — rather than merely produce a prediction or a forecast.
2. *Insulation entitlement.* There must be an independent case that the affected party is entitled not to be judged on the ground in question: typically because the ground concerns who she is rather than what she did, or because it is a feature for which she cannot answer.
3. *Robust unavailability.* Removing the information must actually remove the capacity. Where the excluded feature can be reconstructed from what remains, ignorance is theatre.
4. *Third-party constraint.* The accuracy sacrificed must not be owed to others. We do not blind the surgeon to the patient's allergies; the patient's claim not to be judged by irrelevancies does not extend to a claim to be operated on in the dark.

Condition 3 deserves emphasis, because it yields a result the account was not built to produce. In algorithmic decision-making it is now a commonplace that removing a protected attribute from the inputs does not remove its influence, because correlated features reconstruct it. Dwork and colleagues list, among the practices their framework is designed to interdict, "discrimination based on redundant encodings of membership in the protected set" (*Fairness Through Awareness*, arXiv:1104.3913v2, Introduction, p. 2; Appendix A). This is normally presented as a statistical discovery about high-dimensional data. On the present account it is not merely that. What EN requires is robust unavailability, and where proxies restore availability the requirement is simply unmet; the arrangement fails on its own terms, whatever the measured disparity turns out to be. Unawareness is not an approximation of the ideal that falls short empirically. It is a misidentification of the ideal — which is why the corrective is *awareness* plus a constraint on the mapping, another implementation of the same modal demand.

Condition 1 yields a useful test. Blind analysis in the sciences satisfies neither 1 nor 2: no party holds a claim against the analyst, since the electron is not wronged by being measured in the light. The account therefore predicts that scientific blinding should be governed by purely instrumental considerations — adopted where it improves reliability, abandoned where it does not, lifted once analytic choices are locked in, and owed to no one in particular. That is, so far as I can tell, exactly the shape of the practice. By contrast, the account predicts that adjudicative blinding should be non-negotiable in a way that outruns its measured effects, should survive proof of an individual decision-maker's reliability, and should generate a personal complaint when withheld. That too is the shape of the practice. A theory that draws this line in the right place has earned some credit.

Finally, the account issues a practical recommendation. Where ignorance is merely enjoined rather than engineered — the marker who could look up the name but is told not to — EN is not satisfied, since the capacity remains. The right response is not exhortation but design: unblinding should require a recorded, reviewable act, which converts a private capacity into a public one and restores the party's standing to contest it.

## 7. Objections

**That ignorance cannot be a good.** The account does not say that ignorance is good. It says that a certain relational property is required, and that unavailability is currently the best way to secure it. If some technology made non-use verifiable and irrevocable — a decision procedure whose insensitivity to C could be publicly certified — it would satisfy EN equally well, and blinding would lose its point. The account is not fetishistic about ignorance; it is instrumental about ignorance and non-instrumental about the relation ignorance secures.

**That EN proves too much.** Every decision-maker has capacities to misuse information; if EN were general, all judgment would be domination. But EN is triggered only by insulation entitlements, which are limited and independently argued for, and it is constrained by feasibility and by duties to third parties. Residual discretion is real and is governed by other devices — reason-giving, appeal, review — which, I would add, are recognizably further implementations of the same modal thought. Unavailability, answerability and reversibility are three ways of ensuring that a party's standing does not rest on an official's good will.

**That EN is the appearance theory in modal clothing.** It is not, and the two come apart in both directions. EN is satisfied by a well-designed procedure nobody observes, which the appearance theory cannot credit; and it is violated by an undetected conflict of interest, which the appearance theory must acquit. *Sussex Justices* is a case in which the capacity was real and the influence nil, and the court quashed. EN explains that; a theory about how things look can only explain it by treating the applicant's grievance as a grievance about publicity, which it plainly was not.

**That EN makes the decision-maker's virtue idle.** It does not. Virtue is required for everything the design cannot reach, which is most things. EN says only that a party's entitlement should not *depend* on it, where dependence can be removed at acceptable cost. Constitutional constraints on executive power do not make presidential integrity otiose; they make it unnecessary for a particular and important purpose.

**That the value of information tells against all this.** I. J. Good's result on total evidence is often glossed as showing that free evidence is never harmful. Whatever its exact scope, it does not speak against designed ignorance, and it is worth being clear why not. The theorem evaluates information by its effect on the expected value of the decision-maker's own choice, with her values held fixed; its quantifiers range over acts and states. The good EN identifies is not a property of the act chosen. It is a property of the *procedure's modal profile* and of the distribution of authority it embodies, and this is invisible to any function defined over act–state pairs. The theorem is not refuted by the cases; it is silent about them. Anyone who moves from the value of evidence to a critique of exclusionary rules of evidence — as some do — owes an argument that the good at stake is the one the theorem measures.

## 8. Conclusion

I have defended three connected claims. First, that the statuses conferred by institutional judgment — verdicts, grades, appointments, awards — are individuated in part by their grounds, so that an office has a deliberative jurisdiction and a decision reached outside it is not a defective instance of the same act but a different act. Second, that those subject to such judgments hold a modal entitlement: not merely that the judgment not rest on excluded grounds, but that the decision-maker not be in a position to rest it on them. Third, that designed ignorance is how this entitlement is implemented, and that it is therefore best understood as the externalization of exclusionary reasons — a relocation of authority over what may count, from the officeholder's will to a public and contestable design.

Two consequences are worth flagging. Blinding regimes should be evaluated by their modal robustness rather than by their measured bias reduction; a regime that lowers measured disparity while leaving the excluded feature reconstructible has not done the thing it was for. And the growing use of automated and semi-automated decision procedures should be assessed in the same currency. The interesting question about such a system is not only whether it is accurate or whether its outputs are statistically even-handed, but whether those subject to it are in a position to say that it *could not* have been otherwise on grounds that are none of its business. That is a question about what the system is in a position to do, and it is the question that ignorance, when it is well designed, is meant to answer.
