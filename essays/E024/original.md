# A True Answer Is Not Always the End

An archivist wants to know whether an expedition replied to every letter it received. She checks its register. Each listed letter has a reply attached. Then she finds a note explaining that some letters arrived while the clerk was away and were never entered in the register. She does not know whether those letters received replies.

The archivist now knows the answer to a precise question: did every registered letter receive a reply? Yes. Her evidence for that answer is excellent. Yet she cannot reasonably regard her original investigation as successfully finished. The problem is not that her answer might be false. It is that she has learned something which breaks the connection between that answer and what she was trying to find out.

This distinction matters for how we judge inquiry. We often make a large question manageable by asking a smaller one. We inspect a sample, consult a record, choose a measure, or test one proposed explanation. These choices can be sensible. But the investigation can reveal that the smaller question no longer does the work for which we chose it. We can understand the failure through familiar rules about evidence. The challenge is to keep it separate from failures in how we searched or when we stopped.

I defend a way of assessing inquiry that keeps three judgments separate: the search was reasonable, stopping was reasonable, and the original question was answered. The first two can be true while the third is false. When evidence shows that a smaller answer falls short of the accuracy or scope reasonably required by the original question, that answer alone is not enough to claim completion. Other evidence may close the gap. A limitation too small to affect the required result may not create a gap at all.

This is not a proposed new source of epistemic obligation. It is a diagnosis of how a sound method and a sensible stop can conceal an unsupported claim of achievement. Its value lies in identifying what must be checked before crediting a project with answering the question that started it.

## The question we ask and the task we give it

Some questions are pursued for their own sake. I might wonder how many windows a building has simply because I want to know. If I count them reliably, my inquiry may be over. I need no further purpose to justify it.

Other questions have an assigned job. The archivist asks about entries in the register because she expects them to tell her about the expedition's correspondence. The narrower question is a way of pursuing the wider one. This relation does not have to appear in a written plan. It can be clear from what the person says, which evidence she gathers, and what would satisfy her.

The archivist relies on an assumption: learning about the registered letters will tell her about all the letters. That assumption is different from a presupposition of the narrow question itself. A question's presupposition is something its asking takes for granted, such as there having been a theft when we ask who committed it. The archivist's narrower question can have a perfectly sound presupposition and a fully known answer. What fails is the expectation that the register covers the letters she cares about.

This is why the case does not reduce to the familiar advice to correct false premises. No false answer need be accepted. The inquirer might even reject the faulty assumption in words while continuing to treat her project as complete. “Of course the register misses some letters,” she says, “but I have answered the question in my research notes.” She has corrected a belief without correcting her assessment of the inquiry.

Existing work helps locate the problem. Jane Friedman connects inquiry with attitudes directed toward questions and explains how belief settles a question. She also explicitly distinguishes answering a question from merely dropping it. I accept those distinctions. The narrower concern here is how success in a subtask can lead someone to misclassify the result of the larger project. [Friedman 2019, §§2, 4–5.](https://jfriedmanphilo.github.io/IB.pdf)

Arianna Falbo argues that inquiry can be proper even when a question has a false presupposition. Learning through inquiry includes correcting mistakes, not only acquiring an answer. Her proposed norm gives a necessary condition for permissible inquiry in terms of possible epistemic improvement. The present question comes later: after an improvement has occurred, what entitles us to treat the task as done? A person can learn something valuable without achieving the particular result sought. [Falbo 2024, §§1, 3.1, 5.](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.13089)

Nor is it new to treat inquiry as a process whose parts have different roles. Benoit Guilielmo gives an account in which questioning attitudes both initiate inquiry and guide its actions. My concern is one norm within such a process: how discoveries about a question's assigned role should affect the assessment of completion. [Guilielmo 2024, §§3–4.1.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S001221732400012X)

The proposed contribution is a focused account of that misclassification. The knowledge gained can remain intact while its ability to answer the original question fails. The assessment must follow the particular inference from the small result to the larger conclusion. It cannot depend simply on whether the source is complete or the work was worth doing.

## What a claim of completion requires

Consider two people reading the same register. The first is studying the clerk's filing habits. She wants to know whether every entry has a reply attached. The second is the archivist studying the expedition's correspondence. Both inspect the same entries. Both learn that every entry has a reply. Both read the note about letters that were never registered.

The first can finish successfully. The unregistered letters are outside the task she undertook. The second cannot finish on that basis. The note bears directly on whether the register can answer her question about all letters.

This difference is not a difference in the quality of their knowledge about the register. It comes from the different work that this knowledge was meant to do. We should therefore assess the completion of an inquiry partly by its relation to the issue pursued, rather than by the final answer considered alone.

The reasoning has three steps.

First, a claim of successful completion is a claim of achievement. It says that the relevant task has been accomplished, not merely that some effort has ended. We can dispute what a person achieved while fully agreeing that she has good reason to stop. An exhausted climber may rightly turn back without reaching the summit. An archivist may rightly close her notebook without discovering whether all letters received replies.

Second, success in a part establishes success in a whole only when the part does enough of the required work. In the original case, inferring that every letter was answered requires a basis for extending the result beyond the registered letters. The connection is not automatic. It is something the inquirer relies on.

Third, evidence that exposes a material gap in that basis blocks this inference from the smaller achievement to the larger one. The note does not undermine the count. It undermines what the archivist can conclude from the count. If she treats the original question as settled anyway, she ignores evidence relevant to that assessment.

The point concerns what she has grounds to claim. The missing letters might all have received replies, even though she does not know that. Conversely, she might know from an independent source that all letters were answered. Then she may regard the wider issue as settled, although the register alone did not settle it. We must distinguish whether she knows the answer from whether this particular investigation established it.

She can deal with the note in several ways. She could discover that it refers to a different year. She could find a second archive covering the missing period. Or she could show that the omission cannot affect the result to the degree of accuracy her inquiry requires. Refuting the note is therefore not the only way forward. Alternatively, she can report the answer about registered letters while acknowledging that the other letters remain unresolved.

An inquirer need not state all this in philosophical terms. She can simply say, “This tells me about the register, but it does not yet tell me about those other letters.” That ordinary recognition is enough.

## When an incomplete source is enough

The omission does not block every answer to the original question. Suppose the archivist finds a registered letter and reliable evidence that it never received a reply. Merely failing to find an attached reply would not suffice; suppose a trustworthy contemporary account confirms that the expedition left this letter unanswered. She can now answer the original question negatively: the expedition did not reply to every letter. The unregistered letters cannot undo that counterexample.

The source has the same coverage defect as before. Yet the defect no longer prevents the relevant conclusion. One unanswered letter is enough for the negative answer; evidence that all registered letters were answered was not enough for the positive answer. The difference lies in the inference, not in the number of missing records.

Now change the task. The archivist wants an estimate of the proportion of letters answered, accurate within five percentage points. Suppose she has reliable grounds for thinking that omitted letters make up at most one in ten thousand of all letters received. If the recorded replies are otherwise established, even the worst case about the omitted letters leaves the estimate well within the required margin. She need not discover how those letters were treated. Knowing the bound is enough for this limited task.

These contrasts explain what makes a doubt material without defining it as whatever defeats completion. Identify the answer being claimed, the accuracy reasonably sought, and the particular inference from the evidence. Then ask how the supported limitation could change that inference or the result. Missing letters can overturn a positive claim about every letter. They cannot reverse a confirmed counterexample. Their known maximum share can also be too small to spoil an estimate.

There is no requirement to exclude every imaginable possibility. The note gives a concrete basis for concern in the original case. Bare speculation that archives might be unreliable does not by itself put every historical inquiry back at its beginning. Nor does every supported limitation matter equally. Reasonable standards of evidence remain necessary; this account does not replace them with a mechanical test.

There is also no general duty here to rescue an inquiry once its original route fails. The missing letters may be impossible to trace. The archivist may have another assignment of greater value. She may simply lose interest. In all these cases she can stop. What she cannot gain from a shortage of time, money, or interest is a new evidential connection between the registered and the missing letters.

It can be entirely rational to decide, “I have learned enough to justify spending no more time on this.” That judgment concerns the value of further work. “I have found out whether every letter was answered” concerns what the work established. One may be warranted when the other is not.

This separation also prevents a familiar pressure from distorting inquiry. Institutions often need projects to end on schedule. If stopping and succeeding are treated as the same status, a deadline supplies an incentive to redraw the question until the evidence counts as a full answer. The rule allows a more accurate result: the project ended, this part succeeded, and this part remains open. A deadline need not be denied, and a gap need not be disguised.

## What ordinary epistemology already explains

A strong objection says that this is simply ordinary reasoning about evidence. The target is whether all letters received replies. The register supplies evidence. The note reveals a limit on that evidence. Therefore the archivist lacks adequate grounds for the positive answer. Nothing here requires a special norm of inquiry.

I agree. A careful account that evaluates evidence relative to the actual question can explain the case. The paired researchers do not refute it: they have different targets, so it predicts their different verdicts. The present account makes this familiar requirement explicit where a manageable subquestion has begun to stand in for its target.

Why bother making it explicit? Because a project admits several assessments that can point in different directions. Suppose consulting the register was the best available method. Discovering the omitted letters was a useful result of that method. Suppose, further, that no affordable route to the missing correspondence exists. The search was reasonable, and stopping is reasonable. Neither judgment answers whether the original issue was resolved. Praising the method or approving the stop cannot supply missing evidence about the letters.

This classification helps locate the error without condemning the whole investigation. A critic who says only “the research failed” may wrongly suggest carelessness or wasted effort. A defender who says “the researcher did everything she reasonably could” may wrongly suggest that she answered the question. Both overlook a possible combination: sound work, justified stopping, and incomplete achievement.

The omission matters here because of the task that the narrow question was serving. Countless other unanswered questions about the expedition do not matter. Identifying the target and the inference explains which gap remains. This remains true whether we describe the project as one inquiry with stages or as several connected inquiries. No controversial account of their identity is needed.

## Is this just instrumental rationality?

Rational people revise their means when those means stop serving their ends. If the register no longer answers the larger question, should the archivist not simply use another source or abandon the search?

Daniel C. Friedman connects inquiry with intentions and plans, and grounds its instrumental pressures in ordinary plan rationality. This is a direct reason not to portray the connection between inquiry and means as neglected. His account also allows a difference between an intended stopping attitude and an epistemically appropriate one. [D. C. Friedman 2024, §§2.1–2.2, 6.](https://philpapers.org/archive/FRIIAI-8.pdf)

The rule does draw on a general thought about means and ends. It would be misleading to claim discovery of that thought. But the present rule is not simply a command to choose more effective means.

A rule about effective means would normally tell the archivist what to do if she keeps pursuing the end. This rule also governs what she may conclude about the achievement when she stops pursuing it. She may abandon the end entirely and still be wrong to think she achieved it. Even after she gives up the search, she can still be mistaken about what she found out.

Further, the defect need not involve any inefficient action. Suppose the register was the best available source and searching it was the only reasonable first step. After the note appears, no affordable further search is possible. The archivist's sequence of actions can be beyond reasonable criticism. The error begins only when she counts the original issue as resolved.

This gives the rule a distinct use. It separates criticism of an inquirer's conduct from criticism of her assessment of what that conduct established. We can praise her method, accept her decision to stop, and reject her claim of completion. Ordinary epistemic norms together with an account of goals can explain this distinction. The point is to use them to keep retrospective achievement separate from prospective advice about how to proceed.

The rule is epistemic in a straightforward sense: it asks the inquirer to respect evidence about the reach of what she has learned. Its source need not be unique to epistemology for its application to clarify an epistemic failure.

## Can the inquirer change her mind about the task?

Of course she can. Curiosity is not a contract requiring someone to retain the same interests forever. The archivist may discover that the registered correspondence is more interesting than the full collection. She may decide to write only about it.

The rule must not forbid that change. It requires only that changing the task not be mistaken for completing the former task. “I am now studying the register” is compatible with “I did not resolve my question about all letters.” The new project can succeed on its own terms.

This matters even when there is no dishonesty and no audience. A person can quietly lower a target and feel that she has reached it. There need be no intention to deceive. Yet when she later relies on the result, she may remember the broad question and forget the narrower basis. A careful assessment should preserve the distinction before memory or reporting makes it harder to recover.

Not every refinement changes the task in this way. Suppose the archivist discovers that by “every letter” she and her colleagues always meant official letters received during one specified month. Making that scope explicit may merely clarify the existing question. Evidence about their prior purpose can establish this. By contrast, narrowing the scope only because difficult letters have appeared does not show that those letters were irrelevant all along.

There will be borderline cases. An early curiosity may be vague, and there may be no determinate answer to what would have satisfied it. In those cases the right description may be that the task developed during the work. My rule should not manufacture a fixed original target where none existed. The account is clearest when she knows what she originally wanted to find out and why she consulted this source.

This limit is useful. We need not solve every problem about the identity of research questions to identify the archivist's mistake. Her own reason for consulting the register, together with the note about omitted letters, supplies enough structure.

## What the end of inquiry should preserve

A good inquiry can end with an answer, a corrected question, a partial result, or a justified decision to leave the matter alone. These endings are not interchangeable. Calling them all “success” can hide the very information the inquiry produced about its limits.

The archivist's discovery is not a failure of curiosity. It gives her a better picture of the evidence. The failure would be to let a known answer silence that discovery. Respect for inquiry therefore sometimes requires keeping an issue open after the work stops.

The central requirement is modest but consequential: preserve the difference between what has been answered and what that answer was supposed to settle. When the evidence reveals a gap that matters to the conclusion, a claim of completion needs further grounds. Sometimes another source supplies them; sometimes the gap is too small to matter; sometimes the honest result is partial. The reasonableness of ending the work does not decide which of these is true.
