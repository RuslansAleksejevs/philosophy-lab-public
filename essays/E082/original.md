# What a Second Look Leaves Unchecked

You look at a label, read “18,” and look again before recording the number. The second look agrees with the first. Sometimes this is a good reason to become more confident. Sometimes it merely repeats the same mistake. The difference cannot simply be that the second look uses an independent faculty: both looks use vision, and ordinary checking need not stop until we find a faculty wholly independent of perception. Nor can agreement by itself explain the improvement. A display that copies its previous output will agree perfectly with itself.

I argue that perceptual checking has two effects that should be assessed separately. It can reduce the probability that a belief is false while concentrating the remaining probability of error in causes that the check leaves untouched. These effects can occur together. An agreeing second look can therefore make a belief more credible while making the next repetition less useful. If further assurance is sought, the composition of remaining error helps assess which new inquiry would be informative; it does not by itself establish a duty to continue or determine the best procedure. What has been checked is not adequately represented by the proposition believed, the number of agreeing observations, or even their resulting confidence level. We also need to preserve which routes to error those observations discriminate.

This is a proposal about additional perceptual justification, particularly the reflective justification expressed by “I checked.” It is not a general analysis of perceptual knowledge. Its proposed contribution is a norm for communicating the scope of evidential improvement: a report of successful checking should not invite another person to treat a particular error as evidentially answered merely because total confidence increased. The changing composition of residual error explains why that distinction matters after several genuinely useful checks. This is a development of existing work on contrastive checking and error-sensitive confirmation, not a new general solution to bootstrapping.

## 1. The problem is not whether checking has value

Perception may justify a belief before a perceiver has established the reliability of her senses. Pryor's dogmatism makes this possibility explicit: an experience can provide defeasible justification without antecedent justification supplied by further beliefs (Pryor 2000, p. 519). One need not accept that account to see the present problem. Whatever justifies the first belief, what does the second look add?

Existing discussions already supply several important constraints. Woodard argues that double-checking can rationally seek epistemic improvements even when knowledge is already present, including greater justification and sensitivity to error (2024, §3). Melchior distinguishes checking against one alternative from checking against another, with different sensitivity conditions, and separates ordinary from reflective checking (2023, chapter 3–4 overviews). Fernández-Vargas's stated independence proposal diagnoses bootstrapping in terms of a failure to check a source against an independent source (2022, abstract). The ideas that checking can improve an epistemic position, that error discrimination matters, and that independence is relevant are therefore established starting points, not discoveries of this essay.

Osimani and Landes likewise show how error models affect the value of repeated and varied evidence, allowing evidence to inform both a hypothesis and its source reliability (2022, Worked Example and Conclusions). Thus neither error-sensitive confirmation nor joint learning about sources is new here. The narrower issue is what assurances one may communicate on the strength of a particular result.

The following account concerns graded evidential improvement, not a necessary and sufficient condition for successfully checking. A procedure can have a favorable likelihood ratio even if it would mislead in the nearest world where an alternative holds. Probabilistic discrimination is therefore not being equated with Melchior’s modal sensitivity. The question is what a result adds to warranted confidence, whether or not it qualifies as successful checking under a stricter modal account.

An important question remains once those points are granted. A check seldom confronts falsity as an undifferentiated possibility. Looking more carefully may expose an inattentive misreading but preserve a distortion caused by the lens through which one looks. Two observers can correct each other's fleeting inattention while sharing the same misleading illumination. Different faculties can share a misleading object: a convincingly constructed counterfeit may fool both sight and touch. Calling observations independent or dependent without specifying the error in question hides what they accomplish.

A useful assessment should therefore retain a distinction between the reduction of total error and the redistribution of error among its possible causes. This distinction is especially important when someone proceeds from “it survived checking” to “my way of finding out has been vindicated.”

## 2. A case of selective success

Consider a reader who has just seen “18” on a label. Let p be the proposition that the label says “18.” She plans a second deliberate inspection under the same viewing conditions. Suppose, for illustration, that the possibilities remaining after her first look fall into three classes:

* The first reading is correct.
* The first reading is wrong because of a transient lapse that a second inspection is likely to correct.
* The first reading is wrong because of a persistent distortion shared by both inspections.

These are stipulated epistemic possibilities, not empirical claims about actual rates of visual error. Assume the reader has warranted credences of 0.90, 0.08, and 0.02 in these classes after the first look, and warranted conditional expectations about the procedure. This normative stipulation matters: coherent arithmetic applied to unwarranted inputs would not establish improved justification. The classes are exclusive and exhaustive within the example. Any combination of a lapse and a persistent distortion is assigned to the persistent class if the distortion sustains the false reading across inspections.

Suppose the probability that the second inspection again yields “18” is 0.99 when the first reading is correct, 0.10 when the first reading arose from a transient lapse, and 0.99 when the persistent distortion is present. All these probabilities are conditional on the initial appearance and on the specified procedure. We are not multiplying probabilities as though the two inspections were unconditionally independent.

When the second inspection agrees, the unnormalized weights become 0.891 for correctness, 0.008 for transient error, and 0.0198 for persistent error. The total is 0.9188. The probability that p is true consequently rises from 90 percent to approximately 96.97 percent. This is a substantial epistemic improvement.

But among the remaining error possibilities, the share assigned to persistent distortion rises from 20 percent to approximately 71.22 percent. Most of the remaining danger is now the kind this check does not distinguish from correctness. Moreover, the posterior probability of persistent distortion itself rises slightly, from 2 percent to approximately 2.15 percent. It has not become more probable relative to correctness; both received the same likelihood multiplier. It becomes more probable in the whole distribution because agreement selectively removes transient-error possibilities.

There is no paradox. Agreement is good evidence against an error that would probably produce disagreement. It is no evidence favoring correctness over an error that would probably preserve agreement to the same extent. The probability of p can rise because one competitor loses support, while another competitor retains its odds against p.

This pattern is general. Let T denote correctness, E an error exposed by a check, U an error the check tends to preserve, and C an agreeing result. Conditional on the first observation and background information, the posterior odds of U to E equal their prior odds multiplied by Pr(C|U)/Pr(C|E). Whenever that ratio exceeds one, agreement concentrates the residual error in U. Meanwhile p gains probability whenever C is more likely under T than under the prior mixture of E and U. Both inequalities can hold together, as the example shows.

The calculation is elementary. Its philosophical significance lies in the fact that a check can improve our overall justification without improving our position against the error that increasingly dominates what remains uncertain. “More justified” does not entail “better checked against every still-live source of error.”

## 3. Why the distinction changes the next inquiry

Imagine two readers whose beliefs both reach 97 percent confidence after checking. One has mainly excluded transient misreading; almost all her remaining doubt concerns an optical distortion. The other has varied viewing conditions and mainly excluded distortion; her remaining doubt concerns a briefly obscured character. They can have the same confidence in the same proposition while having different epistemic reasons to choose their next check.

For the first, another look through the same apparatus may do little. An inspection without that apparatus is more promising. For the second, a prolonged inspection under the present conditions may be the useful intervention. Neither the number of preceding checks nor the scalar probability of truth settles this difference. The error possibilities left open determine what new information would matter.

The point is not that persistent errors are always more important than transient ones. A persistent error can be exceedingly improbable. Nor is changing methods intrinsically valuable. A new method can be noisier, can introduce fresh systematic errors, or can depend on the same misleading feature in a less obvious way. What matters is how the proposed method separates correctness from the errors still carrying appreciable weight.

The numerical case also explains diminishing returns without appealing to boredom, cost, or irrational obsession. If repeated inspections have the same conditional profile and, within each of the three classes, their outcomes are conditionally independent, each agreeing result further suppresses E. But it never alters the odds of T to U. Even arbitrarily many agreements leave the limiting probability of correctness at 0.90 divided by 0.92, approximately 97.83 percent. This ceiling follows from the stipulated model, not from a universal limit on perception. Its lesson is structural: repeating an intervention that cannot distinguish two possibilities does not eventually distinguish them by accumulation.

The conditional-independence assumption matters here. If later inspections reveal genuinely new features, or if a persistent distortion predictably changes over time, repetition may acquire further discriminatory power. The demand is not to change methods for its own sake. It is to identify what makes further results informative rather than silently borrowing the evidential value of independent trials.

## 4. The scope of “I checked”

We often use “I checked” to give another person a reason to rely on our inquiry. But three reports must be distinguished: “I performed a test for distortion”; “this result favors correctness over distortion”; and “distortion is now sufficiently improbable.” The first concerns a procedure, the second a change in relative evidential support, and the third an eventual level of assurance. None entails the next without further information.

Suppose a test has three possible outcomes, A, B, and C. Their probabilities under correctness are .4, .3, and .3; under distortion they are .4, .5, and .1. The procedure is potentially informative about the contrast, but an observed A does not alter the odds. It is true that the agent tested for distortion, false that A favors correctness over distortion. An observed B would instead favor distortion, yet it would remain a result of a real test. Furthermore, even a favorable C need not make distortion sufficiently improbable if its prior probability was high. Conversely, distortion may already be sufficiently improbable before any new test.

This separation motivates a norm of evidential reporting: when a checking report is offered as additional assurance against an identified error, its implied assurance should not exceed what the observed result supports against that error, given the earlier evidence. A report of the procedure alone is no substitute for that support.

The norm rests on the report’s justificatory role. In asking “does the distortion concern remain?”, an interlocutor requests a reason to change her assessment of that concern. A reply that invites her to lower its odds while supplying only evidence that leaves those odds unchanged misrepresents the reason available. Even if every sentence is literally true—there was a test, the readings agreed, overall confidence rose—the implied evidential answer can be false. The fault lies in moving between contrasts without making that movement clear.

For the label reader, “I inspected it again carefully” can answer a worry about a hasty glance. Offered as assurance against lens distortion, it invites an unsupported inference if the second appearance is equally likely under distortion and correctness. A suitable report is instead: “The careful rereading reduced the chance of a lapse; it did not discriminate the lens-distortion possibility.” This does not demand that every report list every conceivable error. It requires preserving a live contrast when the report is offered to answer it.

Nor must every perceiver formulate this norm. A child can gain perceptual justification without a model of correlated error. The requirement applies when the check is invoked as a reflective reason for dismissing a concern or conveying stronger assurance. Here “epistemic authority” means precisely what another person is entitled to infer from the report, not a special faculty or an infallible status.

The error categories must not be invented retrospectively merely to protect the favored belief. A proposed class needs some account of why its members respond similarly to the procedure: the same lens is retained, attention is refreshed, illumination is changed, or an independent measurement pathway is introduced. Causal explanations need not be complete, but they should make the expected contrast intelligible. Where such information is unavailable, a perceiver may be unable to determine how much the agreement adds. That is a real epistemic limitation, not a reason to assume independence by default.

There is a further consequence. A later warning about a shared error source can rationally undermine several checks together. If the reader learns that the lens used on every inspection distorts precisely this kind of character, the earlier agreements are not a large stack of separate answers to the warning. They all lie on the same side of the untested contrast. Confidence earned by ruling out fleeting lapses need not protect the belief against evidence of a stable distortion.

This does not mean the original checking was worthless. It performed its legitimate local task. The mistake would be to convert that task's success into unrestricted assurance and then treat later criticism as though it had to overturn each agreement separately.

## 5. Bootstrapping as an overextension of checking

Vogel's gauge case illustrates an invalid route from trusting a source's readings to certifying the source's reliability; comparison with a dipstick supplies the contrasting legitimate procedure (2008, pp. 518–519). Weisberg's No Feedback approach blocks illicit amplification through intermediate conclusions while allowing informative comparisons among perceptual faculties (2010, §§4.1–4.2). The present account is compatible with that diagnosis. Its distinctive concern is how a new observation can be informative and nevertheless leave the relevant reliability contrast unchanged.

Suppose the reader concludes after the first look that p, and then observes that her first look represented p. From these she records that vision succeeded. If the assessment of p simply inherits the first appearance, the apparent success is not a fresh result capable of discriminating correctness from the relevant error. Repeating the maneuver across many labels merely records agreement between an accepted output and the same output redescribed as a fact about the world.

Actual reinspection changes the situation. The second observation can provide new information. That is why treating every use of perception to assess perception as illegitimate would be excessive. Yet actual novelty of observation is insufficient for unrestricted reliability confirmation. If all the new observations preserve the same distortion, they can expose lapses while leaving distortion versus correctness untested. Moving from “these readings survived a lapse-sensitive test” to “the apparatus is free from distortion” exceeds the scope of the evidence.

This yields an intermediate verdict that crude classifications obscure. Some procedures are neither wholly empty self-certification nor adequate tests of the whole source. They are informative checks of a limited range of failures. Their informative results deserve credit against the failures they disfavor; simply having performed them does not establish that every outcome supplies that credit.

Consequently, the proposal does not require antecedent proof that perception is globally reliable. It requires that additional reflective confidence not be credited to discriminatory work a procedure does not do. Local beliefs about how lighting, lenses, attention, or instruments operate may themselves arise through perception. Their provenance does not automatically render every subsequent check empty. Neither must the source model remain fixed. If an agent has warranted uncertainty among alternative models, observations can jointly change her confidence in p and in those models. For example, evidence that is much less expected on a random-output model can lower that model’s weight without an external calibration on that occasion. Such learning must use the observations once within their joint evidential structure; an inferred p cannot then be added as an independent observation validating those same observations. This narrower restriction follows the No Feedback concern rather than prohibiting ordinary learning about sources.

The fixed-profile example establishes no general impossibility of perceptual self-assessment. It shows that where correctness and a particular error predict the same result, that result does not alter their odds. With uncertain models, the relevant likelihoods may instead be weighted mixtures, and those weights may change. The evidential reporting norm survives: the assurance offered must reflect what the evidence actually supports under the warranted model uncertainty, rather than a convenient assumption of a previously vindicated source.

## 6. Objections and limits

An objection is that the entire account merely restates Bayesian conditionalization. The numerical relationship certainly follows from ordinary conditionalization, and no new mathematical principle is proposed. But the philosophical conclusion does not follow from reporting posterior confidence alone. It concerns what a person is entitled to claim has been tested, why equally confident beliefs can require different investigations, and why the same check can be a relevant answer to one criticism but no answer to another. The probability model exposes the distinction; the reporting argument explains why it constrains an assurance given in answer to an identified concern. This application is modest, and exact priority remains open given the extensive literatures on checking and Bayesian source assessment.

A second objection is that the reporting norm smuggles in demanding higher-order knowledge. To know whether a check discriminates, must one first check the check, and then check that checking procedure? The account does not impose this hierarchy on the initial justification of p. It distinguishes possessing perceptual justification from having a particular reflective reason for increasing confidence. Such reasons often depend on modest background understanding. A person who has no warranted basis for thinking that a procedure supplies a new test cannot obtain that basis simply by observing agreement. This limitation is compatible with her having justification from the appearances themselves. Nor need every background belief be supported by another completed check; requiring that would reintroduce the global demand the account deliberately leaves open.

A third objection concerns undetected possibilities. The model's categories omit errors the subject has never considered. Therefore even its careful description might encourage false assurance. That is correct if the model is treated as exhaustive knowledge about real perception. It should instead be understood as an explicit representation of a subject's warranted understanding, open to revision. The norm prohibits extending checking credit beyond established discrimination; it does not certify that the established error partition includes every possible failure. Discovering an omitted common cause is a reason to reopen the assessment.

Finally, does residual concentration determine the next inquiry? No. In the example, total risk decreases, and an agent can rationally stop. The greater residual share of U makes it increasingly necessary to include U when evaluating further assurance. But selecting a procedure also requires its possible outcomes and likelihoods, the epistemic objective, costs, and available alternatives. A high share alone does not establish greater expected information gain from a particular test. The epistemic point is about where remaining uncertainty lies and what can reduce it, not an unconditional duty to continue looking.

An agreeing second look can deserve additional confidence because it makes a kind of mistake less probable. It need not establish that looking is trustworthy in every relevant respect. As checks succeed, they reshape the uncertainty they leave behind. An adequate epistemology of perceptual checking must preserve that shape alongside the degree of belief. Otherwise a series of genuine but selective improvements can be mistaken for a comprehensive vindication of the very mechanism that has never been tested.
