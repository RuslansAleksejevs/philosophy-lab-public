# Who May Say the Review Is Finished?

A transit agency plans to move a bus stop. The route team checks whether buses can turn safely. The scheduling team checks journey times. The finance team checks the cost. During the review, a driver reports that passengers from a nearby supported-housing building will have to cross a busy road to reach the new stop. The pedestrian route was missing from the map used to prepare the proposal. The report is specific, plausible and potentially serious. None of the three teams is assigned to assess pedestrian access.

Everyone completes their allotted work. Every report is accurate. Under the agency’s rules, the teams’ completions jointly authorize implementation without another decision. Each completion therefore does two jobs: it records the end of specialist work and supplies part of the clearance to proceed. The last employee knows about the driver’s report. She has no expertise in pedestrian safety and no authority to choose the site. But she can report that her specialist work is finished while withholding or qualifying its contribution to the wider clearance. She instead supplies an unqualified completion. The stop is moved.

Suppose everyone knows that pedestrian access remains unassessed. The agency accepts completion of the assigned checks as sufficient for implementation even in this case. Nobody mistakes the review for a comprehensive investigation. The agency has a bad policy for dealing with the disclosed uncertainty, and the employee knowingly supplies an authorization under that policy. Complete transparency would not correct this failure. The relevant beliefs are already accurate.

I will defend a limited duty concerning this employee’s act. Someone who knowingly supplies an organizational authorization must exercise reasonable care concerning a serious objection which she knows its supporting review leaves untreated. Where reasonably available, she must qualify or withhold her authorization, or obtain reasonable grounds for treating the outstanding issue as appropriately addressed. Her specialist remit may explain why she cannot answer the objection. It does not, by itself, justify granting clearance across the gap. This duty concerns what she authorizes; it does not make her the pedestrian-safety expert, the author of the agency’s entire action, or the guarantor of a good outcome.

The problem belongs to familiar territory. Dennis Thompson describes the difficulty of locating personal responsibility when many officials contribute to public decisions, while preserving the importance of causation, ignorance and compulsion. Helen Nissenbaum argues that complicated causal and decision structures can obscure accountability without eliminating it. One way to make progress is to identify an act that a narrow job description leaves out. In the case before us, the employee reports completed work and also helps authorize implementation. Those are distinct aspects of what she does. [Thompson, p. 11](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/38306/excerpt/9780521838306_excerpt.pdf); [Nissenbaum, “Many Hands”](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/accountability.pdf).

Three things must be kept separate. First, there is a moral requirement to deal appropriately with a credible, serious reason against imposing a risk on others. Second, there is institutional clearance: the agency’s rules now allow its staff to implement the plan. Third, there is the employee’s responsibility for supplying that clearance. Her completion changes the second. It cannot abolish the first. Whether it violates her own duty depends on the third.

The moral requirement is limited. Not every anxiety demands an investigation. The objection must identify a credible prospect of serious harm or wrongdoing, bear on the proposed action, and be sufficiently supported by the evidence reasonably available that responsible decision makers need to assess it or justify proceeding without settling it. An unexplained feeling that a timetable is unlucky does not meet that standard. Repetition of an adequately answered objection does not automatically reopen a review. And a justified policy for handling minor or familiar uncertainties may make fresh investigation unnecessary.

The driver’s report is stipulated to meet the threshold. That stipulation does not assume the crossing is unsafe. One can reasonably recognize that an omitted pedestrian route needs attention without knowing what an expert will conclude about it. Recognition of a need for an answer can require less knowledge than possession of the answer. Where even the preliminary recognition is reasonably beyond an employee’s competence, the case for holding her responsible changes.

Why does this requirement survive the division of work? Consider a single planner who receives the report. She cannot establish that moving the stop is justified merely by making a list of different questions, answering those, and pointing out that she has finished her list. She might justify acting without further inquiry, perhaps because delay presents a greater danger. But that would require a reason bearing on the decision, not just a fact about the list.

Dividing her work among three people has considerable advantages. It makes expertise available and tasks manageable. But the assignment chart does not alter the passengers’ exposure or the significance of the report. The organization still needs an adequate basis for proceeding. The chart may help identify who should provide it; an omission from the chart cannot provide it instead.

This establishes a continuing moral demand on the undertaking. It does not yet establish a special duty for our employee. A passerby might also be able to help get the report assessed. The further connection is her exercise of authorizing power.

The relevant premise is a principle of reasonable care: when a person knowingly makes an authorization to impose a serious risk available for others to act on, she must respond proportionately to known, credible reasons against issuing it. She need not investigate every possible objection herself. But she cannot defend ignoring a recognized defect in the authorization’s basis solely by pointing to a narrower assignment. The point of care here is to regulate a power she is exercising, rather than to assign her an unclaimed task simply because someone must perform it.

An ordinary comparison helps defend that premise. Someone entrusted with releasing a locked gate should not release it for a vehicle she knows has an unresolved, serious safety warning simply because checking the warning was omitted from her written duties. She might lack the skill to assess it. She can nevertheless recognize that she lacks grounds for this release and seek an appropriate decision. Organizational authorization has a similar structure: it makes a course of action available to others without requiring another exercise of judgment from them. Its moral importance does not depend on moving a physical barrier.

This comparison does not show that authorization carries a unique fundamental moral duty. The same concern for those exposed to risk also grounds duties governing material assistance and the provision of information. What changes with the authorizing act is the application. Our employee supplies a basis on which others are entitled, under the agency’s rules, to implement. Merely ensuring that everyone knows about the gap does not correct her unqualified authorization, since everyone already knows. She must attend to what she licenses, as well as what she tells them.

Compare a technician who supplies an accurate measurement to a manager. The measurement informs the manager’s decision; it does not itself authorize implementation. Even if the technician has an equally easy opportunity to warn, and therefore an equally strong duty to do so, she has no authorization of her own to qualify. Once she has supplied accurate information and an adequate warning, another person still has to decide whether to proceed. Our employee instead helps supply the clearance that makes a further decision unnecessary. If a supposedly routine measurement entry has that function in the known practice, its name does not make it mere information. Conversely, a document called a “clearance” may in practice be only an advisory report.

The duty therefore applies to a specified kind of contribution, not to the last person near a keyboard. If the system secretly converts data entry into clearance in a way a worker could not reasonably understand, its designers may be responsible while the worker is not. If qualification is impossible, or refusal carries disproportionate costs, the appropriate response and the assessment of blame must reflect those facts. The duty is presumptive and proportionate, not a guarantee that every employee can make the organization behave well.

Consider two versions of the transit agency. In the first, any serious new report formally blocks implementation until it receives a disposition. Completing the review removes that internal block. In the second, serious reports have no such status: the three assigned checks always suffice under the rules. Completing the review activates clearance without ever removing an internal block concerning pedestrian access. The moral requirement remains in both agencies. And where the employee knowingly supplies clearance, recognizes the serious unresolved objection, and has reasonable alternatives, the duty of care applies in both. In the first she helps remove a safeguard; in the second she helps use a procedure that lacks it. The argument does not depend on pretending that the second agency already has the better rule.

What would meeting the duty look like? If an appropriate assessor has adequately dealt with the report, the employee can ordinarily rely on that assessment. She need not reproduce the specialist’s reasoning from the ground up. Alternatively, she can qualify her completion so that it records finished specialist work while leaving implementation dependent on treatment of the access question. Or she can withhold the authorizing contribution while seeking an appropriate transfer. What she cannot do is take completion of unrelated tasks, by itself, as a reason that the known gap no longer constrains her authorization.

A qualification must have an actual bearing on reliance. “My route checks are finished; pedestrian access remains unassessed, and my completion does not clear that issue for implementation” can work where it is understood to limit the employee’s authorization. The same words placed in an invisible comment field may do nothing. A visible disclaimer is also insufficient if the employee knowingly continues to provide the very unqualified clearance she disclaims. The aim is to separate the useful result of her specialist work from the wider permission it cannot yet support.

This explains why transferring a concern differs from transmitting it. Forwarding a report changes who possesses information. A transfer establishes who must address unfinished work and how that work bears on the decision. An email to an unattended mailbox accomplishes only the first. So does copying a senior official who treats it as background information while assuming that someone else is handling it.

A reasonable transfer requires grounds for believing that a suitable recipient recognizes the concern as something to address, has the remit and capacity to assess it or arrange an appropriate decision, and can do so while it still matters. An established role can supply that responsibility; a fresh promise is not always necessary. But mere delivery is insufficient evidence of a successful transfer. The sender must have grounds for reliance on the recipient’s role and response, not simply a name to place beside the report.

Timing makes the requirement more demanding. Suppose the safety officer accepts the report on Monday and will assess it on Friday. The stop moves on Tuesday because the original teams have completed their checks. The assessment has a recipient, but the handoff has not connected it to the action it should inform. Either implementation must await the assessment, a justified provisional arrangement must cover the interval, or an appropriate decision maker must address whether to proceed under that uncertainty. An acknowledged assignment with no bearing on Tuesday’s action does not justify Tuesday’s unqualified clearance.

So far the example gives one employee decisive control. Suppose instead that eight of ten approvals authorize implementation. Does the duty vanish for an employee whose refusal cannot stop the other nine from supplying eight approvals?

It does not, if her act is an operative authorization. To make that condition precise, imagine that each approval grants a standing, conditional permission: implementation may proceed when seven other valid approvals are in force. Each remains available as part of the basis for implementation. The first approval supplies such a conditional permission; the eighth makes the required combination available. A tenth approval can still supply another operative permission if the rules continue to recognize it, for example by allowing it to replace one of the other approvals if that approval is withdrawn. The first, eighth and tenth employees exercise the same kind of power, though their causal importance at a given moment differs.

The reason to qualify an approval is then the same reason to qualify a permission when other people might permit the action anyway. Another person’s willingness to authorize does not give me grounds to issue an authorization whose basis I recognize as defective. My qualification may prevent others from invoking my approval across this gap while leaving them able to proceed on other approvals. It limits my authorizing act; it does not guarantee that the agency preserves a block on implementation.

Now change the tenth case. Implementation was irrevocably authorized before the tenth completion arrived, and the late entry is retained only as a record of specialist work. Nobody may invoke it as clearance. This employee supplies no operative authorization, so the particular duty defended here does not apply to that entry. She may still have duties to warn, correct a record or help avert harm. Those duties should not be forced into a claim that she controls closure. Nor does a purely expressive endorsement become an operative authorization just because it is called a signature. This limit leaves the ethics of ineffective endorsement open.

A further difficulty concerns the endpoint of the duty. Does any answer from a supervisor suffice, however bad? It is tempting to contrast an unanswered objection with an explicit but wrongful decision. That contrast can locate a decision, but it cannot by itself vindicate the employee’s authorization. A person cannot acquire adequate grounds to issue clearance simply by finding someone willing to offer an inadequate reason.

Return to the report and compare four responses. First, nobody addresses it because the question falls outside assigned tasks. Second, a supervisor explicitly decides against assessment because it was not assigned. Third, the supervisor refuses assessment to save a trivial sum. Fourth, the supervisor offers a justified decision under uncertainty, perhaps arranging safe temporary access while an urgent assessment proceeds.

In the first case, the employee lacks an adequate basis for unqualified clearance. In the second and third, the position is the same if the inadequacy of the response is reasonably apparent to her. The agency’s moral requirement remains unsatisfied. There is no principled reason to treat an explicit assignment-based refusal as less of a decision than an explicit selfish tradeoff. Both are identifiable bad answers. In the fourth case, she can ordinarily rely on the adequate treatment and complete the relevant clearance. These assessments concern what she can reasonably judge, not an expectation that a nonexpert detect every error in a superior’s reasoning.

An inadequate answer can still change what happens next, but only if the authorization changes too. Suppose the employee reports that her checks are finished, expressly limits their authorizing force, and the manager uses separate authority to override the qualification and implement. Her limited duty concerning her own clearance can then be discharged. The manager has authorized implementation despite the unresolved issue. The moral demand on the agency has not been satisfied, and the employee may face further duties not to assist the wrongful action. A narrow duty can be met while other duties remain. What does not suffice is continuing to supply an unqualified approval and using the manager’s bad answer as a receipt that supposedly makes it acceptable.

The same distinction handles templates. A form naming the concern and announcing “risk accepted” does not, by its wording alone, provide an adequate basis for clearance. If it merely repeats a bad policy, it remains a bad answer. If it implements a justified policy for a class of uncertainties, and there are reasonable grounds for treating this case as belonging to that class, it can constitute adequate treatment without a fresh meeting. If it records a manager’s actual override, it helps identify that authorization but does not make it morally justified. Neither personal sincerity nor customized prose is the decisive condition.

Deadlines therefore need not make the duty intolerable. Appropriate treatment may conclude that further inquiry is unwarranted because the objection lacks support, was already answered, or is outweighed by the harms of delay. The objector need not be satisfied. What matters is whether the basis for proceeding responds reasonably to the relevant uncertainty. The omission of a question from an assignment list can explain why an answer is missing. It cannot, alone, explain why the answer is unnecessary.

Is all this simply negligence in a special setting? It can be understood as a specification of reasonable care. Its contribution is to identify the act to which care applies, the defect a limited remit does not excuse, and what counts as an appropriate response when work is divided. “Do not be negligent” leaves these questions unsettled. The proposed account answers them without requiring each worker to master the whole project.

Consider two otherwise identical agencies whose new stops both happen to be safe. One obtains an adequate assessment of the report. The other authorizes implementation while leaving a recognized serious objection untreated without adequate reason. The second can have acted wrongfully despite the safe outcome. This comparison establishes independence from actual injury; it does not distinguish the account from negligence, since negligence too can be wrongful without causing harm. Nor does it establish a separate right to any favored review procedure. The claim is that reasonable authorization must respond to the ex ante significance of the concern. Later good fortune does not supply that response.

The account also respects the distinction between individual and collective agency. Samuel Mortimer argues that participants’ self-narratives can affect which collective actions are attributable to them and can obscure opportunities to intervene. He nevertheless allows that someone whose involvement in collective wrongdoing was unintended may be culpable for negligence in enabling it. The present argument need not attribute moving the stop to every employee. Its object is the clearance the employee knowingly provides. Where her understanding reasonably excludes even that function of her act, the case for blaming her weakens. [Mortimer, §§3.2–4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/papa.70002).

Herlinde Pauer-Studer argues that a professional’s claim to be disengaged from an institution can conflict with the constitutive standards of the practice in which they participate. The present claim does not require a verdict on the coherence of an employee’s entire professional identity. Someone can accurately describe her modest role in a generally decent agency while exercising one of its powers without reasonable care. The role description is incomplete as a defense when it mentions the local work but omits the known authorizing function of completing it. [Pauer-Studer, §2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/japp.12320).

There are also close antecedents concerning procedural authority. In a recent working paper, Peter Kahl distinguishes legitimate closure from an illegitimate claim to epistemic finality. His discussion also stresses identifiable procedural authority and decisions about outstanding risk. Those overlaps preclude treating the difference between closing a review and settling every question as a discovery here. The candidate contribution is more specific: a duty governing local authorizing contributions, including when they are nondecisive, and a transfer condition which connects an unresolved task to the authorization it should inform. This proposal does not establish priority over the wider literature on professional handoffs and institutional responsibility. [Kahl, “Closure without finality” and discussion of procedural authority](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20439758).

Maximilian Kiener distinguishes answerability—an obligation to answer and explain—from liability to blame or punishment, and considers making answerability a condition of certain roles. That distinction helps prevent an inquiry from manufacturing a person who deserves punishment merely because someone must answer. The present proposal supplies a ground of assessment in a person’s knowing exercise of authorization. It is compatible with an obligation to explain that exercise, but an explanation does not automatically justify it. Nothing here requires rejecting Kiener’s more general account of taking answerability. [Kiener, §§3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-022-10313-9).

For an organization investigating failure, identifying the last person to click “complete” is consequently insufficient. It must establish what the entry authorized, what its author reasonably knew, what alternatives were available, and what those alternatives would have cost. Threats to employment and systems that make meaningful qualification impossible can mitigate individual blame while strengthening the case against those responsible for the arrangements. More powerful agents remain responsible for creating and maintaining a process in which no one is equipped to address a foreseeable class of concerns. A local duty does not transfer all institutional fault downwards.

The practical proposal should be stated separately from that assessment of existing conduct. An organization should let people finish specialist work without automatically granting broader clearance. It should connect a transferred assessment to the action it is supposed to inform. It should distinguish clearance supported by adequate treatment from an override that proceeds despite an outstanding qualification. These arrangements can make responsible action easier and responsibility more accurately attributable. They cannot ensure that people use their authority well.

The transit employee is right about her limits. She cannot determine every relevant fact or control the whole agency. Those limits justify a division of work and a practice of reasonable reliance. They do not make the job chart an adequate answer to a known serious objection. “I have finished what I was assigned” may be completely true. Using that truth to help authorize implementation is a further act, and it requires care of its own.
