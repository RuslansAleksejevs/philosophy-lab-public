# A Right Without Reliance: How an Accepted Promise Can Bind

## Abstract

A promise can give its recipient a directed claim even when the recipient has not changed plans, taken a risk, spent money, or given something up in reliance on it. This claim is narrower than the thought that every promise must be kept whatever the cost. It concerns an undertaking that the promisor deliberately offers as a commitment, that an identified recipient understands and accepts as such, and that concerns a permissible act within the promisor’s control. The best explanation is a limited form of normative power: through a public practice, two people can make a bounded part of one person’s future conduct answerable to the other. The recipient’s authority to demand performance or release the promisor differs from the practical reasons to perform and from the harm caused by breach. Reliance can strengthen the claim, change its priority, or ground repair; it need not create the claim. A practice-based account alone cannot justify this authority, and calling it a “power” does not settle the question. Its moral case rests on reciprocal agency, while its force remains defeasible and limited by the undertaking’s terms, consent, feasibility, and competing duties.

## 1. What a reliance case shows—and what it does not

Suppose I promise to bring you a book on Friday. You already own it, make no plans around my promise, and lose nothing if I fail to bring it. If I do not come, you have no reliance loss to recover. It does not follow that I have done nothing wrong to you. You may still ask me to keep the promise, release me from it, or demand an explanation. Those responses concern a relation between us, not merely the size of your loss.

This example is easy to overstate. Many ordinary promises are not morally binding: people speak loosely, predict what they will probably do, joke, or make offers that are never accepted. Nor does a promise settle what should happen when performance becomes impossible or would violate a more serious duty. The question here concerns a narrower case. The promisor deliberately communicates an undertaking, the recipient understands it as one and accepts it, and the promised act is permissible, feasible, and within the promisor’s control. The recipient need not have changed a plan, incurred a cost, or even expect that the promisor will keep the undertaking. Someone can accept an offer of commitment while doubting the speaker’s reliability.

The claim at issue is directed. It belongs to this recipient in a way that another person’s general disappointment does not. It gives the recipient standing to ask for performance, waive it, or release the promisor. That standing is not the same as a guarantee that performance must win against every other reason. It is also not the same as a claim to compensation for reliance loss. We should therefore separate three questions: whether the recipient has a claim at all; how much weight it has in the circumstances; and what response is owed if performance is excused or withheld.

Reliance can matter greatly to the second and third questions. If I book a venue because of your promise, the costs and risks I take may make performance more urgent and make repair more extensive. But that can be an added reason to perform or compensate without being the source of the promissory claim itself. Otherwise we would have to say that a recipient who wisely avoids depending on another person has no standing at all. That conclusion confuses being wronged with suffering a measurable loss.

## 2. A promise changes a relation, not just an expectation

The crucial question is not only why a promisor has a reason to act. It is why the recipient, rather than any interested observer, has authority to demand the act or release the promisor. The distinction is important. A promise might give the promisor a reason—perhaps because keeping promises is useful or admirable—without giving anyone in particular a claim. A directed obligation adds a holder: it makes the promisor answerable to the promisee.

A deliberate promise is more than a forecast about the promisor’s future behavior. It offers to place a specified act under a shared normative description: “this is something I undertake to you.” The recipient’s acceptance takes up that offer. What changes is not ownership of the promisor’s choices in general. The change is a limited authority over one described commitment. The recipient may insist on performance or release the promisor; the recipient cannot use the promise to control unrelated choices.

This is a form of normative power. The promisor uses a recognized act of promising to make a commitment, while the recipient’s acceptance establishes the relation in which that commitment is owed. Crescente Molina’s account of deontic control identifies a close version of this idea. The promisee has a distinctive interest in controlling whether the promissory obligation continues, an interest that a third party who merely expects the act does not share. Molina also distinguishes the ground of that interest from the conditions for creating the power: the promisor must intentionally promise and the promisee must accept. The account does not show that every utterance called a promise automatically creates a right, nor does its label by itself justify the right. It does clarify what reliance alone misses: a promise may give its addressee authority over the status of a commitment even when the promised act has little practical value.

Why should the promisor’s words have that effect? Not because sounds or private wishes magically produce duties. The act works within a public practice. Shared conventions make it possible to distinguish a promise from a prediction, an expression of hope, or casual reassurance; they identify whose undertaking it is and what its terms are. Hume’s discussion of promises makes the importance of common signs and social convention clear: private will, by itself, does not naturally create a special obligation. But a convention’s existence is not yet a moral defense of every rule it contains. The practice explains how a commitment can be made intelligible; it cannot alone establish that the recipient ought to receive standing.

The further moral premise is that people may create limited, voluntary obligations to one another when doing so respects their equal agency and does not violate prior rights or duties. Promises are one way to exercise that capacity. A person may choose to make a bounded part of future conduct answerable to another. The recipient, in turn, may accept or refuse that role. If acceptance has no normative effect, the promisor remains the only person with authority over whether the commitment matters. The recipient receives, at most, a prediction and a hope. Recognizing a defeasible claim gives both choices moral significance: the offer is not imposed on the promisor, and the accepted undertaking is not unilaterally erased by the promisor’s later change of mind.

This is a defense of pro tanto standing, not a license for arbitrary private control. The recipient’s authority is bounded by what was clearly undertaken, by the recipient’s acceptance, and by the moral permissibility and possibility of performance. A promise to commit an injustice cannot make the injustice due. A promise cannot give the recipient authority over acts that were not included in it. A major competing duty may excuse performance or defeat the claim’s priority. The promisee’s power to release the promisor is real, but it is not power to rewrite the promisor’s life.

## 3. Three cases that reliance theory should keep apart

Consider first a reliance case. You promise to deliver a key; I travel across town because I trust you, and you do not come. My travel may add a claim to reimbursement or another form of repair. It may also make the promised performance more urgent. It is tempting to treat this as the paradigm of promising, but it contains two possible grounds of complaint: the undertaking you gave me and the loss I suffered by relying on it. The second can exist without the first, and the first can exist without the second.

Now consider a confidence case. You promise to send me a document tomorrow. I make no plan based on that assurance, but I accept your words as a commitment. If tomorrow you simply change your mind, there is no reliance loss. Still, the undertaking was addressed to me and accepted by me. You have acted contrary to a relation you voluntarily created. Your failure can be objectionable even if the proper response is modest: perhaps I can ask you to send it now, or release you, or ask for an explanation. The absence of practical reliance limits the practical stakes without dissolving the relation.

Finally, consider the low-stakes book. I already have a copy, do not expect to need another, and will incur no cost if you fail to bring one. Suppose nevertheless that you clearly say you are committing to lend me your spare copy, and I accept. A theory that requires useful assurance or reliance will have trouble explaining why your later unilateral reversal is any wrong to me. The normative-power account can: I accepted a limited claim over this act, not because the book is valuable, but because your commitment made the act answerable to me. The value lies in my authority within the relation, not in the object’s market or practical value.

There are important limits to this case. If your words were a social pleasantry, if the two of us understood them as a prediction, or if I did not accept the offer as a commitment, the promise may never have been made. Merely uttering “I promise” is not enough. The public practice and our shared understanding matter. But where those conditions are met, low stakes do not by themselves cancel a claim. The claim may be easy to release and may be outweighed by a small inconvenience or a more urgent need; those considerations concern priority. They do not prove that no relation was created.

## 4. The strongest objection: does uptake really justify authority?

The most serious objection is that the account may smuggle the conclusion into its description. Calling the promise a “shared normative relation,” or saying the recipient “accepts” it, cannot itself show that the recipient gains a right. A promisor may intend to make a commitment, and a recipient may welcome that commitment, yet it remains open whether morality permits those facts to create a directed duty. Hume’s appeal to public convention helps explain how promises become meaningful, but social usefulness is not enough to prove a promisee’s exclusive authority to release the promisor. The practice may be efficient and still unjust.

This objection is right about what remains to be defended. The moral basis cannot simply be “people do this” or “the promisee wants control.” Rather, the best case is reciprocal and limited. The promisor voluntarily uses a mutually intelligible form to invite a specific person into a commitment relation. The recipient can decline it. If the offer is accepted, the recipient’s standing protects an ordinary form of interpersonal agency: each party can take part in defining what the other has undertaken to them, within the stated terms. Recognition of that power is one reason the practice can be valuable. It allows people to make dependable plans together, but it also allows them to recognize one another as agents whose accepted terms matter even when practical coordination is not at stake.

This is not a deductive proof that every accepted undertaking must bind. A recipient’s acceptance cannot cure coercion, fraud, incapacity, injustice, or a promise beyond the promisor’s legitimate authority. Nor can the promisor make any demand valid merely by uttering it. The practice must permit a fair understanding of what has been offered, and the parties must be able to refuse it. In difficult cases, those conditions may fail. The account therefore explains a central, ordinary class of promises rather than giving a complete theory of every promise-like exchange.

A second objection asks why the recipient should have both a claim and a release power. These are related but distinct. A claim gives the recipient standing to demand what was promised. A release power lets the recipient waive that claim or excuse its continuation. The latter does not mean the promisee can enforce performance at any cost, and the former does not mean the promisor is never excused. A competing duty can justify not performing while leaving the promisor answerable for notice or explanation. A genuine impossibility may remove the demand for performance while leaving duties of apology or repair. The precise response depends on the reason for nonperformance and on what the promisee releases.

A third objection is that the low-stakes case makes morality too fussy. If the book is harmlessly late, perhaps no one should be blamed. But the conclusion need not be that every technical lapse deserves punishment. Directed claims are not verdicts of maximal blame. The promisor may have a good excuse, and the promisee may reasonably waive performance. The point is only that a promisee can have standing even where the stakes are small. If morality recognizes only claims attached to measurable harm, then it cannot explain the difference between a promisor’s failure to the promisee and an identical failure that no one was entitled to expect.

## 5. What reliance contributes

The conclusion is not that reliance is irrelevant. It often reveals how much a promise matters to its recipient, and a promisor may have a special reason to protect plans they invited someone else to make. A reliance loss may support compensation even where the recipient did not accept a promise as a commitment. Conversely, a recipient can accept a commitment but suffer little loss. These are different routes to obligations, and a sound account should not force one to do the work of the other.

Reliance may also change what performance is owed. If I promise to attend your medical appointment and you arrange your care around me, my failure may endanger you. That changes the urgency and consequences. If I promise to bring a book you do not need, breach may be minor, but the directed claim need not disappear. The difference is in weight and response, not necessarily in whether a claim exists.

The promisee’s control interest should likewise be understood modestly. A promise is not a transfer of the promisor’s agency. It is an accepted limit on how the promisor may treat one undertaking. The recipient cannot demand performance contrary to a stronger moral duty, and the promisor can seek release. But the promisor cannot treat the promise as merely a prediction they are free to revise. The act of commitment matters because it gives another person a reason to take the relation seriously—and, in a valid case, a standing to say what follows from it.

## Conclusion

A recipient need not reorganize a life around a promise before acquiring standing to the promised act. Practical reliance can increase the harm of breach, make performance more urgent, or ground a claim to repair. It is not necessary for the basic directed relation. An intentional undertaking, understood and accepted within a fair public practice, can give the promisee a bounded power to demand performance or release the promisor. That power is justified, where it is justified, by reciprocal agency: one person voluntarily offers a limited commitment, and another chooses whether to accept it. Hume helps explain why public convention is needed to make promising intelligible; Molina clarifies the distinctive control interest of the promisee. Neither point by itself settles the moral argument. The remaining case is a limited one: accepted promises can create defeasible directed claims, while the weight of those claims and the proper response to breach remain sensitive to competing duties, actual harm, and the parties’ choices.
