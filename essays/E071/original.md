# What the Envoy Still Controls: Delegated Speech in the Zi Gao Episode

Zi Gao is sent on a mission he did not choose. He expects to be received politely but fears that the other ruler will not act. Confucius first tells him that a minister serves without choosing the business. He then warns him about how messages are carried: words can be embellished, instructions departed from, or a settlement pressed. What kind of agency remains when the mission is fixed but its transmission can still affect what happens?

The Zi Gao episode in chapter 4 of the *Zhuangzi* supports a limited answer. In the English translations considered here, the passage places compulsory service beside advice about communicative conduct that can affect trust and negotiations. This juxtaposition invites a distinction between authority over an assignment and control over some of one's actions in carrying it out. It does not establish a doctrine of moral responsibility, blame for the mission, or a duty to disobey. The envoy's conduct can make a causal difference without that fact settling whether he is morally answerable for the mission's end.

That distinction matters because three questions are easily run together: who chose the political purpose, what can the intermediary control, and who deserves blame for the resulting outcome? The scene speaks most clearly to the first two. Its answer to the third remains uncertain. Read this way, the passage is neither a defense of obedience nor a theory of complicity. It offers a narrower picture of constrained agency: lack of authority over the end does not make every means causally inert, while control over a means does not make the agent responsible for the whole outcome.

## Compulsory service and controlled conduct

Zi Gao, Duke of She, is preparing to travel from Chu to Qi on his king's important business. He anticipates a formal reception without prompt action and fears the consequences whether the mission succeeds or fails. Confucius answers first by describing a subject's allegiance as unavoidable. Giles translates the minister as serving his prince “without reference to the act but only to the service”; Legge says the subject serves “without reference to or choice of the business” (Giles, ch. IV, pp. 46–47; Legge, ch. IV, para. 3). Both renderings stress the envoy's lack of choice about the commission.

The advice then turns from the commission to the transfer of messages. Giles says that intermediaries may overstate expressions of approval or anger, making their words reckless and unconvincing, to their own danger. Legge likewise warns that expressive excess fails to command belief and puts the messenger at risk. Both translations therefore connect features of the envoy's speech with consequences for credibility and safety. In each, the messenger is constrained by the task but is not described as a transparent passage through which nothing can change.

The translations also describe a broader causal sequence. Giles compares speech to wind that raises waves and says that clever, one-sided words can prompt anger; when one party is driven too far into a corner, resistance follows. Legge says that words can obscure the point at issue and that quarrels arise from artful and one-sided speech. These are not accounts of the envoy's sole control over a negotiation. The point is more modest: the passage treats some communicative conduct as capable of worsening an already difficult exchange.

The advice about instructions and settlement needs particular care. Giles renders a rule as “Neither deviate from nor travel beyond your instructions,” but the translator's footnote says “travel beyond” is literally “urge a settlement.” Legge renders two injunctions separately: do not depart from instructions and do not urge a settlement. The English texts thus support discussion of instruction-fidelity and pressure toward settlement, but they do not establish a stable three-part taxonomy in the source. I use “expressive overflow,” “departure from instructions,” and “pressure for settlement” only as an analytic organization of the translations. It is not a claim about three distinct Chinese terms or acts. The classical Chinese passage was not available in this research, so that question remains open.

These cautions narrow but do not erase the interpretive point. The scene moves from service that the minister cannot choose to directions about conduct that the envoy can regulate at least in the practical sense presupposed by advice. The text does not name this a division between ends and means; that is a reconstruction of the sequence. It is also not a claim that Zi Gao enjoys unrestricted freedom. The instructions operate within an assigned role and a situation he did not make.

## Causal contribution is not moral answerability

An envoy who adds a threat absent from the message may make a difference to how it is received. If the threat provokes resistance, it can be one cause among several. That description identifies a causal contribution; by itself, it does not establish blame. The same distinction applies to urging a settlement: a messenger can affect the negotiation without selecting the mission's purpose or controlling the receiving ruler's response. The episode's warnings make such consequences salient, but they do not tell us what moral verdict follows.

This separation avoids two opposite overstatements. It would be too strong to blame Zi Gao for every consequence of a mission whose content and purpose he did not choose. It would also be too strong to infer that his lack of authority over the purpose makes all his actions in transmitting it causally irrelevant. The story permits a middle description: an intermediary may have limited control over the contribution he makes, even when he lacks control over the assignment. Whether that contribution is culpable depends on questions the passage does not answer, including what the mission demands, what the envoy knows, what alternatives he has, and what harms are foreseeable.

This is why “responsibility” is not the right conclusion to draw from the passage alone. One might use “responsibility” in a purely role-relative sense, meaning answerability for performing an assigned task competently. But even that risks sounding like a moral doctrine supplied by the text. The present argument therefore uses the narrower terms “control” and “causal contribution.” The envoy's acts may be attributable to him as parts of the exchange without the scene deciding whether they make him blameworthy for the mission. Neither exact transmission nor restraint proves that the assigned purpose is just. Conversely, the fact that the stated rules are prudent does not remove the intermediary's causal role from the account.

## The prudential reading remains strong

The strongest rival interpretation says that the passage is counsel for self-preservation and stable negotiation, not a philosophical account of agency. It tells Zi Gao that he cannot avoid service and advises him to convey messages without excess, avoid unauthorized changes, and not force an agreement. The stated reasons concern distrust, danger to the envoy, conflict, and the durability of a settlement. The text does not ask whether the mission is just, instruct the envoy to refuse it, or assign him blame for its consequences. Giles's closing appeal to Destiny and Legge's parallel language of resignation reinforce the pragmatic and resigned frame.

That rival reading is not defeated here. It may fully explain why the advice is given. The limited claim is that a prudential purpose and an account of controllable conduct can coexist. A warning can protect the messenger precisely because his way of speaking can affect what happens. The passage therefore makes the envoy's practical agency visible without turning that fact into a moral verdict. This reading does not refute a general view that agents can be instruments of their superiors, and the episode itself does not present or refute such a theory. It only resists describing this messenger as causally inert within the scene.

The distinction has a modest philosophical payoff. It keeps separate the source of an action's authorization from the intermediary's influence on its execution. A ruler can authorize an end; an envoy can still add force, alter scope, or press for a result, at least as the translations present the advice. Those facts matter for explaining how an outcome arose. They are insufficient on their own to decide who is morally answerable. The passage gives us a way to ask that further question more carefully, not an answer to it.

## A bounded interpretation, not a literature-gap claim

This essay does not claim that scholarship has overlooked the Zi Gao episode or that its distinction is unprecedented. Kwek's political reading treats chapter 4 as relevant to officials and administrators and discusses Zigao's mission in the portion consulted (2019, pp. 412–415). The SEP discussion of Chinese heart-mind and the limited excerpts from Lai concern responsive agency in the Yan Hui episode, not a verified account of the present argument. Rathnam's 2026 publisher page provides title, metadata, and an abstract that broadly discusses engagement with government while preserving life; the chapter itself was marked inaccessible and was not read. These checks are too narrow to establish what the literature has or has not said about the messenger passage.

The contribution claimed here is correspondingly small: a reconstruction of how the passage, in the available translations, puts unavoidable service next to advice about controllable communicative conduct, while leaving moral answerability unsettled. This may be a useful way to state the structure of the scene. Whether it adds a novel argument to scholarship remains provisional, especially pending access to the Chinese text, fuller secondary literature, and the Rathnam chapter. No global originality claim follows from the present reading.

The passage's combination remains striking even under these limits. Zi Gao is told that he cannot choose the business, then told to attend to how he carries it. Giles and Legge give grounds for seeing that conduct as consequential, though their renderings diverge over how to divide the instructions. The safest conclusion is neither that the envoy is morally guilty nor that he is merely an empty conduit. He has constrained practical control over parts of the communication, and those parts may contribute to an outcome. The moral status of obeying the mission, and the degree of answerability for what it causes, remain open.
