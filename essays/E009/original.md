# Before You Tell Us to Try Again

A town votes to close the bus service linking an outlying district to its centre. The money will support a service used by more people. The debate has been open, the district's residents have voted, and the majority has a serious case. Some residents nevertheless object that losing the bus will make it much harder to attend public meetings, use the library's internet connection, and organize around common problems. Officials dispute this prediction. They propose to proceed and restore access if experience shows that the residents were right. A substantial petition will trigger reconsideration.

That answer contains a problem distinct from the merits of either service. The residents' ability to secure reconsideration may depend on the capacities they say the policy will remove. If they are right, the promised remedy becomes hardest to obtain when it is most needed. “You can try again” is an inadequate assurance when the decision may take away the means of trying.

The inadequacy does not, by itself, make closure impermissible. Perhaps the risk is small enough to accept without protection. Perhaps retaining the route would impose worse risks on other citizens. But officials cannot count an unusable remedy among their reasons for asking residents to accept closure. They must justify the risk without that protection or provide protection that works.

This distinction supports a precise requirement. When future correction is offered as a reason to accept a policy that may impair political standing, the route to correction must be assessed under the hypothesis that the impairment occurs. Its reliability in ordinary circumstances is insufficient. The assessment must follow the route from discovering and establishing the injury through obtaining and implementing a remedy. If the hypothesized injury would disable an indispensable step, the assurance loses the corresponding justificatory weight. Where acceptable implementation depends on that assurance, the route must be protected before the risk takes effect.

This is a contribution to the justification of decisions under disagreement, rather than a complete theory of permissible political risk. Its further implication concerns the order of evidence and protection: an authority cannot rely on later proof of injury while leaving the production or effective use of that proof exposed to the injury itself. The argument identifies when “let experience decide” requires institutional preparation before experience arrives.

Democracy's claim on a losing citizen cannot simply be that the majority is correct. Citizens can disagree responsibly about transport, taxation, or which public institutions should survive. Giving a majority authority permits collective action without requiring agreement about the outcome. Its justification must therefore give the dissenter some reason to accept decisions she continues to regard as mistaken.

Equal participation can supply such a reason. Others' judgments cannot count only when they converge with one's own. Yet equality concerns a continuing relationship. Participation in one decision is not a payment that extinguishes one's claims to participate later. Losing citizens remain entitled to form political judgments, express and associate around them, and bring objections before institutions with power to respond. A policy's effects on those capacities therefore matter even if its enactment was fair.

These commitments are familiar. Thomas Christiano connects democratic authority and its limits to public equality, and discusses how persistent minorities may lack meaningful political input despite nominal inclusion. Philip Pettit makes individual contestation central to democracy and defends advance arrangements that reduce the burden of challenging decisions. I draw on the continuing importance of citizens' standing without assuming that every threat to it defeats a policy's authority. [Christiano 2011, pp. 2–4, 12–14](https://www.jesp.org/pdf/36346f73-90e0-819f-97d2-cda0dceac43a); [Pettit 2004, §§2.2–2.3](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1616).

The political consequences of substantive policies are also well studied. Daryl Levinson and Benjamin Sachs show how policies can entrench power without explicit barriers to repeal, while explaining why ordinary political change cannot simply be separated from objectionable entrenchment. Gabriel Levine recommends assessing policies' democratic consequences and including triggers for later review. Eva Krick examines exclusion from acquiring, producing and disseminating knowledge, and the institutional conditions of overcoming it. Neither continuing contestation, political feedback, review triggers nor the importance of access to evidence is a discovery of this essay. [Levinson and Sachs 2015, Introduction and Part III](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/political-entrenchment-and-public-law); [Levine 2023, Part II.B, Principles 4–5](https://direct.mit.edu/ajle/article/doi/10.1162/ajle_a_00059/117629/DEMOCRATICALLY-DURABLE-REGULATION); [Krick 2026, “Access-denying forms of epistemic injustice”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41269-026-00429-6).

My proposed specification connects these concerns at a particular point: assessing an assurance of correction when the alleged injury would change the conditions under which correction works. A general commitment to accessible contestation leaves open which circumstances to use in judging accessibility. A calendar of future reviews leaves open who will be able to supply evidence then. Looking from the standpoint of the hypothesized impairment exposes a dependence that either arrangement can conceal.

Return to the town. There are two questions. Will withdrawal of the service seriously impair residents' political standing? If it does, how reliably could that impairment be established and repaired? Officials might reasonably answer the first question “probably not” while answering the second “not reliably.” Their confidence about the first cannot settle the second. A petition that works only when civic access remains intact provides no protection against the loss of that access.

The distinction is easily missed when institutions have a record of responding well to complaints. Suppose the town has granted every warranted petition about transport. That record may demonstrate conscientious officials and sensible deliberation. It does not establish that residents deprived of transport can assemble the next petition. Evidence of what happens to complaints that arrive cannot alone establish what happens to people whose problem prevents their complaints from arriving.

The same problem can survive more elaborate arrangements. A scheduled review may receive testimony almost entirely from beneficiaries. A public advocate may obtain evidence but lack the means to bring it before a deciding body. A committee may order an adequate remedy whose funding depends on renewed political pressure by the disadvantaged residents. In each case, a protection appears stronger if its assessment stops before the step exposed to the political injury.

There are three parts to the argument. First, future correction can be a genuine reason for accepting a fallible decision: some mistakes are less objectionable to risk if those affected can identify them and secure an appropriate response. Second, how much this reason supports proceeding depends on the actual prospects of correction when the mistake occurs, rather than on the existence of institutions that work well in its absence. Third, a policy can itself lower those prospects by impairing the capacities needed to challenge it. Consequently, assurance of correction must be assessed using the political circumstances the disputed injury would produce. Otherwise, a reason for accepting the risk has been overstated.

The first premise does not imply that eventual repair cancels every injury. Lost time, humiliation or exclusion may remain uncompensated. Nor does the argument assume that correction is the only reason for accepting a decision. It establishes how to evaluate one important reason without allowing it to borrow its force from the wrong circumstances.

Nothing in this logic is confined to politics. An insurer who promises compensation for destroyed records but requires those records as proof has designed a defective remedy. The political case should not be made to seem original by denying that resemblance. What changes is the justification in which the remedy figures. Officials are asking people to accept continuing coercive decisions despite disagreement, partly because their opportunity to challenge mistakes persists. When a policy impairs that opportunity, it can weaken both citizens' standing and the corrective reason offered for accepting its exercise. Their having enjoyed an initial vote establishes neither that the later opportunity survives nor that losing it is an acceptable risk.

The political argument therefore adds an assessment of democratic justification, not a new general law of remedial design. It also shows why a remedy for political injury may need to be prepared before that injury can be proved. Imagine that decisive evidence about access will become available only after closure. Residents can ordinarily be asked to provide evidence for their claims. But if collecting or presenting that evidence requires the threatened access, simply waiting does not leave the evidential situation unchanged. It selects conditions in which one side may be less able to substantiate its position.

A prior record, independent monitoring or assistance in presenting claims can prevent that dependence. None commits officials to believing the residents' prediction. The majority may reject it while accepting that, if it is right, there must be a workable way to find out and respond. Where the policy's justification needs that prospect, officials must secure it before the policy endangers it. Where the policy is acceptable even without it, the present argument alone does not require the precaution. This is a conditional ordering requirement, not an unconditional priority for all safeguards.

The distinction matters in cases involving different risks. Compare two otherwise identical towns. In both, reliable evidence establishes that the petition would fail if serious civic impairment occurred. In one, the chance of such impairment is substantial. In the other, the chance is one in ten thousand. These are hypothetical probabilities, not estimates about transport. Both petitions fail the same test of corrective adequacy. It does not follow that both towns owe the same precautions.

If substantial risk to ordinary civic access could be reduced greatly at negligible cost, with no relevant competing demand on resources, providing protection is strongly supported. The further premise is ordinary proportional care for an important interest: a serious, readily avoidable threat should not be imposed for negligible gain. The test of correction identifies why the petition does not already supply that care; it does not independently establish this premise.

Now suppose the evidenced risk is very small, while a safeguard would certainly reduce access elsewhere—for example, by withdrawing staff who help many residents use existing public services. Even if no resulting individual burden is comparably grave, their number, duration and distribution can matter. This essay does not establish that the safeguard is mandatory. Omitting it may be defensible after a fair assessment of those competing claims. The town must nevertheless treat the feared injury as lacking the petition's supposed protection. Reliable evidence for a low probability is different from weak evidence that any risk exists; neither distinction rescues the petition.

The assessment must also concern the feasible set of policies. Many individually modest safeguards can consume a fixed civic-access budget. Choosing which protections to provide then requires comparing them, not treating each as a choice between a safeguard and indifference. A shared complaints service might offer better protection than several specialized mechanisms. Alternatively, a reform might reduce background exclusion substantially while making a remaining, rarer injury harder to contest. That reform can be defensible despite the defective channel. Its benefits and total risks must do the justificatory work that the channel cannot.

No universal numerical threshold follows. The categorical claim is about the evidence needed to credit a protection. The judgment about how much protection is owed remains sensitive to likelihood, severity, distribution, competing rights and opportunity costs. A polity could accept this claim while disagreeing about the right theory for making those comparisons. Where an unprotected risk remains impermissible under the assessment governing the case, officials must change the proposal, supply adequate protection or postpone it. An unusable remedy cannot close the justificatory gap.

This also resolves a possible ambiguity about officials' words. Imagine two councils proposing the same closure with the same risks, benefits and petition mechanism. One advertises the petition as insurance against civic exclusion. The other candidly admits that it would provide no such insurance. Their substantive obligations concerning residents' access are the same; removing a promise does not improve the policy. The first council additionally offers a defective reason. The second can justify proceeding only if its remaining reasons are sufficient. Honesty eliminates the misleading assurance, not any duty independently owed to the residents.

The immediate defect thus concerns a reason for accepting the exercise of authority. It may expose an impermissible exercise where the other reasons are insufficient. It does not automatically remove the decision's binding force, establish permission to resist it or invalidate the government as a whole. Those conclusions require further arguments. A fair initial procedure can remain morally relevant even when one assurance offered for its outcome fails.

Which capacities fall within the political concern? An indiscriminate answer would preserve privilege. Almost any policy changes resources or incentives, and those changes can affect politics. An estate tax may diminish a wealthy family's advertising budget. Ending a professional monopoly may weaken an association defending it. Neither loss is necessarily a loss of equal standing.

The relevant object is a fair practical opportunity to act as a citizen, including obtaining public information, communicating objections, associating and invoking procedures that can address one's treatment. Serious obstacles arise when people must depend on exceptional sacrifice or a patron's goodwill to perform ordinary civic activities while others can perform them as a matter of course. This is not a guarantee of equal influence over every outcome, much less of equal success. A functioning mailbox is not enough if the polity demands evidence and forms that the affected people cannot practically obtain or prepare.

The wealthy family may retain ample opportunity after losing its advertising advantage. An association can lose an unjust privilege while its members remain able to organize. Protection need not preserve a group's leaders, membership or established views. Conversely, merely offering individual complaint rights can be inadequate when the policy destroys the only affordable means through which residents can discover a shared injury and coordinate their response. Association matters because citizenship is often exercised together, not because every existing organization must survive.

This standard remains contestable. The point is not to settle all questions about political equality by definition. It is to distinguish the underlying objection from the further question about an advertised remedy. A claimant must identify a civic activity, a mechanism of serious obstruction, and evidence that available alternatives may fail. If the objection is sufficiently supported to require an answer, the authority cannot evade it by describing a corrective procedure that presupposes its falsity.

What would make the town's assurance credible? Consider an illustrative arrangement whose functions are established before closure. Residents can report difficulties locally or through home visits. A public advocate has resources to record access needs and assemble relevant evidence, so using the process does not require travel to the centre. An appointed review panel must give reasons, can consider competing testimony, and has authority to order interim civic transport where it finds the specified impairment. Funds and administrative authority for carrying out those orders are provided as part of the closure plan. A designated operator must deliver the service; nonperformance is checked through the same accessible local channels.

Follow one complaint. A resident reports being unable to obtain public information after closure. She need not first visit the library to learn how to complain. The advocate documents the obstacles without requiring the resident to organize a substantial petition. The panel can hear the evidence without restoring the bus first. If it finds an impairment warranting interim transport, implementation does not require the resident to mobilize a fresh majority for an appropriation. Monitoring delivery likewise does not depend on her reaching the centre. At each step, the alleged injury has been prevented from disabling the proposed response.

These arrangements satisfy the test only if they are sufficiently credible in practice. Suppose the governing coalition can routinely withhold the designated funds, and its increased insulation from the district makes withholding predictable. Then local reporting and a successful hearing do not solve the problem. The later funding dependence repeats the earlier petition dependence. A legal duty to implement, with no credible prospect of compliance, is insufficient evidence of protection.

Officials consequently need grounds for expecting the relevant route to function: actual staff and resources, a workable means of identifying affected people, clear authority over the needed response, and reasonable expectations of compliance. Past performance may support those expectations, but only insofar as it illuminates performance under the hypothesized impairment. No particular institutional label establishes this. An insulated panel can lack effective powers; a democratically controlled body can sometimes have them.

This last possibility departs from Pettit's more specific institutional argument that bodies hearing contestations must be distinct from the elected forums responsible for the contested measures. His defense of contestation should not be taken as endorsing every arrangement permitted here. My narrower test does not decide that institutional question. It asks whether the threatened capacity is indispensable to making the remedy work. Organizational separation may help, but the example shows why it is not sufficient. [Pettit 2004, §2.3, pp. 62–63](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/document/1616).

Neither the panel nor the funding arrangement must be infallible or immune to all future political change. A fair hearing may make a mistake. An operator may suffer an unrelated failure. Such risks affect how much assurance can reasonably be claimed. The particular error to avoid is counting as protection a route whose reliability has not been evaluated under the injury it addresses. If that injury predictably obstructs implementation, its effect belongs in the assessment; it cannot disappear merely because claimants can enter the process.

Nor must the remedy guarantee favorable judgments. A panel can fairly conclude that remaining access is adequate. Protection must allow that conclusion to be contested on the same fair terms without granting endless appeals or automatic victory. There can be a final decision on a claim. What makes the arrangement useful is its credible ability to identify and address warranted complaints despite the specified impairment, not its commitment to validating every complainant.

There is a related restriction on interpreting silence. A lack of complaints may indicate that residents have adjusted successfully, or that they can no longer report their difficulties. If silence is just as likely under either hypothesis, it provides no basis for favoring the first. If some reporting remains possible, silence may still be informative, but its weight must reflect the impaired reporting process. It cannot be treated as if every serious problem would reliably reach officials.

This creates a special danger for a democratic experiment. The policy can alter not only the conditions citizens wish to challenge but also the evidence by which its acceptability will later be assessed. A well-attended review among beneficiaries can reinforce confidence without testing the concern about those absent. The appropriate response is neither to assume that silence proves injury nor to abandon learning from experience. It is to preserve, or independently check, the means through which the relevant experience becomes publicly available.

A serious objection remains: citizens will disagree about fair access, adequate evidence and reliable remedies. Why should an author's conception of these matters constrain an equal vote? Jeremy Waldron's warning is pertinent. Disagreement about democracy's conditions does not eliminate the importance of a fair procedure for deciding them. His argument against judicial review is conditional on reasonably functioning institutions, and its failure in other circumstances does not automatically vindicate courts. Comparative institutional judgment remains necessary. [Waldron 2006, pp. 1359–1361, 1399–1402](https://www.humanities.mcmaster.ca/~walucho/3Q3/Waldron.Core%20Case%20Judicial%20Review%20Yale%20LJ.pdf).

The present requirement specifies a question for legitimate institutions rather than installing an authority outside them. Officials can reject unsupported mechanisms of impairment, assess competing safeguards and decide what residual risk to accept. But they must distinguish those judgments from the claim that an offered route supplies protection. Otherwise their answer to disagreement assumes away the contingency it was supposed to address.

The requirement can even make action under disagreement easier to justify. Officials and residents need not first agree on whether the loss of the bus will cause exclusion if they can establish a corrective route that remains usable if it does. They can disagree about closure while assessing concrete questions about local reporting, evidential assistance and implementation. That further assessment can also be contested. There is no promise of a procedure beyond all challenge—only a specification of what a justified appeal to future correction must address.

Protection may incidentally help opponents organize. That does not make it an unfair subsidy for losing. The relevant support should attach to the civic obstacle, not to a person's vote or affiliation. Today's supporter can encounter tomorrow's injury. Protecting ordinary means of association differs from funding a party to regain office. A remedy can work properly while leaving the underlying policy, and the government responsible for it, in place.

Finally, the argument permits necessary action and irreversible choices. A dangerous bus service may need to close immediately. A reform may protect more people from worse exclusion even when every available option leaves a serious risk. Officials can justify proceeding on those grounds, while acknowledging the limits of correction and arranging feasible repair. They cannot make the risk smaller merely by calling a petition available. And a town can demolish a building even though later voters cannot restore the identical building: citizens may still challenge remaining consequences, seek compensation or demand changes preventing repetition. Correctability concerns what a route can actually address, not a promise to undo history.

The town should therefore assess two different proposals: closure with a credible route to address the feared civic impairment, and closure without one. They may receive different verdicts even when they involve the same transport decision. A paper petition procedure must not erase their difference.

The precise result is an ordering condition on a familiar democratic justification. When permission to proceed depends on citizens' ability to establish and correct a political injury later, sufficient means of doing so must survive that injury from the start. Whether the risk is acceptable without those means is a further question. Keeping the questions separate avoids both an indiscriminate demand for precautions and an unwarranted reassurance. A democracy may ask citizens to live with a fallible decision. If it asks them to do so because they can try again, it must assess their ability to try in the world where its critics are right.
