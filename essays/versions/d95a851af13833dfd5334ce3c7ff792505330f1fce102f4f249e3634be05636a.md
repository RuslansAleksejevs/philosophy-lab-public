# What a Complete Explanation Must Leave Open

A machine gives a prize to one of two people. Its rules guarantee a winner, give each person an equal chance, and use a genuinely random process. Anna wins. We know how the machine works. We also know why the prize was offered and why these two people qualified. Have we explained everything about the result?

There are two tempting answers. One says yes: the result came from a fully understood process. The other says no: nothing explains why Anna won rather than Ben. Neither answer should be accepted without asking what “everything” covers. Understanding the process can settle one question while leaving another unanswered. Yet the unanswered question does not make the understood process worthless.

This distinction matters beyond lotteries. A metaphysical theory may promise to explain why the world exists, why it follows certain laws, or why every contingent fact obtains. A contingent fact is one that could have been otherwise. Such a theory can appear to keep its promise by moving to a broad enough description: some outcome occurred, some world exists, some choice was made. The individual result then disappears from view.

I defend a limit on that move. If an explanation claims to leave no contingent fact unexplained, it must keep track of the real differences that its broad descriptions leave open. Combining alternatives into a single description does not settle the differences between them. Moving a difference into an earlier choice does not erase it either. This requirement does not establish that every difference must have an explanation. It shows what someone must give up when they accept a difference for which there is no further reason.

## The question changes when the alternatives change

Suppose the machine has three possible outcomes: Anna wins, Ben wins, or nobody wins because the machine is switched off. It is working and switched on. That explains why someone wins rather than nobody. But the same information would be available if Ben won. It does not tell us why Anna is the winner rather than Ben.

We can make this point without assuming that every explanation must guarantee its outcome. Suppose a defect gives Anna a nine-in-ten chance. The defect can help explain why Anna won rather than Ben, even though Ben could still have won. The issue is not whether the reason rules out the alternative. It is whether the reason has any bearing on the difference being asked about.

The distinction has a clear predecessor. Van Fraassen argues that a why-question depends on the alternatives under discussion and on what sort of answer is relevant. The same event can receive an answer under one comparison and fail to receive it under another. His account also resists the idea that complete information automatically amounts to a single, context-free explanation. These points appear in chapter 5 of *The Scientific Image*, especially sections 2.8–2.9 and 4.3. My argument accepts their lesson about questions. It asks what follows when someone makes the further, much stronger claim that no contingent fact remains unexplained.

That claim cannot mean merely that every question we happen to ask has an answer. We could achieve that by asking only easy questions. Nor can it mean that every fact has at least one description under which something explains it. Anna's victory is a prize being awarded, an event occurring, and something happening. Explaining the broadest of these descriptions would then count as explaining the most specific. The promise would shrink as soon as it met resistance.

A serious claim to completeness must therefore say which differences it covers. It need not answer confused questions, explain contradictions, or distinguish two names for the same possibility. But if it accepts Anna's victory and Ben's victory as different possible results, it cannot silently discard their difference while claiming to have explained the whole result.

## A test using the same machine

Consider two accounts of the lottery. The first describes the construction of the machine, its activation, its fair chances, and the path by which it displays the result. It ends with Anna's name on the screen. The second gives all the same general information and ends with Ben's name.

Some parts of the two accounts differ because the outcomes differ. Anna's name is displayed in one; Ben's in the other. Those facts show what happened. Other parts explain later differences. Once the screen displays Anna's name, the rule that the displayed person receives the prize explains why the money goes to Anna. The display provides a perfectly useful explanation of the transfer.

But now ask why the display showed Anna rather than Ben. If the random process has no further feature favouring Anna, the earlier account is unchanged when we exchange the names. This symmetry marks the limit of that account. It explains the procedure and the equal chances. It does not favour one winner over the other.

The test is not simply whether the same sentences could be true if the other outcome occurred. Reasons that favour an outcome need not guarantee it. Rather, ask whether exchanging the outcomes leaves their entire treatment by the offered explanation unchanged. That includes their chances, the relevant causal roles, and any reasons for preferring them. If all those features remain the same, the account has supplied no basis for the difference.

Why should that matter? Because an answer to the difference question must do more than accompany the actual result. If a fact counts as explaining Anna's victory over Ben solely because it occurred alongside Anna's victory, it would equally count as explaining Ben's victory over Anna alongside that result. The proposed explanatory link would add nothing beyond a record of whichever outcome happened. To go beyond that record, the account needs something relevant to the contrast: a bias, an earlier decision, an actual causal route that differs, or some other feature whose explanatory role can be stated.

This does not make probabilities the sole measure of explanation. A person may explain her decision by a reason without giving us a numerical chance. Nor does a probability by itself establish an explanation: a later report can make us certain who won without causing or otherwise explaining the win. The symmetry test supplies a limited negative result. When every explanatory feature offered treats two real alternatives alike, that account does not explain their difference. It supplies no general recipe for a successful positive explanation.

A defender of random explanation can accept this. The random mechanism produced the actual victory; it made that victory possible in a definite way and gave it a definite chance. Calling this an explanation is reasonable. What needs defending is the additional claim that its success settles every question about the outcome. One may instead say that the mechanism fully explains the outcome in a causal sense, while no reason favours this result over its equal alternative. That is a clear position. It leaves the contrast unanswered, rather than answering it by the word “fully.”

## Why moving the choice does not finish the job

Suppose we improve our account. Anna won because a selector entered state A; had it entered state B, Ben would have won. We have now explained a difference by an earlier difference. This is real progress. We have located where the outcomes separate.

But if the selector's entry into A rather than B is itself a genuinely random event, the same question reappears there. We can explain the machine's response to the selector without explaining why the selector took that state. The question has moved, not vanished.

There is no defect here for an ordinary explanation. Lipton's opening discussion in “Contrastive Explanation” makes the relevant point: knowledge of a cause can explain an effect without knowledge of the cause's own cause. We need not understand the entire history of the universe to understand a broken window. My claim concerns an account whose stated scope includes every contingent fact. Such an account cannot borrow the modest scope of an ordinary explanation exactly where its wider promise becomes difficult.

Nor does adding more intermediate stages by itself help. A switch may depend on a signal, the signal on a choice, and the choice on a prior condition. Each link can explain something. If the account ends at a contingent difference that it does not explain, however, the last difference is still within its announced scope. An infinite chain raises further questions about whether its members and the chain as a whole can be explained. Nothing here settles those questions. The narrower conclusion is that merely relocating the first unexplained difference, whether once or many times, does not show that it has been removed.

This gives a practical way to assess claims of completeness. Begin with an accepted difference between possibilities. Follow the proposed explanation to any earlier difference on which it relies. At each step ask whether that earlier difference is explained, declared necessary, denied to be a real difference, or accepted without further explanation. These answers have very different consequences. A change of vocabulary should not hide which answer the theory has chosen.

## The old dispute about choosing between equals

Leibniz and Clarke disagree over a case with the same structure. Leibniz holds that choice requires a reason and denies that God selects between alternatives with nothing to recommend one over the other. He also rejects certain alleged alternatives as the same state under different names. These are distinct responses: some differences are excluded by the demand for a reason; others dissolve because there was never a real difference to explain. See Leibniz's Fourth Paper, sections 1–6 and 18–19.

Clarke replies that an active being need not behave like a balance with equal weights. There can be a reason to act even when no way of acting is preferable. The capacity to act belongs to the agent; it need not come from an unequal pull of motives. See Clarke's Fourth Reply, sections 1–2.

The present argument does not show that Clarke's agent is impossible. Imagine that two roads are equally good and a traveller needs to reach home. The need to get home explains why she takes a road rather than staying put. Her decision to go left can explain why she travels left. There may be nothing irrational about that decision.

Yet the goodness of going home does not distinguish left from right. If we ask why she chose left, appealing again to the shared goodness of both routes repeats an answer to a broader question. Appealing to her actual choice gives us the point at which the histories differ. It does not automatically supply a further reason for that choice.

The distinction protects Clarke's central possibility while limiting an inflated conclusion someone might draw from it. Acting without a preference between equal options need not be acting without any purpose or cause. But a purpose common to both options cannot, just by being a good purpose, settle why this option was selected. A defender can allow a basic act of selection that needs no further contrastive reason. What the defender cannot also claim, without more argument, is that the shared purpose supplies that missing reason.

This is especially important when a theory uses choice to explain the world. A reason to create some good world can explain creation rather than non-creation. If several worlds are equally eligible, that reason leaves their difference open. A particular act of choosing may explain which world exists. If the choice is contingent, a theory promising reasons for all contingent facts must then address it. Saying that the choice is basic is a possible stopping point. It revises the promise; it does not fulfil it by repetition.

## Three ways the demand can be resisted

The first objection is that this makes completeness too demanding. No explanation could pass the test, because new questions can always be invented.

The objection would be decisive if my claim were that every explanation must answer every question. It is not. A good explanation can be deliberately narrow. Even a broad theory can specify which questions it aims to answer. The requirement applies when the theory itself accepts the relevant possibilities and promises that no contingent fact remains unexplained. Asking about a difference already within that scope does not enlarge the promise. It tests whether the promise survives a more exact description.

The second objection is that some apparent alternatives are not genuine. Exchanging two labels may not produce a different world. The symmetry test could then manufacture a problem out of notation.

This is right, and it points to a substantive escape. If Anna and Ben are different people who would receive different benefits, their victories are different outcomes. Renaming them consistently does not erase that difference. By contrast, two descriptions of the location of the whole universe might express no difference beyond a changed coordinate scheme. A theory that establishes this has removed a false contrast. It has not merely bundled two accepted outcomes together. The burden is to show that the alleged distinction lacks a corresponding difference in reality. Lack of an explanation for a difference does not by itself show that the difference is unreal, unless one has already justified a strong principle requiring every difference to be explained.

The third objection is deeper. Why should an unexplained contrast count as an unexplained fact? Perhaps the fact that Anna won is fully explained by the process, and “rather than Ben” simply imposes an optional demand. Every fact can have an explanation even if not every contrast does.

This is a coherent reading of a universal principle of explanation. It is also weaker than the promise that nothing about the actual result is left without a reason. The two readings should be kept apart. On the weaker reading, one and the same process can explain either outcome, with no basis for which one occurs. On the stronger reading, that remaining difference needs attention. Neither reading can acquire the advantages of the other merely by using the phrase “sufficient reason.”

The consequence is not verbal. A weak principle may support the conclusion that every event has a producing source. It does not, on that basis alone, exclude arbitrary selections among possible worlds, identify a uniquely preferred world, or show that all real differences reflect differences in their explanatory grounds. Those further conclusions require the stronger demand or some independent argument. The distinction therefore changes which metaphysical inferences are available.

## A modest conclusion with a sharp edge

There may be irreducible chance. There may be free choices for which no prior reason favours the selected option. There may also be fewer real possibilities than our descriptions suggest. None of these views is ruled out by the argument here.

What is ruled out is a particular shortcut to completeness. An explanation of why some acceptable outcome occurred cannot become an explanation of every detail merely by calling that outcome “the event.” An earlier selection can explain a later result while leaving its own contingency open. A theory can stop there, or it can argue that the alternatives were never real, or it can offer more explanation. It must make that choice openly.

The useful question is therefore not just whether a world has an explanation. It is which differences between possible worlds that explanation addresses. Once those differences are kept in view, a theory's stopping point becomes easier to see. We can then debate whether the remaining contingency is acceptable, instead of mistaking its disappearance from the description for its disappearance from reality.
