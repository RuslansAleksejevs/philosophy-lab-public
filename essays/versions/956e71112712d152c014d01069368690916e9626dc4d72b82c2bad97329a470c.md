# Losing the Concern, Keeping the Debt

Can someone stop having a moral reason by deliberately ceasing to care? The question is not whether she can stop feeling its force. People become indifferent, cultivate callousness, and sometimes welcome the loss of a scruple. The question is whether changing the psychology through which a requirement once moved her can remove its present authority.

A tempting argument against desire-based accounts is that an agent could otherwise escape morality by altering her desires. But that argument moves too quickly. An impermissible act can successfully change what someone subsequently ought to do. Destroying a borrowed instrument can make returning that instrument impossible, even while making compensation obligatory. Moreover, the person might retain blame for engineering her indifference even if she no longer has the original reason. Neither the badness of the change nor the survival of blame establishes the persistence of a reason.

I argue for a more discriminating test. Consider an unfulfilled interpersonal obligation whose performance remains available after the agent removes her concern. Compare the removal with a release granted by the person to whom performance is owed. Both may leave the agent equally indifferent, but only one changes the relationship that originally made performance due. This contrast gives us a reason to reject the assumption that a motivational condition necessary for acquiring a practical reason must remain necessary for retaining it. It does not establish a complete theory of moral reasons. It identifies a specific choice for theories that ground reasons in an agent’s present motivational psychology: abandon persistence, distinguish moral obligation from practical reason, or allow some reasons to outlast the motivations from which they arose.

## 1. The view under pressure

Bernard Williams’s opening distinction concerns two interpretations of claims that an agent has a reason to act. On the internal interpretation, an appropriate connection with the agent’s motives is required; an external interpretation lacks that condition (Williams 1981, opening passage). The relevant contrast should not be caricatured as one between doing whatever one currently feels like and obeying morality. A motivation-based theory may consider information, deliberation, commitments, and dispositions far beyond a passing appetite.

My target is the following restricted thesis, which I call present-base dependence:

> An agent has a practical reason to perform an action only if an appropriate route of sound deliberation from her present motivational set can support motivation toward that action.

This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient condition and not a theory of how strong reasons are. It permits unnoticed reasons and motivational conflict. A presently unmotivated debtor can still have a reason to repay if sound deliberation from her existing concerns could move her to do so. To test the thesis we therefore need more than distraction, weakness of will, or a momentary absence of feeling.

There is already a substantial problem here. Kent Hurtig considers whether an agent temporarily deprived of motivation retains reasons associated with his ordinary concerns. His examples include externally induced indifference to an opportunity; he also examines appeals to earlier motivations (Hurtig 2006, author manuscript, §V). The possibility of deleting an inconvenient concern is also not new. Peter Suber explicitly considers a self-modifying agent removing a second-order desire that produces guilt or hesitation (Suber 2002, §5).

The present argument concerns a different explanatory demand. It does not infer a surviving reason from the harm done to the altered agent, from future regret, or from the wrongness of altering her. It asks what has happened to an outstanding claim held by somebody else. That focus lets us distinguish the historical origin of a reason from the conditions under which the reason expires.

## 2. An obligation with an open exit

Mara accepts temporary custody of Leila’s emergency fund. Leila will request its return on Friday. Mara understands this arrangement, sincerely cares about Leila and about being trustworthy, and recognizes a reason to return the money. Assume that there is no defect in the arrangement: no coercion, deception, unjust purpose, or conflicting emergency. The money remains available, and returning it requires only a simple transfer.

On Thursday Mara undertakes a precise psychological intervention. It eliminates every element of her motivational set from which sound deliberation could support returning this money: the relevant affection, concern for trustworthiness, aversion to wrongdoing, and any instrumental concern that would independently favor return. She retains factual memory, ordinary practical competence, and the ability to make the transfer. She can pursue other projects coherently. She does not mistake the money for her own. There is no threatened sanction, reputational advantage, or future reward that would supply a replacement route. On Friday Leila requests her money.

This is an idealized case. Its purpose is to isolate a proposed dependence, not to predict a medical technology or describe ordinary depression. It stipulates selective motivational alteration while preserving agency and the available action. If such alteration is impossible in principle because every agent necessarily has a route to honoring an entrusted claim, that is a substantive escape from the case. It requires an account of that route, rather than a reminder that actual people usually retain overlapping concerns.

Compare a second case. Before Friday, Leila freely and competently gives Mara the money, releasing her from the obligation to return it. There are no creditors or other affected parties. Mara then undergoes the same intervention. Her resulting motivational profile can be the same in both cases. Her information about release differs, but by hypothesis neither information state activates a relevant concern. The practical opportunity is also the same: she can transfer the money back.

In the release case, the special reason to return the money as something owed has ended. Other reasons to help Leila might remain; they are outside the comparison. In the alteration-only case, the same special reason appears to remain. The money is still held on the original terms, the return is still possible, and Leila has done nothing to relinquish her claim. What has changed is Mara’s responsiveness to it.

The comparison does not show that all reasons are independent of motivation. It shows why these two changes should not be assigned the same normative effect. Leila’s release exercises a power over what is due to her. Mara’s intervention exercises a power over Mara’s mental life. From the fact that the latter power once controlled her responsiveness, it does not follow that it also controls what remains due.

## 3. Why persistence is more than disapproval

A defender of present-base dependence will ask whether this simply assumes that morality always supplies reasons. We should separate the steps.

First, the claim that the obligation persists has a relational justification. The arrangement enabled Mara to take custody without acquiring ownership. Treating her subsequent withdrawal of concern as terminating the obligation would make the continuation of Leila’s claim conditional on a decision wholly reserved to Mara. Yet this was not one of the terms on which custody was accepted. The purported exit would change the arrangement unilaterally while leaving Mara in possession of its benefit. There is therefore a direct explanation of why release matters and psychological alteration does not: release changes an authoritative term of the relationship; alteration changes neither the undertaking nor Leila’s control over the money.

This explanation is normative. It relies on the familiar judgment that an agent cannot cancel another person’s legitimate claim merely by changing her own attitude toward it. But it does not rely on the universal thesis that every moral consideration supplies a reason to every possible agent. Its application is narrower: an agent who already had a reason connected with a legitimate undertaking cannot terminate the undertaking merely by eliminating her route of responsiveness while keeping its benefit and her ability to perform.

Second, a present obligation supplies a present practical reason only if we accept an additional bridge. Call this local moral rationalism: where an agent remains able to discharge a legitimate obligation of this kind, the fact that performance is still owed counts in favor of performing it. This bridge is modest in scope but indispensable. Nothing in the meaning of “moral obligation” alone forces every metaethical theory to accept it.

There is a practical rationale for accepting the bridge. Leila’s request on Friday concerns what Mara can now settle. Returning the money would discharge the outstanding claim, whereas explaining the Thursday intervention would not. If the requirement supplies no reason at all for Mara’s present action, its normative significance has shifted entirely to others’ attitudes and actions: their criticism, protection of Leila, or recovery of the fund. Mara herself confronts no practical consideration in favor of making the transfer. That is a coherent account, but it concedes that the surviving obligation lacks the action-guiding authority for its bearer that ordinary repayment demands purport to have.

This is why merely preserving blame is insufficient. Suppose Mara’s intervention was culpable on Thursday. A tracing account can attribute Friday’s refusal to that earlier culpable choice. But it answers a backward-looking question: why may Mara be blamed? It does not answer the present question: does she have any reason now to return the money? Mara could acknowledge that her earlier self acted badly and still maintain, under present-base dependence, that no current reason favors repayment. Leila’s outstanding claim challenges that second assertion independently of the first.

Indeed, the persistence argument does not ultimately require culpable self-alteration. If a third party produces exactly the same motivational result, Mara may be less blameworthy or blameless. Yet while she remains the same competent custodian, the money does not become hers. This variant helps identify the ground of persistence. It is the unextinguished relationship, not a penalty imposed for choosing the intervention. Culpability brought the problem into view; it does not solve it.

## 4. The limited trilemma

We can now state the conflict precisely. Grant that selective alteration leaves no relevant deliberative route from Mara’s present motivational set. Three claims cannot all survive:

1. Present-base dependence is true.
2. The legitimate, still-performable obligation remains after alteration alone.
3. Local moral rationalism is true of that obligation.

The second and third claims yield a present practical reason to return the money. The first, together with the stipulated motivational result, excludes that reason. Nothing contradictory follows merely from motivation changing over time. The contradiction arises only when a specific account of reasons meets a specific account of persistence and a specific bridge between obligation and reason.

The first response is to deny persistence. Perhaps the obligation was itself conditional on Mara’s continuing concern. But why should the terms of custody include that condition? Mara’s original concern may explain why she undertook the arrangement or why the undertaking initially supplied her with a reason. Neither fact shows that Leila acquired only a claim revocable by motivational withdrawal. To infer the latter is to slide from a condition of entry into a condition of exit.

A second response preserves the obligation while rejecting local moral rationalism. Mara still morally owes the money but has no reason to return it. This answer should not be dismissed as incomprehensible. It gives morality a critical standpoint that need not supply reasons to every person it criticizes. Its cost is also exact: even someone who voluntarily entered a legitimate arrangement with a recognized practical reason can retain its obligation while losing every present reason to comply. Moral requirements then survive as standards without necessarily surviving as practical authority for their bearer.

A third response denies complete deletion. There may be universal concerns or constraints of rational agency that secure a route from any competent motivational set to repayment. But formal consistency alone will not identify Leila as the person to whom money must be returned. A route must contain enough substantive material to distinguish returning entrusted funds from pursuing Mara’s unrelated projects. If that material is essential to agency, the theory owes an argument for its necessity. If it consists in a concern that normal people happen to retain, it leaves the stipulated case unanswered.

Finally, a theorist can reject present-base dependence while retaining a historical connection to motivation. Mara’s earlier concerns helped establish a reason that remains until the underlying relationship is appropriately terminated. This is the possibility obscured when internal and external reasons are treated as exhausting every useful explanatory distinction. A reason can have a motivational entry condition without a continuously motivational retention condition.

## 5. What a historical repair must avoid

Appealing to history cannot mean that every abandoned desire continues to generate reasons. If Mara once wanted to learn the flute and now freely prefers painting, the former desire need not keep supplying a reason to practice scales. Nor should an irrational youthful undertaking remain binding simply because it was sincerely desired. History is not a storehouse of eternally authoritative motives.

A plausible repair must therefore track what the earlier motivation helped to establish. In the fund case, an authorized undertaking altered the relationship between two people. An abandoned hobby need not have done so. The relevant historical fact is not merely that Mara wanted something; it is that she assumed custody on terms that confer a continuing claim. Performance, release, invalidity, or other morally relevant changes can end that claim. Mere withdrawal of concern need not.

This proposal leaves an important explanatory question open. Perhaps the undertaking itself supplies the reason, and Mara’s earlier motivation merely explains her participation. In that case the historical repair ultimately supports an external account of this reason. Alternatively, a theorist might hold that the earlier motivational connection was genuinely necessary to the reason’s formation, while denying its necessity for continued possession. The case does not decide between those views. Its contribution is to expose their shared disagreement with a continuously present motivational requirement.

Nor does persistence imply that obligations survive every transformation. Death, loss of practical agency, destruction of the object, or justified transfer of responsibility may change what can now be required. The case deliberately preserves identity, competence, and performance. An account that treats the disappearance of a particular concern as itself the destruction of agency risks defining moral responsiveness into agency. An account that treats it as a relevant incapacity must explain why Mara can execute the transfer for a newly offered reward but cannot bear a reason to execute the same transfer as discharge of an existing claim. A distinction between ability to act and susceptibility to a particular reason is precisely what is at issue.

The point also does not authorize forced psychological restoration. Suber’s discussion makes clear that respecting self-modification and preventing harm to others raise distinct questions (2002, §§4–6). Mara could retain broad authority over her own mind while lacking authority to convert entrusted property into her own. Requiring return of money is not automatically requiring restoration of affection, guilt, or any particular inner character.

Moral authority is often examined at the point where an unconcerned agent first encounters a demand. The retention problem asks a different question: what can remove a reason that the agent already had? In the fund case, the answer depends on the continuing relationship and its legitimate modes of discharge. Losing the concern can explain why Mara no longer responds. It cannot by itself explain why Leila is no longer owed. A theory that identifies those two losses must either defend that identification or say plainly that what remains owed supplies its bearer with no reason to act.
