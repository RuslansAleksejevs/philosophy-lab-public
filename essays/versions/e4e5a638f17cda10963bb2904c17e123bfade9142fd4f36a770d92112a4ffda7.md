# What an Ending Can Explain

A historian writes that a municipal decree laid the foundation of a later surveillance system. The description may be true even if its authors intended only to improve tax collection. But consider the next sentence: the decree's foundational role explains why the council adopted it. Something has gone wrong unless a further argument connects the later system to the council's earlier deliberation. The problem is not simply that the historian has used a concept unavailable to the council. An unfamiliar concept can accurately describe pressures that affected people who lacked it. Nor is the problem that the historian knows the ending. Historical understanding often depends on that advantage. The problem concerns what the ending is being asked to explain.

I defend a constraint on retrospective historical explanation: an ending can help identify an earlier occurrence without thereby explaining its production. If an explanation uses a description made applicable by later events, the historian must distinguish the features doing the identifying from the features doing the explanatory work. When this distinction cannot be sustained, the historian must either supply a connecting mechanism or acknowledge a different explanatory target. This constraint applies to unintended structural processes as well as intentions. It is compatible with narrative explanation and does not demand that history reproduce an actor's vocabulary.

The proposed contribution is a diagnostic for a recurrent inferential error. A narrative can establish that an occurrence belongs in a history and then covertly treat that membership as explaining why the occurrence happened. I call this the transfer from selection to production. Its danger is greatest in accounts that expressly disclaim crude claims of foresight. One can deny that anyone anticipated the ending while still letting that ending organize an explanation in which the earlier development appears necessary. The diagnostic shows how to retain the organization while removing the illicit necessity.

## Retrospective truth and explanatory work

Danto's analysis establishes an essential starting point. Historical descriptions may depend on occurrences later than what they describe. He also distinguishes pursuing a project from achieving the result: the former need not become false when the latter fails. Both the legitimacy of retrospective description and this difference between pursuit and success are already explicit in “Narrative Sentences” (1962, pp. 154–155, 164–165). The question here is what follows when such descriptions occur inside explanations.

Consider a wholly hypothetical decree. In 1820 a council standardizes household registers to distribute local taxes. In 1850 a new government uses the surviving registers to identify dissidents. Assume that the records actually enable this use, and that the council neither anticipated it nor responded to actors secretly planning it. Three questions can then be distinguished.

Why did the council standardize the registers? Why did these records enable the later surveillance system? Why does the decree merit attention in a history of surveillance? The first asks about production; the second about a later consequence; the third about the historian's selection. The questions overlap in evidence, but answering one is not sufficient for answering the others.

Tax disputes might answer the first. The records' consistency and availability might answer the second. Their demonstrated use in surveillance might answer the third. A successful narrative can integrate all three answers. Trouble begins when it compresses them into “the needs of the emerging surveillance state explain the decree” without telling us whether those needs were operative in 1820 or arose three decades later.

The word “emerging” does not settle the issue. It may summarize an evidenced development already exerting pressure. It may instead project the later system backward, letting a destination stand in for the forces that produced an earlier step. In the latter case the claim's apparent plausibility comes from combining two true thoughts: the decree was adopted, and it eventually served surveillance. Their conjunction does not explain its adoption.

The error is subtler than a false statement of intention. A historian can write that the council did not know what it was helping to build, and still suggest that building it was the explanation of the council's conduct. That suggestion could be vindicated by a structural mechanism. It cannot be vindicated merely by the accuracy of the eventual description.

## A test using divergent continuations

The relevant diagnostic begins by fixing the explanandum more precisely than “this historical development.” Suppose the claim is about the production of the decree in 1820. Hold fixed the circumstances alleged to explain that production, including the actors' beliefs, institutions and available resources. Now compare two continuations of the story. In one, the registers later support surveillance. In the other, an accidental warehouse fire destroys them in 1849, before the later government can use them.

The decree's place in a history of surveillance changes dramatically between these continuations. On the assumptions of the example, however, its production does not. If a proposed explanation of adoption becomes unavailable solely because the later fire occurs, it has depended on something that distinguishes the decree's subsequent career rather than its earlier production. That dependence demands an explanation of its own.

This is a test of inferential dependence, not a request to imagine an entire alternative history. Its force is conditional: to the extent that an interpretation itself treats the later event as independent of the earlier producing conditions, it cannot also give that later event an indispensable role in explaining those same conditions. The historian need not establish that the two continuations are metaphysically possible duplicates. The initial claim supplies the local commitments being tested.

A verbal version of the test asks whether the account can replace “the foundation of surveillance” by “the standardization of household registers” while retaining its grounds for the council's action. If those grounds are tax disputes and administrative incentives, the explanation survives. If the only ground was that surveillance later required systematic records, it does not. The substitution does not ban the original phrase. It reveals whether that phrase identifies the thing explained or carries an unsupported explanatory inference.

The two versions check each other. Mere substitution can erase a relevant feature by changing the question. An explanation of why the decree was a foundation of surveillance obviously cannot discard surveillance. Divergent continuations expose the proper diagnosis: the account is explaining the decree's later status, not its earlier production. Conversely, an account can survive a superficial change of labels while continuing to smuggle the future into claims about earlier “needs.” Asking what changes when the continuation changes uncovers the remaining dependence.

The resulting rule has three parts. State which occurrence or status is being explained, and when it obtains. Separate the later-dependent description from the earlier features alleged to explain it. Then either show that those features retain their explanatory force across the relevant change of continuation, or explain why the changed continuation legitimately changes the target or the mechanism. This is a burden of articulation, not a complete algorithm for judging histories.

## Why the constraint follows

The argument depends on a modest principle about explanation. An explanation of why something occurred must distinguish the conditions relevant to its occurrence from the conditions under which we happen to select it for discussion. Selection can make a question pressing; it cannot by itself answer that question. A doctor may investigate a childhood exposure because a patient later becomes ill. The illness helps identify which exposure to investigate, but its occurrence does not, just by doing that, explain why the child was exposed. Historical explanation has the same inferential distinction even when its objects are more contested.

Suppose an earlier occurrence satisfies description D because a later outcome occurs. From the outcome we may infer, given the relevant connection, that D applies. If we then cite the applicability of D to explain the production of the earlier occurrence, we risk an explanatory circle: the outcome licenses the description, and the description makes the earlier occurrence look as though it happened for the sake of the outcome. What is missing is independent support for the producing relation. The diagnostic searches for that missing support.

This does not assume that explanations must appeal only to earlier events. Constitutive explanations need not describe causal succession. Institutional decisions can depend on contemporaneous rules, and an extended process can be explained partly by its later phases. The question is whether the features cited are relevant to the specified target. A later election may partly constitute a successful democratic transition. It cannot, solely by completing the transition, explain why an earlier crowd assembled.

Nor does the argument assume that outcomes can never be evidence of earlier causes. A later administrative practice may be excellent evidence of an otherwise undocumented prior plan. That evidential route differs from the route under criticism. If the later use makes the historian infer an earlier plan, the plan is the proposed explanation of adoption. Its existence remains an evidential hypothesis, to be compared with alternatives such as opportunistic reuse. The outcome does not serve as both evidence for the plan and unquestionable proof that the plan must have existed.

This distinction between evidence and explanatory ground also limits the divergent-continuations test. If the fire destroys the only evidence available to the historian, historical knowledge changes while the original explanation can remain correct. The test asks whether the purported ground of the earlier occurrence changes, not whether later researchers can still discover it. Losing evidence and losing a cause are different failures. An account should say which it claims.

## Structures, functions and collective projects

An objection now presents itself. The test appears to privilege the actor's perspective. Councillors can be moved by economic pressures they misunderstand, and institutions can develop capacities nobody intends. If every explanation must be recoverable in an agent's terms, much important history would disappear.

The objection defeats a stronger restriction than the one defended here. The operative requirement is independence from the irrelevant later continuation, not availability to the actor. Suppose the tax system rewards officials who produce comparable records, and promotion incentives predictably shape their choices. These incentives can explain standardization even if no councillor can articulate the mechanism. Their explanatory force survives the fire. Describing them with present-day social theory is therefore unobjectionable on the present criterion, although the theory still needs evidence.

A functional explanation also can pass. Suppose records that facilitate policing tend to be retained, whereas others are discarded. This selection mechanism can explain why the archive available in 1850 has its particular composition. Its target is survival and reproduction over an interval, not the initial creation of every document. The later practical use is relevant because it feeds back into decisions to maintain the institution. Once the feedback is dated and evidenced, the explanation has earned its reference to function.

The same mechanism may explain subsequent standardization. Officials in 1855 could revise registers because policing agencies now require it. It would then be correct to explain those revisions through the needs of surveillance. The account becomes misleading only if this valid explanation of later maintenance silently expands into an explanation of the institution's original birth.

This distinction matters for histories of capitalism, empire, bureaucracies and scientific disciplines. Such histories frequently trace long processes whose participants possess heterogeneous aims. They need not pretend that everyone pursued the eventual configuration. They do need to distinguish why some component was created, why it survived, why it spread, and why later agents transformed it. An ending may supply the subject of a history containing all these mechanisms. It cannot substitute for their differences.

There are also genuine long-term collective projects. A movement may explicitly seek a political order that takes generations to realize. The later outcome then helps us assess success, while the earlier publicly sustained goal helps explain action. Had the movement ultimately failed, its prior activity could still have been directed toward that goal. The test preserves its orientation toward the future without making successful realization a prerequisite of its earlier intelligibility.

## Does the test assume a neutral past?

A deeper objection concerns the identification of the supposedly fixed occurrence. Perhaps “standardizing household registers” is no more neutral than “laying the foundation of surveillance.” Historians constitute their objects through descriptions. Replacing one description with another may change the historical event itself, leaving no shared object whose production can be compared.

Roth challenges the idea that an ideal chronicle could start from events already individuated independently of descriptive schemes; he also rejects the demand that one privileged explanatory form govern historical inquiry (1988, pp. 3–4, 8–9). This objection prevents the diagnostic from relying on a uniquely given inventory of historical atoms. It does not prevent local comparison within a proposed account.

The historian who claims that the decree founded surveillance already commits to some relation between a decree and a later system. We can ask which records count as the decree, which votes brought it about, and which later uses connect it to surveillance without declaring these the only possible historical objects. The test holds fixed the account's own commitments for the purpose of checking an inference. If the author instead means by “the foundation” an extended complex comprising both the decree and its later appropriation, that answer is legitimate. It also concedes that an explanation of the complex is not automatically an explanation of why its earliest component occurred.

Narrative objects may therefore remain plural and revisable. What cannot remain indefinite is the object being explained at the point an inference is made. A single history might explain an administrative innovation, its transformation into surveillance infrastructure, and its later symbolic role. Object plurality makes such distinctions more important, not less.

Roth also allows assessment of narrative forms by the inquiries and solutions they support (1988, pp. 12–13). The present diagnostic is compatible with that modest ambition. It does not rank whole narratives by correspondence to a complete past. It identifies a reason to withhold acceptance of one explanatory transition, and directs research toward the evidence that could repair it: incentives, prior expectations, material affordances, selective retention or institutional feedback.

The test will sometimes produce an inconclusive result. We may not know whether a later use reveals a prior plan or represents a new appropriation. In that case “the decree later became useful for surveillance” may be established while “surveillance explains its adoption” remains unsettled. A history loses explanatory confidence but retains retrospective knowledge. This is a substantive epistemic distinction, not merely more cautious wording.

## What hindsight is for

One final worry is that these distinctions remove the main insight of historical narrative: seeing how apparently disconnected occurrences belong to a development. But the account leaves that insight intact. It denies only that belonging to a development is one uniform explanatory relation.

In the hypothetical case, hindsight reveals an important continuity: administrative solutions can create capacities available for later political appropriation. The council's motives do not exhaust the significance of its action. Yet the continuity can be understood more precisely once the difference between creation and appropriation is preserved. Tax collection produced the records; standardization made them useful beyond that purpose; a later regime selected and exploited those capacities. This account can explain something the councillors could not understand while refusing to make them instruments of an ending that had no operative role in their decision.

The practical result is a stronger use of hindsight. An ending tells the historian where to look, which earlier capacities became consequential, and which transformations require explanation. The historian then works backward epistemically while distinguishing the directions of the relations discovered. Later evidence can disclose an earlier cause; later conduct can realize an earlier project; later institutions can appropriate earlier materials. Each relation can support a narrative. None is interchangeable with the others.

Anachronism is consequently not exhausted by the use of historically unavailable words. An account using impeccable period vocabulary may still explain an earlier action through a significance it acquired only afterward. An account using modern concepts may accurately recover the mechanisms that produced it. The crucial question is what licenses the inference from description to explanation.

Historical distance gives us more descriptions of what happened. It does not give every description equal explanatory authority. Where an ending changes only why we select an earlier occurrence, we have learned its significance. Where it helps disclose how that occurrence was produced, we may have learned its explanation. Keeping those achievements distinct is what allows a history to possess both.
