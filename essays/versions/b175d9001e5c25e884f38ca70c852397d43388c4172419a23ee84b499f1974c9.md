# When Faith Cannot Be Recommended Unchanged

Trust can help make a proposition true. My confidence that a new association will work may lead me to join it, making possible the cooperation I expect. This is a powerful objection to the thought that responsible belief must always wait for a fact to exist independently of the believer. Yet it leaves a further question unanswered. If confidence helps make the association work, may I recommend the same confidence, expressed in the same way, to everyone involved?

Sometimes I may not. What my confidence helps produce may depend on someone else's continuing inquiry. If encouraging others to adopt my practical posture would eliminate that inquiry, the success of my present posture does not justify the invitation. The recommendation changes the conditions under which the belief was supposed to help make itself true.

I defend a limited claim. When an invitation to faith is justified by its prospective contribution to truth, its justification must concern the pattern of uptake being invited. Where that uptake would remove inquiry essential to the claimed contribution, the advocate must preserve or replace the inquiry, or narrow the invitation. This is a constraint on a particular argument for recommending belief. It is not a complete account of justified belief, nor a demand that somebody always disbelieve what others believe.

The distinction matters for understanding a tension within William James's defence of faith. James takes cooperation to illustrate the power of belief to create facts. W. K. Clifford, his famous opponent, explains how authority depends on continued questioning. Read together, they reveal a possibility that the opposition between faith and evidence tends to conceal: a person's freedom to proceed in confidence may depend on a division of inquiry that the person has reason to protect. Inquiry then belongs within the justification of shared faith, rather than entering only as an independent check upon its excesses.

## 1. What the historical dispute permits

James does not defend believing anything one wishes. In “The Will to Believe,” his central permission applies to options that are live, forced and momentous, and that cannot be resolved on intellectual grounds as he understands them. He acknowledges both psychological limits on voluntary belief and the proper role of detached judgment. His claim is that suspension is not always a neutral way to preserve access to truth. Waiting can close an opportunity as decisively as a negative judgment (James 1897/1912, §§I–IV, VII–VIII).

Section IX gives this thought a causal form. Confidence in another's friendliness can lead to an approach that brings friendliness into existence. In coordinated enterprises, participants' expectations can sustain the contributions on which success depends. These examples challenge a conception of the believer as someone whose only possible epistemic role is to register an independently settled fact. In some cases, forming the belief changes what there will be to register.

For present purposes I grant the strongest useful version of this argument: there can be a permission to adopt a belief in advance of ordinary evidence for its content when adoption appropriately helps bring that very content about. Whether this permission is epistemic, practical, or both remains disputed. My argument applies to anyone offering the truth-producing effect as a justification. It does not settle the dispute by stipulating the correct classification.

Clifford is a more interesting opponent than the claim that we should never act until certainty arrives. In section II of “The Ethics of Belief,” he expressly permits action on probabilities where the evidence does not yet justify belief, since action and observation can produce evidence for later belief. He also permits reliance on testimony. The recipient need not personally repeat every experiment. What matters includes the speaker's opportunity to know, competence, sincerity, and the practices through which results are tested (Clifford 1877, §II).

His discussion of the chemist is especially important. The authority of the result is sustained by people who question it. The recipient's ability to rely on another is therefore not an exemption of the result from inquiry. It is a way of relying on inquiry conducted elsewhere. Clifford's social picture is not simply an indefinitely repeated command to each person to start again alone.

These passages do not reconcile the authors. Clifford can maintain that all useful ventures should be undertaken as probabilistic action rather than belief. James can maintain that this division misses cases in which the believing attitude itself matters. But their accounts permit a common question: what has to remain in place for a particular venture in confidence to be responsible?

James's answer cannot plausibly be “nothing.” In the Preface to the collected essays, he advocates risk taken responsibly and hypotheses open to experience; his defence of public religious experimentation assumes tolerance and a fair field. The essay itself ends by respecting the freedom of those who choose to wait (§X). The interpretation most favourable to James therefore leaves ample room for continued inquiry. What it does not by itself specify is how a public invitation to faith must preserve the conditions on which the invitation's justification relies.

## 2. A confidence that changes its own background

Consider a hypothetical project to produce an accurate community reference work. It needs both contributors, who supply entries, and a checker, who compares uncertain entries with the underlying records. Trust can encourage contribution. Checking can identify mistakes that sincerity and enthusiasm do not remove.

Make the structure deliberately simple. Three people have enough time for one substantial role each. Two contributors and one checker are sufficient for an accurate completed work. Three contributors without a checker will produce a larger but unreliable work, because a known class of copying errors will remain undetected. One contributor and two checkers cannot complete the work in time. These are stipulations of the example, not empirical claims about actual reference projects.

Ben is prepared to contribute if Ada joins. Chen has committed to checking. Ada is deciding whether to join and finds confidence in the project's success necessary to undertake the work. Grant, for the sake of the argument, that a Jamesian defence of her confidence succeeds: by helping secure Ada's participation, her belief helps bring about the accurate work that it represents. The point is not to derive this permission from premises acceptable to every evidentialist. It is to ask what follows if the permission is granted.

Ada subsequently recommends that everyone display the same faith by putting all available time into contributions rather than verification. She offers the project's progress as evidence that confidence works. Chen follows her recommendation and stops checking.

The defect is not adequately described as insufficient sincerity or excessive optimism. Ada's initial confidence could have done precisely the good claimed for it. Nor does the defect arise because she lacked personal access to every record. Her reliance on Chen was part of a reasonable division of work. The defect is a change of scope. Confidence expressed through Ada's contribution was beneficial against a background that included Chen's checking. Confidence expressed through the practical posture now recommended to Chen removes that background.

The comparison is elementary:

| Practical pattern | Result stipulated in the case |
| --- | --- |
| Ada and Ben contribute; Chen checks | Accurate completed work |
| Ada, Ben and Chen all contribute; nobody checks | Completed work containing undetected errors |

The simplicity is useful. It prevents us from confusing the point with the discovery of an unexpected psychological law. A reason for adding a contributor to one arrangement is not automatically a reason for turning its checker into another contributor. The truth-making argument must follow the change in arrangement.

Crucially, Chen may already believe that the work will be accurate. Checking does not require believing that the project will fail, or even withholding belief in its success. It can be what makes confident expectation appropriate. The problematic recommendation therefore combines an attitude with a way of expressing it. An invitation simply to share Ada's confidence, while retaining Chen's checking, is untouched by the example.

This qualification is not an escape from the ethics of belief. James connects belief with conduct throughout his essay, and his causal justification depends on conduct through which confidence affects the world. Someone who cites those effects must identify the relevant expression of belief. “Confidence works” is too indeterminate when one expression recruits effort and another withdraws verification.

The lesson is not that each permission must remain beneficial if everyone exercises it identically. A permission need not be universalized in that manner. The lesson concerns an actual proposal to enlarge uptake. Ada chooses to recommend a changed pattern; she must defend that pattern, rather than borrow a justification from the one she is replacing.

## 3. What an advocate must show

An argument from self-fulfilment can be represented without assuming any particular theory of probability:

1. Adopting this belief, through the relevant activities, helps secure the truth of its content under specified conditions.
2. That connection supplies a reason for adopting it in those circumstances.
3. Therefore, these people should adopt it in the way now recommended.

The first two claims do not suffice for the third. The recommended uptake may change the conditions in the first claim. This is particularly easy to miss where the advocate relies on other people's checking as an unmentioned part of the environment.

The required comparison is between the prospective patterns relevant to the recommendation. The advocate need not calculate an effect for every imaginable audience. But she cannot establish the effect of a broad invitation by holding fixed an activity that the invitation is expected to stop. If checking matters to the work's accuracy, the relevant prospect is accuracy after the proposed change in checking.

This requirement has two applications that should be kept distinct. Inquiry may help produce the desired result: Chen's checking removes errors. Alternatively, inquiry may chiefly support the advocate's grounds for thinking that the venture remains viable. Periodic examination might reveal that a once effective method has ceased to work. Ending that examination need not immediately make the original belief false. It can instead remove the advocate's basis for continuing to recommend the method as truth-producing.

In the second case the conclusion is correspondingly weaker. Loss of a source of warrant is not proof of failure. There may be other adequate grounds, or the relevant conditions may be stable enough to require no further checking. The objection applies where the advocate knows, or has reason to recognize, that her continuing claim relies on the inquiry being withdrawn. She must find another basis, qualify her recommendation, or cease to offer that justification. A true outcome by luck would not supply the missing prospective argument.

The resulting requirement is conditional rather than expansive. It does not say that one must preserve every source of evidence, investigate forever, or maximize the community's total stock of truths. It says that one may not justify the uptake of a practice by a dependency that the expected uptake itself removes. Another reason could still support the belief or recommendation. The defective inference alone cannot do so.

This also explains the positive duty implicit in the case. If Ada continues to invite participation on the original ground, she must preserve the required function, arrange a workable substitute, or limit the invitation. She need not personally become the checker. Nor may she simply assign Chen an indefinite unpaid burden and call it epistemic necessity. The allocation of work requires its own practical and moral justification. What the argument rules out is treating the function as both indispensable to the appeal and dispensable in the resulting practice.

## 4. Does collective reasoning already solve the problem?

The underlying social insights are established. Hardwig argues that a recipient's reasons can depend on inquiry possessed by others (1985, pp. 335–338). Zollman's work explains why the distribution of investigation can affect a community's success, and why permanently preserving disagreement is not the aim (2010, introduction and conclusion). Neither dependence nor the epistemic value of distributed inquiry is my proposed discovery.

The closest positive comparison is Hayward's defence of collectively self-fulfilling optimism through team reasoning. He retains evidential requirements concerning feasibility, potential partners and altruism, and distinguishes self-fulfilment from merely obtaining further apparent evidence (Hayward, §§3.1–3.3). My question concerns the maintenance of those requirements: does the uptake being urged preserve the inquiry supporting the collective assessment? His account can accept this constraint; recognizing it does not refute his qualifications.

Still, “consider the collective” does not yet answer the maintenance question. A collective can be described too coarsely. If its members are represented only as potential supporters or opponents, the inquiry done by supporters disappears from view. The reference project's collectively successful arrangement contains different contributions. The choice is not simply between everyone having confidence and everyone waiting. It is between ways of organizing the activities through which confidence is expressed.

The proposed constraint therefore changes the content of the recommendation. Instead of “each of us should imitate the successful believer,” it permits “each of us should help sustain this arrangement, within which confidence and checking have different tasks.” This need not reduce the number of believers. It changes what participation asks believers to do.

The distinction also limits a familiar accusation: that questioning a cooperative project merely undermines the confidence required for success. Some questioning will indeed do that. But if the advocate's own warrant depends on the activity in question, reducing confidence is not sufficient to establish its dispensability. The inquiry may be part of what distinguishes justified confidence in this project from an equally contagious confidence in a defective one. The advocate cannot count its benefits when establishing the case and ignore its role when responding to its practitioners.

This is not a blanket defence of dissent. A critic must be able to identify a relevant function: detecting a kind of error, checking a material assumption, or maintaining a basis for the continuing appeal. Inquiry can be redundant, badly directed, excessively costly, or replaceable. What changes is the burden of explanation. Once the relevant dependence is established, the advocate must show how the function survives the proposed uptake, rather than treating the questioner as someone outside the project whose interference merely needs to be tolerated.

## 5. Four objections

**The proposal is only ordinary instrumental rationality.** There is indeed a simple instrumental structure: a reason for a policy must concern the policy actually proposed. The philosophical work lies in identifying what a truth-making defence of faith commits itself to. If prospective effects are invoked as reasons for belief, those effects cannot be counted selectively, ignoring the change in other people's epistemic activities. The constraint is internal to that defence, even though satisfying it requires practical organization. It does not first assume that non-evidential belief is wrong and then disguise the prohibition as a concern about institutions.

Its modesty is a strength, but also a limit on its significance. It supplies no new general account of rationality. It identifies an invalid transfer between two questions often compressed into one: whether confidence has a beneficial causal role in an existing arrangement, and whether recommending its practical expression more widely preserves that role. Distinguishing them explains how apparently successful faith can be an inadequate model for imitation without showing that the original believer was irrational.

**The demand for inquiry cancels the leap of faith.** This would follow if the advocate had to possess advance evidence sufficient to settle the outcome. That is not the requirement. Ada can remain uncertain whether the others will persevere. A venture can remain risky. What she cannot do is rely on checking in defending the venture while recommending that checking disappear. Recognizing a known dependency does not require certainty about all the venture's consequences.

Nor does the condition impose indefinitely many checks upon checks. It addresses a specified reason being used for a specified invitation. Ordinary reasonable reliance can support the existence of the needed activity. If the activity can be carried out under confidence, there need be no compulsory pocket of disbelief. The argument is compatible with fallibility and with a stopping point in inquiry.

**If preserving checking helps success, a team already has reason to preserve it.** Correct. The account predicts convergence with sound collective reasoning. It explains why that convergence matters for the public defence of belief. Preserving inquiry is not always an external moral restriction reluctantly placed on a beneficial epistemic venture; in these cases it is part of what makes the truth-making appeal available at all. An advocate who omits it has incompletely specified the venture she is defending.

This observation also prevents an overreaction. One might infer from the reference case that social faith always needs some people to adopt the contrary belief. That is false. What must be distributed is relevant work, not necessarily credence. A community of confident people can assign fallible, serious checks without pretending to doubt for the public good. It is the continuing activity, and its access to correction, that matters in the example.

**What if scrutiny itself destroys the good that faith creates?** Some relationships may be injured by repeated demands for assurance. The argument provides no universal licence to make those demands. Where intrusive checking is unnecessary, it supplies no protection. Where it is replaceable, a less destructive substitute may be appropriate. But suppose the very inquiry required to sustain the truth-making argument is also incompatible with the proposed form of faith. Then that particular argument cannot support that particular form of faith unchanged.

This may be an uncomfortable conclusion, but it is not a contradiction. Some valuable practices lack a justification of the proposed kind. Their defenders can offer different reasons, accept a more qualified confidence, or describe the risks more candidly. The possibility of a valuable good does not entitle us to count incompatible conditions as though they formed one feasible route to it.

## 6. Faith from within an arrangement

There are further reasons to deny that self-fulfilment by itself settles what we should believe. Langton already discusses harmful social self-fulfilment, while de Bruin examines accurate credibility assessments produced by damaging social conditions (Langton, §2; de Bruin 2025, §§3–4). The present problem arises earlier: even a benign belief can be recommended through a causal argument that changes its own background without acknowledging the change.

James is right to draw attention to truths that participation helps make possible. Clifford is right to draw attention to inquiry that makes reliance on others responsible. Neither insight tells every member of a community to adopt the same practical posture. A person's confidence may do valuable work precisely because it is situated alongside different work.

The resulting limit on public appeals to faith is precise. Show that the uptake being invited supports the truth-making effect being offered as its reason. If that effect depends on continuing inquiry, include the inquiry in the proposal, find a substitute, or restrict the appeal. A freedom to believe in a cooperative venture does not itself justify dissolving the division of work that sustains it. Sometimes the proper expression of shared faith is to keep checking.
