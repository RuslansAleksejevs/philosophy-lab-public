# Can Tomorrow Cancel Yesterday's Survival?

An object can acquire a new description because of something that happens later. A letter becomes the last letter someone ever wrote; an uneventful meeting becomes the beginning of a friendship. But can something later determine whether an object had already survived an earlier transition? If a person was successfully reconstructed on Monday, could the activation of a second reconstruction on Tuesday make it false that the first reconstruction was ever that person?

This is not a question about changing an already fixed past. A theory may assign a single, unchanging truth value to the entire history and still make Monday's identity depend on Tuesday's events. The issue is explanatory: whether facts about a completed interval suffice for persistence through that interval. I will call the requirement that they do *completed-persistence stability*.

I defend a restricted conclusion. In a class of delayed-replication cases, completed-persistence stability conflicts with the combination of two attractive claims: a sole adequate successor preserves identity, and simply being activated first cannot privilege one otherwise equally adequate successor. The conflict exposes a choice that talk of “nonbranching continuity” can obscure. A theory must sometimes let the future defeat an earlier identity, give temporal priority constitutive force, or deny that sole adequate continuation guarantees identity. There is no general solution consisting merely in checking whether branching has happened yet.

The philosophical point is not the familiar observation that identity cannot branch. It is that a rule introduced to prevent branching must also specify when its exclusion of competitors is evaluated and why that temporal boundary has authority.

## 1. Persistence over an interval

Suppose two possible histories agree in all the physical, psychological, causal, and institutional facts up to the end of Monday. They include the same relevant origin, the same preservation processes, and the same present competitors. They differ only in whether a stored record is activated on Tuesday. The claim at issue is that the Monday successor is numerically identical with the original person.

Completed-persistence stability says that this claim has the same truth value in both histories. This is a supervenience requirement across histories, not a requirement that an observer can determine identity on Monday. Relevant facts could be unknown or enormously difficult to interpret. Nor does it demand that identity depend only on the intrinsic qualities of two endpoints. Their causal connection, intervening history, and surrounding social practices may all matter. The restriction concerns later differences alone.

For the comparison, we need not assume that the very same person literally occupies two possible worlds. We can designate the original by its origin and the successor by the particular reconstruction process. Corresponding claims in histories with matching prefixes can then be compared. “The original” must not covertly mean “the person whose whole life ultimately includes Tuesday's successor.” Such a description would build the disputed future dependence into the selection of the subject.

Why prefer stability? Because completed persistence ordinarily explains the completion of an object's history so far. If a tree survived last winter, the later production of a genetically identical sapling should not determine whether that winter belonged to the tree's life. The sapling might reveal that our earlier account of the process was mistaken; that is different from its later existence constituting the earlier failure of survival. More generally, a theory owes an explanation when a fact introduced solely to settle the continuation of a career also settles whether an already completed part of that career existed.

This motivation is defeasible. Some objects are selected by their entire histories: the longest uninterrupted performance, for instance. Their boundaries legitimately depend on later events. Stability is proposed for persistence claims about objects designated without such future-dependent selection. Showing that some career descriptions are global is therefore insufficient to reject it. One must argue that the relevant ordinary object's individuation is global too.

The concern has clear precedents. Gustafsson explicitly considers identity's dependence on later connections and discusses restricting admissible connections to stages without temporal gaps as a response (2019, author PDF pp. 16–17). His analysis also includes asynchronous replication. Thus neither future dependence nor delayed duplication is a new objection here. My aim is to isolate the role of temporal priority in the resulting choice, using a deliberately spare case.

## 2. The delayed competitor

Imagine a process that destroys an original person, O, while preserving two equally complete records, A and B. A reconstruction using either record would reproduce the same relevant psychological organization by the same kind of causal procedure. The records are physically separate but otherwise equal in the respects the proposed continuity criterion recognizes. Neither dormant record is itself a person. Nothing depends on a claim that such technology is possible in practice; the argument applies conditionally to theories allowing this sort of preservation.

In history One, record A is activated on Monday, producing person A-person. Record B remains dormant and is eventually destroyed. On the account under examination, this is successful survival: O is A-person. There is a stored alternative, but no second actual successor.

In history Two, everything through Monday is the same, including B's existence and condition. On Tuesday, a later decision results in its activation, producing B-person. A-person and B-person subsequently coexist, lead separate lives, and are numerically distinct. In particular, neither is a mere later stage of the other: they arise independently from O's records.

Consider three commitments.

**Sole-successor sufficiency:** In history One, the relevant continuity to A-person, with no other actual successor, suffices for O's identity with A-person.

**Completed-persistence stability:** Since histories One and Two have the same prefix through Monday, O is A-person in history Two as well.

**Activation-order neutrality:** Where two successors have the same kind and degree of continuity from O, and neither derives from the other, the mere fact that one is activated earlier does not make that one, exclusively, identical with O.

The third commitment concerns numerical identity, not whether both successors have equal welfare, rights, or claims to an inheritance. It also excludes cases where waiting damages a record or the earlier activation creates a causally superior continuation. We hold those differences fixed. The only proposed advantage is being first.

The conflict is immediate. Sufficiency gives O's identity with A-person in history One. Stability carries that identity into history Two. Neutrality then excludes the asymmetric verdict that A-person alone is O. Since the two successors are otherwise equally qualified, retaining A-person's identity requires extending the same verdict to B-person. But numerical identity is transitive and symmetric: if both are O, they are each other. They are not. The three commitments cannot jointly govern this case.

Notice what has not been assumed. Continuity alone was not taken to imply identity in every history. It suffices only in the stipulated one-successor history. Nor was simultaneous branching assumed. The later competitor is precisely what makes stability relevant. Finally, the contradiction is not generated by a mere possible competitor. The alternative record exists in both histories. What differs is whether a second person eventually becomes actual.

Someone who thinks the dormant record already defeats survival rejects the first commitment as applied here. That is a coherent exit, but it expands the relevant competition from actual persons to preserved capacities for producing persons. The account then needs to say which capacities count and when they cease to matter. Destroying a backup may acquire identity-making significance even when nothing changes in the surviving person. This is a distinct metaphysical cost, not a successful application of the original sufficiency claim.

## 3. Three ways to pay

Rejecting stability lets a complete history determine whether Monday's reconstruction belongs to O's life. There is no logical impossibility in this. History Two contains facts that history One lacks, and the theory may make identity depend on them. An eternalist need not describe Tuesday as reaching backward to alter Monday. The two whole histories simply contain different identity facts.

But the explanatory question remains. Why does an independently activated person on Tuesday defeat a relationship completed on Monday, when the Monday person's constitution and causal ancestry are unchanged? “Because identity cannot branch” does not yet answer it. Identity's logic excludes identifying O with both. It does not itself decide between denying both identifications and retaining the first. Choosing the former requires a substantive account of why the complete set of successors, however late they appear, has priority over completed persistence.

Rejecting activation-order neutrality offers a second response: the first adequate successor is O, and any later reconstruction is a new person. This rule can preserve Monday's identity in every extension of the history. It treats the original identity as unavailable once a successful continuation has occurred. The later successor's equally good resemblance and ancestry cannot reclaim it.

That position should not be dismissed as a mere verbal trick. Temporal order can be constitutive in other domains. A race winner is determined partly by arrival order, and an appointment may go to the first qualified applicant. But these examples work because winning and appointment are roles governed by rules that privilege order. They do not show that being a particular person has the same structure. If priority constitutes personal identity, the account needs a reason appropriate to persistence.

One candidate reason is that continuation must be settled as the world develops. The first adequate successor supplies a settled bearer of the identity, whereas no such bearer existed earlier. This explains priority by stability itself. It is a coherent package rather than an independent proof: those who prize completed persistence may have reason to accept the accompanying temporal asymmetry. The package predicts that a tiny delay can decide identity even when it changes no degree of psychological or physical continuity. Its explanatory ambition should be stated plainly.

Rejecting sole-successor sufficiency is the third response. A unique reconstruction may preserve memory, character, and information without preserving the original. Or the presence of the dormant alternative may make identity indeterminate. Either position avoids the inference used in the argument. It also prevents a familiar inference in defenses of reconstruction: that because there is exactly one sufficiently continuous successor, nothing further could be required for survival.

These responses are not necessarily equally plausible. The argument establishes the need for a choice, not a ranking independent of one's wider account of persons. Its value is diagnostic. A defender of a nonbranching condition should specify whether it excludes all eventual competitors, excludes only competitors already present, or treats unexercised capacities as competitors. Those are different positions with different consequences.

## 4. Does calling persons stages solve the problem?

A fourth possibility changes what persistence claims mean. Sider's stage view analyzes temporal predication through relations to temporal counterparts rather than ordinary numerical identity between stages at different times (1996, sections 1–3). Such an account can allow a relation from O's stage to both successors without making those successors numerically identical. It therefore need not accept the identity interpretation used above.

This response is outside the stated three-way conflict, because the conflict concerns strict identity. It is nevertheless philosophically informative. A relation suited to explaining psychological continuation can preserve completed connections and recognize later branching, provided it is not required to be numerical identity. The difference between continuity and identity is doing real work.

One should consequently resist an easy equivocation in presenting a solution. Saying that O continued into A-person and B-person may describe branching continuity. Saying that O is numerically each successor asserts something incompatible with their distinctness. A theory can defend the first claim and reinterpret ordinary persistence language accordingly. It cannot preserve all the original commitments simply by using the same word for the new relation.

The same caution applies to complete temporal careers. A theory may count two careers overlapping before their divergence. This need not deny the reality of the shared earlier process, but it changes the individuation of the objects undergoing it. The prefix can be unchanged while the number of complete careers differs. What cannot be inferred without argument is that a difference in the number of those careers changes the answer to an ordinary claim about which locally designated object already persisted. The relation between career counting and ordinary individuation is a substantive bridge, not a free consequence of temporal extension.

## 5. Boundaries of the argument

The strongest objection is that neutrality simply demands too much. A-person already lives as O when B-person appears; that fact itself distinguishes the two. But using A-person's prior identity as the distinguishing fact explains why neutrality fails only after choosing stability and sufficiency. It does not refute the incompatibility. It endorses the priority solution and identifies its basis. The remaining issue is whether chronological precedence, through its power to settle identity, may outweigh equal continuity.

A different objection grants the argument but calls it an instance of the familiar reduplication problem. That is substantially right about its materials. The delayed case matters because it separates temporal stability from a broader requirement that identity depend only on two relata. One can permit surrounding facts, present competitors, and causal history to affect identity while resisting dependence on later additions. Conversely, rejecting the broad intrinsicness requirement does not by itself justify every dependence on the future. The narrower distinction makes a theory's commitment visible.

The case also excludes backward causation. If Tuesday's activation changes the physical facts on Monday, the histories no longer share the required prefix. Likewise, deterministic laws may prohibit two complete histories with exactly the same entire past and different futures. The argument is conditional on an admissible space of histories allowing the stipulated divergence, whether because the laws permit it or because the account of persistence is being tested across a broader modal range. It is not a theorem about every physical world.

Finally, no claim has been made that personal identity exhausts what matters in survival. Equal concern for the successors can be justified even by a theory that identifies O with neither. Practical concern might therefore be more stable than identity itself. That is a reason to distinguish questions, not to hide the metaphysical choice behind a practical verdict.

The demand for a nonbranching identity relation is unavoidable. The demand that its verdict await every possible later competitor is not. A criterion of persistence must explain where it draws the temporal boundary of competition. Delayed replication shows why that boundary cannot be supplied by identity's logic alone: preserving completed survival gives temporal priority a role, while refusing temporal priority makes earlier survival vulnerable to later history unless unique continuity was insufficient from the start.
