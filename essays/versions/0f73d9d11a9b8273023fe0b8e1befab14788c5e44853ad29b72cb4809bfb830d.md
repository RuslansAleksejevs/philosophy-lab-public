# Off the Map: Unawareness, Obligation, and the Duties That Survive It

**Abstract.** Work on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility rests on an assumption
it has never isolated: that whatever an agent fails to be aware of is a proposition she was in
a position to entertain. Formal epistemology recognises a second failure — *unawareness*, the
absence of an item from the agent's space of possibilities — which is not a doxastic deficiency
at all. I give a criterion for the notion of representability this requires, argue that a
principle I call **Ought Implies Representability** governs the deliberative 'ought', and show
that it generates a sceptical problem at exactly the frontier where the gravest novel harms
occur. The problem is solved by observing that options are individuated more finely than
requirements: unawareness of an option rarely dissolves obligation, it *coarsens* it. Coarse
requirements are, in the semantics of deliberative questions, the *partial answers* that survive
when a presupposition of the fine-grained question fails — which also disarms the duality
objection that a lapse of obligation yields an equally inaccessible permission. Because the only
requirements that bind a genuinely unaware agent are *de dicto*, and because compliance with a
*de dicto* requirement is not first-personally verifiable, frontier ethics must be institutional
rather than substantive. A residue of cases remains in which nothing binds; I argue that this
residue is real, that its size is an empirical matter, and that it is correctly classified as
tragedy rather than fault.

## 1. Two failures

Nadia is a hospital pharmacist. Consider two ways her work can go wrong.

**Sign.** She reconstitutes a drug in the usual way. The vial carries a label: *do not warm
above 25°C*. She does not read it, warms the solution, and a patient is harmed. Nadia did not
know that warming was dangerous. But the thought *this might need to be kept cool* was available
to her; the evidence was in her hand; had she raised the question she would have known how to
settle it, and would have settled it correctly.

**Tubing.** In a different decade she prepares neonatal infusions using the standard giving set.
The set is licensed. Its material has passed the toxicological screen of the day: the protocol
exposes test animals to the highest dose the assay will carry, finds no observable effect, and
divides by a safety factor of one hundred to fix the human limit.[^1] Years later it emerges
that a plasticiser in the tubing harms neonates — but only at low chronic exposure, and only
during a narrow developmental window, so that the high-dose screen was not a weak test of the
hazard but a test of something else. Nobody in 1975 — not Nadia, not the manufacturer, not the
regulator, not the toxicologist who designed the screen — entertains the possibility that a
substance harmless at high acute dose might be harmful at low chronic dose. That possibility is
not one they consider and reject. Their science has no place for it: the governing assumption,
which every calculation in the field embodies, is that effect is monotone in dose.

The two cases differ in a way that matters. In *Sign* there is an untaken precaution. In
*Tubing* the precaution was taken; what failed was the precaution's design, which presupposed a
carving of the possibilities from which the actual hazard was absent. Nadia's reasoning is
impeccable given her repertoire, and so is everyone else's. The repertoire is the problem.

English describes both cases by saying Nadia was "unaware" of something, and the philosophical
literature on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility follows English. That literature
is large and sophisticated, and it is built around failures of the first kind. Its central
puzzle is the regress generated by tracing: if blameworthy ignorant wrongdoing requires culpable
ignorance, and culpable ignorance requires a culpable "benighting act", and culpability for that
act requires awareness in turn, then, as Rosen puts it, "the only possible locus of original
responsibility is an akratic act" (Rosen 2004: 307, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). The
responses to this regress — weakened internalism, epistemic-vice views, quality-of-will views,
capacitarianism — disagree about almost everything except the shape of the failure they are
theorising.

My claim is that *Tubing* has a different shape, that the difference is structural rather than
one of degree, and that seeing it changes what we should say about obligation and not merely
about blame.

## 2. The Propositional Assumption

Start with how the literature fixes its terms. As Yates's survey records, "recent theorists of
the epistemic condition take true belief to be necessary and sufficient for awareness, and they
identify ignorance as the lack of true belief, the opposite of awareness (Peels 2010; Rosen
2008; Rudy-Hiller 2017)" (Yates n.d., §1). Awareness is thus *defined* as a doxastic state, and
its complement is a negative condition. Call this:

> **The Propositional Assumption (PA).** The relevant epistemic failures are failures with
> respect to contents the agent could have entertained: false belief, missing belief, or
> suspension.

Two clarifications about the charge. First, PA is a frame, not a doctrine anyone defends.
Second — and here I correct an overstatement it would be easy to make — the literature does not
lack a place for unawareness. Its official definition of ignorance is negative, so the unaware
agent trivially counts as ignorant. The complaint is not that there is no cell but that
unawareness is *subsumed* rather than distinguished, and that the theories built on the
subsumption go wrong when the subsumed case is the actual one.

They do go wrong, and in every position on the map.

*Volitionists* require an occurrent, *de dicto* belief that the act is wrong; ignorance is
culpable only if it traces to an act performed "in full awareness" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). Both
the missing belief and the benighting act are specified by contents the agent could have
entertained. *Weakened internalists* substitute dispositional for occurrent belief, where a
dispositional belief is one "disposed to be occurrent under certain conditions" (Yates n.d.,
§1) — which presupposes that the content is in the repertoire, waiting. *Epistemic-vice*
theorists locate culpability in overconfidence, dogmatism and incuriosity, on the ground that
these explain a failure to respond to challenges the agent encountered; a challenge one cannot
formulate is not a challenge one dismisses. *Quality-of-will* theorists hold that an act can
express contempt for another's standing without any *de dicto* moral belief, but the expression
runs through the agent's take on what she is doing, and Talbert and Harman accordingly retain a
*de re* awareness requirement (Talbert 2013: 242; 2017a: 53; Harman 2011: 465; 2015: 67, cited
in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.3). Angela Smith's answerability view is the strongest candidate here,
since on it failures of factual awareness "sometimes reveal inadequate moral concern and thus
are blameworthy" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.3, on A. Smith 2005 and 2017); Yates classes her under
the analysis of ill will as "objectionable evaluative attitudes" (Yates n.d., §2.c.i). An
attitude, though, is expressed toward something the agent's mind has hold of.

*Capacitarians* come closest to breaking free, since they deny that awareness is required. But
their disjunctive condition is that agents "should and could be aware of [relevant
considerations] given the available evidence, the opportunity to adequately process it, and
their cognitive capacities" (Sher 2009: 87, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.4). Evidence, here,
is evidence *for* something. A body of physical signals counts as available evidence for an
agent only relative to hypotheses she can raise; the low-dose studies of the 1990s are not
evidence available to Nadia in 1975 in any sense that could ground a should-have-known claim,
even though the molecules were there.

Notice, finally, that the requirement most obviously relevant to *Tubing* — what Rudy-Hiller
calls "awareness of alternatives" — is formulated by Levy as the demand that agents have
"epistemic access to a range of alternatives", such that "they can only appropriately be blamed
for performing an action if they believed that alternatives were available to them, and
understood the significance of these alternatives" (Levy 2011: 111, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022,
§1.1). That is a belief condition on an alternative already in view. It says nothing about
alternatives that are not in view at all.

## 3. Unawareness, formally

The distinction I need is standard elsewhere. In the logic of general awareness of Fagin and
Halpern, awareness is a syntactic operator independent of the knowledge operator: "implicit
knowledge is the standard (S5) notion of knowledge; explicit knowledge amounts to implicit
knowledge and awareness" (Halpern and Rêgo 2006: 2). Semantically, *A<sub>i</sub>φ* holds at a
state just in case φ belongs to the set of formulas the agent is aware of there, and explicit
knowledge is *X<sub>i</sub>φ ⇔ K<sub>i</sub>φ ∧ A<sub>i</sub>φ* (2006: §2). In the decision-
theoretic literature the same notion is glossed as a "lack of conception rather than a lack of
information" (Schipper 2014: 1, quoted in Roussos forthcoming, §1).

The payoff of this architecture is that awareness and belief are orthogonal. An agent can stand
in the knowledge relation to a content while failing to be aware of it. Unawareness is not a
false belief, a missing belief, or a suspension; it is not a position in doxastic space but
absence from the space.

Two features of unawareness do the work that follows. Both concern the agent's access to her own
condition, and they pull in opposite directions.

> **Opacity.** No agent explicitly knows, of any particular content, that she is unaware of it.

> **Transparency.** An agent may nevertheless know that there is something she is unaware of,
> without any witness to the claim.

Halpern and Rêgo state Opacity for models in which awareness is "generated by primitive
propositions", where an agent is aware of a formula iff she is aware of every primitive
proposition in it: in such models "it is impossible for an agent to know that he is unaware of a
specific fact" (2006: 2). That class will not do for my purposes, because Halpern and Rêgo
themselves observe that in it, under quantification, "the formula A<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x)
is valid, which does not seem to be reasonable in many applications" (2006: 7). *De dicto*
awareness of one's own unawareness would then be a triviality rather than something an agent can
lack. So I state Opacity for the class they recommend instead, in which awareness is only
*weakly* generated by primitive propositions. It survives there.[^2]

Transparency is their positive result: *K<sub>i</sub>(∃x(A<sub>j</sub>x ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>x))* "is
consistent and … can be true at state *s* even though there might be no formula ψ … such that
*K<sub>i</sub>((A<sub>j</sub>ψ ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ))*" (2006: §3); the purely self-directed form
*X<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x)*, "which says that agent *i* (explicitly) knows that there
exists a formula of which he is not aware", is also in the language (2006: 2). Their
illustration is exact for my purposes: a physician referring a patient to an oncologist "knows
that an oncologist is aware of things that could help the patient's treatment of which he is not
aware", yet "is unlikely to know which specific thing he is unaware of … (if he knew which one
it was, he would not be unaware of it!)" (2006: 2).

One caution, which I owe the reader before leaning on any of this. The bare self-directed form
is cheap. Quantification in this logic ranges over the quantifier-free sentences of the language
(2006: §3), an infinite set; so for any agent whose awareness set is a proper subset of it at
every state she considers possible, *∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x* is true throughout and
*K<sub>i</sub>∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x* holds automatically. What the formalism establishes is a
*consistency* result — there is no logical barrier to knowing one's repertoire is incomplete
without knowing where — and not the substantive premise an agent actually needs, which is that
she is missing something *relevant to this decision*. That premise is not a theorem. It has to
come from induction on the history of her field, and §6 is where I go to get it. The formal
result licenses the shape of the duty; it does not supply its occasion.

Opacity and Transparency are compatible, and their joint truth is the structural fact this essay
turns on. An agent can know, with full justification, that her repertoire is incomplete, while
being constitutively unable to say where. She is not in the position of someone who knows there
is a sign she has not read. She is in the position of someone who knows only that there are
kinds of sign.

## 4. What "representable" means

The argument to come needs a notion of an option's being available to be entertained, and that
notion had better not be left to intuition, because almost every verdict below turns on where
its boundary falls.

> **Representability.** An act-description φ is *representable* for *S* at *t* iff *shall I φ?*
> is a question *S* can pose and treat as answerable: iff, from her present repertoire, she can
> say what would count as settling it.

And the criterion that makes this testable:

> **The Revision Criterion.** A gap in *S*'s option space is a failure of representability, and
> not merely of attention, iff closing it requires *awareness revision* — introducing a new
> proposition, drawing a new distinction, or correcting a logical relation *S* treats as fixed
> — rather than a redistribution of confidence over propositions *S* already recognises.

The vocabulary is Roussos's. He separates awareness revision, "the formation of a new subjective
awareness state, or space of subjective possibilities", from belief extension and belief
revision, the latter being the only one that "involves … evidence about what is the case"
(Roussos forthcoming, §1). Within awareness revision he distinguishes three kinds: *expansion*,
recognising that one's set of possibilities was not exhaustive; *clarification*, recognising
that its members were not mutually exclusive; and *refinement*, introducing a finer partition
inside one already recognised (§2).

Two readings of representability have to be excluded, and the criterion excludes both.

The **thin** reading says that φ is representable if the agent possesses the concepts in φ and
could combine them. On that reading *Tubing* evaporates: Nadia has TUBING, PLASTIC, DISSOLVE,
CONTAMINATE, DOSE and EFFECT, so *might something come out of the tubing?* is within
combinatorial reach, and the case is ordinary ignorance with a heavy evidential burden. I grant
the inference and deny the premise. Possessing the constituents of a question is not possessing
the question, because concepts are not free-floating: they are held under commitments about how
they combine. *Might a dose too low to register in the screen matter more than a dose high
enough to register?* is not a recombination of DOSE and EFFECT; it is the denial of a relation
under which Nadia's field holds them. This is Roussos's clarification case exactly. Naledi could
combine RAIN and SUN before her sun shower; what she could not do was entertain their
conjunction, because for her "RAIN and SUN were genuinely exclusive and exhaustive options
before" (§2). Clarification, he insists, is not reducible to recombination, because it "involves
changes to one's conceptual understanding such that one better understands what is necessarily
false of the world" (§2).

The **thick** reading says that φ is representable only if the question would actually arise for
the agent in her situation. That collapses representability into salience and excuses everyone
who simply failed to think of something — including Nadia in *Sign*, for whom *is this
heat-sensitive?* did not arise either. The criterion blocks this, because the test is
answerability, not occurrence. Nadia in *Sign* can say exactly what would settle her question:
read the label. Her failure is inattention. Nadia in *Tubing* cannot say what would settle hers,
because the settling procedure available to her — the screen — is one whose design already
embodies the assumption at issue.

Three consequences are worth flagging now. First, representability is relative to a repertoire
and therefore to a time and an agent; every verdict below is conditional on a repertoire
ascription, and I will say which one. Second, the formal models do not supply this criterion.
They individuate awareness relative to a fixed language, and the phenomenon I care about is the
growth of the language; note 1's restriction of Opacity to a model class is a claim about models,
not about minds. Third, the three kinds of awareness growth are not normatively on a par.
Refinement gaps typically leave a coarser representable requirement in place — if the missing
option is *use glass tubing*, the coarser *find out whether the tubing matters* is usually
representable, and then §7 below binds the agent to it. Expansion and clarification gaps often
leave nothing coarser. *Tubing* is constructed as a clarification case for precisely this
reason: the coarse requirement *test the tubing* was discharged.

## 5. Ought implies representability

> **Ought Implies Representability (OIR).** If *S* deliberatively ought to φ, then φ is
> representable for *S* at the time of deliberation.

Two clarifications. "Ought" here is the *deliberative* ought — the one that answers the question
an agent asks in deliberation and grounds accountability when she does not comply. It is not the
objective or fact-relative ought that ranks acts by their upshots, which is addressed to no one
and is not subject to OIR.[^3] And OIR constrains requirement, not evaluation: *Tubing* is a bad
outcome, the glass-lined line would have been better, and none of that is in dispute.

The case for OIR is that a requirement failing it cannot play the role requirements play. A
requirement is something that can be put to the agent; that she can comply with *as such*; and
whose violation licenses the demand *why did you not?* An unrepresentable requirement fails all
three. It cannot be put to her, since putting it to her would consist in making her aware of it.
She cannot comply with it as such, since compliance would be accidental. And *why did you not use
a glass-lined line?* has, in 1975, no answer that is not a report of the state of the art —
which is to say, no answer that is about her.

I should say plainly what this argument is. It is the guidance argument for perspectivism about
the deliberative ought, and I claim no credit for its form. The literature runs it via credit:
on Lord's thesis, "if you deliberatively ought to φ, then it must be possible that you perform
this required action in a way that is creditable to you" (Lord 2015, 2017b, as reported in
Vollmer 2026, §2), and Lasonen-Aarnio's feasibilism rests on the premise that "it cannot be by
mere luck that one complies with the deliberative norm" (Vollmer 2026, §2). My three clauses are
that argument's three moments. What I add is a *new way for its antecedent to fail*.

That matters, because the failures this literature discusses are of two other kinds. One is
ignorance of a fact; the other is inability. Vollmer's organising case is a doctor who is
"unable to give drug A" and does not know it (2026, §2, reporting Way and Whiting 2017). The
possibility that an option is fully within the agent's power, fully consistent with her values,
and simply not in her space, does not appear; when Vollmer touches something adjacent — plans so
complicated one "cannot even grasp" them — he observes that "in those circumstances, it is
usually transparent to the subject that they are unable to intend such an ungraspable plan"
(2026, §3). Opacity says that for unawareness this is exactly what is not so.

Three distinctness claims, of descending strength.

**OIR is not *ought implies can*.** In *Tubing*, Nadia *can* use a glass-lined line: she has the
hands, the training and the opportunity, and suitable glassware is in the cupboard. On any
ability-plus-opportunity analysis the antecedent of OIC is satisfied, so OIC imposes no bar.
What Nadia lacks is not the ability to perform the act but the ability to pose it. One could
resist by reading "can" as *can do intentionally, as the upshot of deliberation*; I grant that
this reading covers the case, and note only that it is not the reading at work in debates about
determinism and alternate possibilities, and that its consequences have not been drawn.

**OIR is weaker than Kiesewetter's principle, and that is its advantage.** Kiesewetter holds
"that a subject only deliberatively ought to φ if they are in a position to justifiably believe
that they ought to φ" (Vollmer 2026, §3). An agent who cannot represent φ is in no position to
justifiably believe that she ought to φ, so the principle entails OIR. I accept the entailment
and deny that it makes OIR redundant. Kiesewetter's principle is an epistemic-access condition
on the *normative* fact, and it is controversial for a familiar reason: it appears to excuse
the agent who has the option in view but cannot see why it is required, and so to make moral
ignorance exculpating. OIR takes no stand on that. It can be held by someone who thinks moral
ignorance never excuses, because it requires access to the *option*, not to the *reason*. A
principle with a weaker antecedent and fewer commitments, which suffices for the consequences in
§§6–10, is not made idle by being entailed.

**OIR is distinct in content from Levy's rationality condition, but here the claim is weaker
than I would like.** Levy requires that conforming to a standard be "something they can do
rationally (and not merely by chance or accident)", where the relevant rationality concerns
"what I can decide to do as the result of engaging in reasoning" (Levy 2009: 735, quoted in
Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.2). Read as stated, that formulation covers Nadia: she cannot decide to use
glass tubing as a result of reasoning, since the option never enters her reasoning. So the
condition and OIR are not extensionally distinct here, and I withdraw any suggestion that they
are. What remains is that Levy's condition has two independent sources of failure and he develops
only one. His target, Mr Potter, can represent the option of reforming his business and cannot
see the reason. Nadia is the mirror: she can see the reason — *do not poison infants* is fully
intelligible to her and she is moved by it — and cannot represent the option. Normative access
and representational access come apart in both directions, and they call for different
treatments.

## 6. The frontier problem

OIR looks harmless until it is combined with Opacity and with a claim about frequency.

Kyle Stanford has argued for a New Induction: that "we have, throughout the history of scientific
inquiry and in virtually every scientific field, repeatedly occupied an epistemic position in
which we could conceive of only one or a few theories that were well confirmed by the available
evidence, while subsequent inquiry would routinely (if not invariably) reveal further, radically
distinct alternatives as well confirmed by the previously available evidence as those we were
inclined to accept [on the strength of that evidence]" (Stanford 2006: 19, quoted in Stanford
2023, §3.3). I do not need his conclusion about theories, and I do not endorse it. I need a
practical analogue, and it is worth being explicit that it is weaker than his in one respect and
so cheaper to buy. Stanford needs the unconceived alternatives to have been *as well confirmed*
as the accepted theory. I need only that some unconceived option would have been *better* than
the one taken. The practical claim is therefore entailed by the theoretical one without entailing
it.

Even so, it is a premise and not a theorem, so here is the argument. Technique is cumulative:
the set of options available in a domain grows, and grows by conceptual as well as material
innovation. Its growth is not predictable from inside, since an option one could predict would
already be in the space — that is Opacity in its practical form. So at any time the set of
options an agent's field recognises is a proper subset of those that will later be recognised,
and no one inside can say which. That is all the frontier problem needs, and I take it to be
close to undeniable. What is not undeniable, and what I assume rather than show, is the
*distributional* claim that the omitted options are disproportionately valuable where technique
is newest. My grounds are that novelty is where the smallest fraction of the possibility space
has been explored, and I note the assumption rather than defending it further.

Put the three together:

1. (OIR) Unrepresentable acts are not deliberatively required.
2. (Opacity) An agent is never in a position to identify a specific gap in her repertoire.
3. (New Induction, practical form) Repertoire gaps are normal rather than exotic, and are
   densest where technique is newest.

The conclusion is that obligation at the grain of options lapses at the frontier, that it lapses
invisibly, and that it lapses in inverse proportion to the novelty and hence the scale of
possible harm.

I must resist a temptation here, which is to call this worse than the volitionist regress and
leave it at that. The reframing matters. What lapses is *option-grained* obligation, and someone
might reply that no one ever thought obligation was individuated that finely. But that reply is
not available, because the standard account of the deliberative ought makes its verdicts
option-shaped by construction. On the two-stage picture, "deliberation always proceeds in these
two steps, one being concerned with finding out which options are open, the other with
determining which of these options is the best" (Hedden 2012, 2015, as reported in Vollmer 2026,
§5); and Vollmer argues that the central deliberative question is "Which of my options ought I to
do?", to which "a complete answer … should include a specific course of action" (2026, §4). If
that is what the deliberative ought answers, then the frontier problem is a crisis internal to
the account, not one imported from outside. It is also worse-behaved than the regress in one
respect: the regress threatens to make blame rare, whereas this threatens to make *requirement*
absent, and absent precisely where we most want to say something.

It is also not the familiar moral luck. Nelkin defines constitutive luck as "luck in who one is,
or in the traits and dispositions that one has" (Nelkin 2025, §1), and the draft of this argument
I began with contrasted repertoire luck with that. The contrast was wrong. Being a pharmacist in
1975 rather than 1995 is circumstantial luck: "luck in the circumstances in which one finds
oneself", the category to which the Nazi-collaborator cases belong (Nelkin 2025, §1). So the
right claim is narrower. Repertoire luck is a species of circumstantial luck with a distinctive
feature: the standard reply to circumstantial luck is counterfactual — we ask what the
comfortably situated agent would have done in the bad circumstances, holding her dispositions
fixed — and that test is not merely hard to run here but ill-posed. Dispositions to deliberate
are dispositions to respond to *represented* options, so there is no way to vary the repertoire
while holding the deliberative dispositions fixed. Asking what Nadia would have done had she been
aware is asking about an agent whose deliberative question is different from hers. I state this
as a structural observation rather than a proof; someone who has a workable counterfactual on
offer would blunt it.

## 7. The grain of obligation

The frontier problem is generated by a tacit equation of two things that ought to be kept apart:
the individuation of options and the individuation of requirements. Options are fine-grained
act-types indexed to technique: *use a glass-lined line*, *stage the rollout in three cohorts*,
*chelate the effluent*. Requirements need not be. Nothing in the concept of a requirement demands
that its content be an available act-type.

> **Coarsening.** If *S* is unaware of option *o*, *S* may nevertheless be bound by a requirement
> *R* such that (a) *R* is representable for *S*; (b) *o* would have been a means of complying
> with *R*; and (c) *S* is in a position to comply with *R* knowingly — there is some strategy,
> representable to *S*, whose execution constitutes compliance with *R*. Where such an *R*
> exists, unawareness of *o* does not unbind: what *S* is required to do is *R*.

Condition (c) is not decoration, and a version of this principle without it is invalid. Without
(c), *do not expose patients to avoidable toxic risk* satisfies (a) and (b) in every case
whatever, including the cases §10 says are residual, so unawareness would never unbind and the
residue would be empty. Condition (c) also blocks a cheaper trick. If representability were
assessed on the *form of words*, coarsening would always succeed, since *do not wrong anyone* is
representable by every competent agent. It is (c) that makes representability attach to the
requirement rather than the sentence: a requirement one can state but has no representable way of
discharging is not one the agent is in a position to comply with knowingly. And (c) keeps §5
honest. If the argument for OIR is that a requirement must be put-able, compliable-with as such,
and answerable, then a coarse requirement whose only means of compliance is unrepresentable fails
those tests exactly as the fine one does. Consistency demands (c).

Three families satisfy it, or fail it instructively.

**(i) Outcome constraints, which do not survive.** *Do not expose patients to avoidable toxic
risk* is representable and intelligible to Nadia, and the glass line would have complied. But
there is no strategy representable to her whose execution constitutes compliance: the constraint
tells her to avoid something she cannot pick out. It fails (c). I therefore take the outcome
constraint off the requirement side altogether. Its role is evaluative: it locates the objective
wrong, explains why recall, compensation and regret are in order, and says nothing about what
anyone deliberatively ought to have done. This is a real cost to the view — it means coarsening
does not, by itself, restore accountability anywhere — and it is the price of the schema's being
valid.

**(ii) *De dicto* procedural requirements, which do survive.** *Given that you know there is
something you do not know, proceed in ways that tolerate being wrong.* Stage the introduction.
Prefer reversible interventions to irreversible ones. Instrument the outcome so that surprises
surface. Monitor for effects you have not hypothesised. Do not scale until the small case has run
long enough for the unknown to show itself. These have contents of the form *I know there is
something I am missing*, and Transparency establishes that there is no formal barrier to contents
of that shape. More importantly, each specifies a strategy whose execution *is* compliance, so
(c) is met. The duty does not ask Nadia to conceive of low-dose toxicity. It asks her to act as
someone who may be conceiving of less than there is.

This family is not the precautionary principle, and it is worth saying why not. As Hansson
records, that principle "is often believed to be a general instruction to be cautious", but in
its legal sense it "concerns how we should act when it is scientifically reasonable to suspect a
risk to health or the environment, but the evidence is not strong enough to show conclusively
that the risk exists" (Hansson 2022, §6.2). That is a condition on a *represented* hazard which
is under-evidenced. Family (ii) operates one step earlier, where there is no hazard hypothesis
to be under-evidenced at all. Nor is it Guerrero's caution principle, which urges "restraint in
the face of uncertainty regarding the potential harms that our actions might bring about"
(Guerrero 2007: 79, reported in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.1) — again a principle keyed to a
represented possibility of wrongdoing. Family (ii) is the nearest neighbour of both, and differs
from both in taking a purely *de dicto* trigger.

**(iii) Awareness-transferring requirements.** *Route this decision through someone whose
repertoire is not yours.* Halpern and Rêgo's own example has this form: the physician refers to
the oncologist knowing that the oncologist is aware of what he is not, without knowing what that
is (2006: 2). Consultation, external review, adversarial testing and mandatory second opinions
are ways of acting on knowledge of one's unawareness without converting it into knowledge of what
one is unaware of. They are representable, specifiable in advance, and auditable afterwards. This
family is a species of what the literature calls duties of inquiry, which "impose obligations to
undertake information-gathering actions" as distinct from norms demanding awareness itself
(Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.4, citing Rosen 2004: 302; 2008: 600–1; H. Smith 2014; Wieland 2017a).
Locating it there also answers a standing worry about awareness norms — that "becoming aware is
not something we do but something that happens to us" (Sher 2009: 112, quoted in Rudy-Hiller
2022, §3.4), which leads Clarke to deny that such norms generate obligations at all (Clarke 2014:
167, reported ibid.). Families (ii) and (iii) sidestep that worry: they demand actions, not
states of mind.

### Coarse requirements as partial answers

There is a way of putting all this that makes the structure visible and answers an objection I
would otherwise owe.

Take the deliberative question in the form Vollmer defends: *Which of my options, A, B or C,
ought I to do?* A semantic presupposition of a question is "a proposition which is entailed by
every complete answer", and "if some presupposition of a question fails, there is no complete
answer to that question" — though "questions with failed presuppositions might still have partial
answers" (Vollmer 2026, §4). Vollmer's contribution is an **ability presupposition**: the
question presupposes "that one is, in fact, referring to proper options", refined to the claim
that "the best among the supposed options in the set one is referring to is, in fact, an open
option" (2026, §5).

Unawareness is the mirror-image failure, and it needs a second presupposition, which is mine:

> **The Completeness Presupposition.** *Which of my options, A, B or C, ought I to do?*
> presupposes that the best *open* option is among those referred to.

Vollmer's failure is a listed item that is not an option; mine is an option that is not listed.
Both are failures of the reference of "my options", and both should be handled the same way. Three
things follow.

First, in *Tubing* there is no complete answer to Nadia's deliberative question — no particular
option she deliberatively ought to take. That is OIR restated in the semantics of questions
rather than asserted as a principle.

Second, and this is the payoff, the duality objection dissolves. Against verdicts of the form
"there is nothing the agent deliberatively ought to do", Blumberg and Fritz object that since
"ought or requirement and may or permission are duals, a denial of the former seems to lead to an
endorsement of the latter", and the resulting permission "is just as inaccessible to the doctor
as the supposed fact that they ought to give B" (Blumberg and Fritz 2025, as reported and quoted
in Vollmer 2026, §3). The objection has force against a bare denial of obligation. It has none
against presupposition failure, because the deliberative *may*-question carries the same
presupposition as the *ought*-question — as Vollmer argues for his case, so that neither drug "is
an option that the agent deliberatively may choose" (2026, §5). Nadia is not deliberatively
permitted to use the plastic set. The question to which permission would be an answer has
misfired.

Third, coarsening just *is* the survival of partial answers. When the completeness presupposition
fails, "some normative guidance is still to be had" (Vollmer 2026, §4). *Do not proceed in a way
that depends on your list being complete* is a partial answer. So is *route this through someone
whose list is longer*. Families (ii) and (iii) are not a rescue bolted on to a failed theory;
they are what a deliberative question with a failed completeness presupposition leaves standing.

The upshot is a redescription of what we are doing when we say, after a disaster, *you should have
thought of that*. Taken literally, the reproach violates OIR and is unfair. Taken as it is almost
always meant — *you should not have proceeded in a way that made everything depend on your having
thought of everything* — it is a charge under (ii) or (iii), and it can be fair.

## 8. Pricing caution

A duty that is never priced is not a duty but a mood, and the objection that precaution is either
vacuous or paralysing is fair until the price is stated. Expected value will not do the pricing:
one cannot weigh an unrepresented harm. So the variables must be ones available without
representing the hazard. Two are, and both are in use.

**Scale of exposure** is representable: Nadia knows how many neonates are on infusions this year
whether or not she knows what is in the tubing. **Irreversibility** is representable: she knows
whether a mistake can be undone. The duty in family (ii) is then to buy a margin proportionate to
exposure and irreversibility, defeasible against the representable costs of delay.

That this is not an invention is worth showing, because it is the best evidence that these duties
are real. Safety engineering already prices caution this way. Safety factors are margins set as
multiples of a measured quantity: "in food toxicology, the highest dose allowed for human
exposure has traditionally been calculated as one hundredth of the highest dose … that gave no
observable negative effect in experimental animals" (Hansson 2022, §4). Inherent safety is
preferred to secondary prevention because "as long as the hazard still exists, it can be realized
by some unanticipated triggering event" (§4) — an argument from irreversibility that names no
hazard. And Hansson makes the general point explicitly: inherent safety, safety factors and
multiple barriers "all aim at protecting us not only against risks that can be assigned
meaningful probability estimates, but also against dangers that cannot be probabilized, such as
the possibility that some unanticipated type of event gives rise to an accident" (§4). He adds
that "it remains … for philosophers of technology to investigate the principles underlying safety
engineering more in detail" (§4). Family (ii) is an attempt to say what those principles are
answering to.

Note that *Tubing* is a case where a safety factor was applied and did not help, because a
multiplier on the wrong quantity is still the wrong quantity. That is not an objection to pricing
caution this way. It is the reason family (ii) is plural — staging, instrumentation, monitoring
for unhypothesised effects — and the reason family (iii) exists.

## 9. Objections

**"Unawareness is just very deep ignorance."** I previously answered this by saying that
ignorance is corrigible by evidence and unawareness is not, since evidence is evidence for a
hypothesis. That answer is too strong and the awareness literature refutes it: Roussos observes
that "in most real cases, agents undergo changes of awareness because they gain some evidence
about the new possibilities", as when "Naledi might see a weather report which assigns a 30%
chance to SNOW" and thereby both becomes aware of snow and gains evidence about it (Roussos
forthcoming, §2). Anomaly-driven concept formation is the normal history of discovery, including
the discovery in *Tubing*. The distinction survives in a different form, and it is the Revision
Criterion of §4: the contrast is not between evidence-responsive and evidence-unresponsive
failures but between evidence that redistributes confidence over a fixed space and evidence that
prompts revision of the space. Roussos's separation of awareness revision from belief revision
gives exactly that line, and it explains why intermediate cases exist — most real episodes
involve both processes at once — without making the distinction one of degree.

**"Quality of will already handles it: Nadia's repertoire reflects her cares."** I accept a
limited version. What one notices is shaped by what one attends to, attention is shaped by
concern, and some gaps really are the shadow of a defective will. The test that limits it:

> **The Shared-Gap Test (restricted).** If a gap in *technical* repertoire is shared by every
> agent within *S*'s deferential reach, whatever their evaluative orientation, then the gap
> supplies no *differential* explanation of *S*'s conduct in terms of *S*'s will.

Three amendments to the version I began with, each conceding something.

*The reference class.* "The relevant community" was unspecified, and the verdict is wholly
sensitive to it: narrow it to hospital pharmacists in one decade and every gap is shared; widen
it to everyone with relevant expertise and few are. The principled class is the one family (iii)
makes normatively relevant — the agents *S* could actually have consulted. If a polymer chemist
within Nadia's reach had the concept, her gap is not shared in the sense that matters, and her
failure is a failure of family (iii), not an excuse.

*The conclusion.* I no longer claim that a shared gap is "not an expression of any particular
agent's will", only that it removes the differential explanation blame requires. The weaker claim
is what the argument supports and all the argument needs.

*The collective-vice reply, which I concede.* Where the uniformity of a gap is *explained by* the
uniformity of whom a community counts — harms to people absent from the profession, or poorly
represented in it — the gap is not independent of concern at all; it is the community's
incuriosity, and a shared vice is still each member's. I had no answer to this and now think none
is available: the test simply does not apply to such cases, which is why it is restricted to
technical repertoires in communities of plural orientation. The concession has a consequence I
welcome. If correlated repertoires can encode correlated indifference, then composing deliberative
bodies so that their repertoires are *not* correlated is itself a requirement of family (iii).
Safety engineering says the same thing about barriers: "if two or more barriers are sensitive to
the same type of impact, then one and the same destructive force can get rid of all of them in one
swoop" (Hansson 2022, §4). Reviewers who share a repertoire are correlated barriers.

**"OIR excuses the slaveholder who 'could not conceive' of abolition."** The slaveholder is not
unaware of the relevant *options*. *Do not beat this person*, *do not sell this child*, *let these
people go* are representable to her; they are not technical novelties. What she may lack is the
moral concept under which those options are required. That is ignorance, not unawareness, and
coarsening handles it in the strongest way: there is a representable requirement — do not inflict
this suffering on this person — which she violates and which she is in a position to comply with
knowingly, so (c) is met. The volitionist worry that she cannot *see* the requirement as a
requirement is a serious worry about moral ignorance, on which OIR takes no stand; as §5 noted,
that is one reason to prefer OIR to a full epistemic-access principle. My thesis does not extend
the domain of excuse by an inch in the interpersonal case. Its domain is gaps in the repertoire of
*means*, not gaps in the perception of *claims*.[^4]

**"Does Opacity not undercut the *de dicto* duties too? Nadia might be unaware that she is in a
domain where unknown effects are possible."** Yes. This is the residue, and I do not think it can
be argued away.

## 10. The residue, and why frontier ethics is institutional

Transparency says that *de dicto* knowledge of one's own unawareness is possible, not universal.
Roussos marks the distinction: "conscious unawareness is the state in which an agent is aware than
there is something they are unaware of", distinguished "from the presumably more common state of
unconscious unawareness, in which the agent is unaware of their unawareness" (Roussos forthcoming,
§1 n. 2). Where an agent is unconsciously unaware at the level of the *domain* — not merely of the
item, which Opacity guarantees, but of the fact that she is in the kind of situation where items
go missing — families (ii) and (iii) lapse with everything else.

> **The Residue.** Unawareness fully unbinds just where no representable requirement — not even a
> *de dicto* procedural or deferential one — was available. Here there is a wrong without a
> wrongdoer: the objective ought is violated, no deliberative ought is, and no one is accountable.

I take this to be the correct verdict rather than a cost. It is what we in fact say about
genuinely first-of-a-kind harms once the retrospective illusion of foresight fades: that something
terrible happened, that it was nobody's fault, and that this does not make it less terrible.
Refusing the verdict drives two distortions — hindsight scapegoating, which manufactures a fault
to match the harm, and blanket exculpation of technical domains, which infers from *no one
conceived of it* to *nothing was required*. Both assume that the grain of obligation must match
the grain of the harm.

**The verdict on Nadia.** A theory ought to decide its own case, so: on the repertoire ascriptions
I have stipulated, *Nadia is in the residue and the regulator is not*. Nadia used the licensed
device, by the approved protocol, for its approved indication; she was within a system that had
discharged the coarse requirement *find out whether the tubing matters*; and nothing available to
her marked neonatal infusion as a domain of live unknown effects rather than as settled practice.
There was no representable requirement she failed. The regulator's position is different, and the
difference is not moral but informational: the regulator knows the material is novel, knows the
exposed population is large and physiologically atypical, and knows that the screen is a screen
for acute effects. That is conscious unawareness at the domain level, and it is enough to trigger
family (ii): staged approval, post-market surveillance, monitoring designed to catch effects
nobody hypothesised. If those were in the regulator's institutional repertoire and were not used,
the regulator is accountable and Nadia is not.

That pattern — unawareness pushing requirement upward rather than away — is the theory's main
practical consequence, and I want to make the argument for it rather than assert it.

*From Opacity to external verification.* Compliance with a *de dicto* precautionary duty is not
first-personally verifiable. To check that I have been suitably cautious about what I cannot
conceive, I would have to survey what I am failing to conceive, which is what Opacity forbids. I
can verify that I executed a *procedure*; I cannot verify that the procedure was adequate to my
own blind spots. Someone whose repertoire differs from mine can. So for this class of duty,
verification is necessarily externalised — that is not a psychological claim about weak-willed
individuals but a consequence of the structure of the duty. (I therefore withdraw the claim,
which I cannot support, that a duty with no salient object loses every contest with a live one.
Family (iii) is a counterexample to it: *call the oncologist* has a perfectly salient object.)

*The meta-level regress, conceded and bounded.* Institutional designs are techniques with
histories. "Rotate reviewers with different repertoires" is an invention, and in some period it
was outside everyone's repertoire. So OIR applies at the meta-level and the frontier problem
recurs one level up: at a true frontier, the institutional options that would compensate for
unawareness may themselves be unconceived. I accept this. What I deny is the inference that the
institutional conclusion therefore works only where the problem was mild. The two levels have
different dynamics. Technical options are domain-specific, so a field's repertoire must be built
field by field. Institutional forms — staging, blinding, independent review, monitoring, rotation
— are domain-general and transferable: invented once, they are available everywhere, and the
practical New Induction is correspondingly weak for them. Institutional innovation is rare, slow
and shared, which is exactly why the meta-level repertoire is close to saturated in a modern
society even where object-level repertoires are not. The regress is real and it bites in 1750. It
does not bite now.

*What kind of duty institutional design is.* Here I have to choose, and I choose the first horn:
the duty to build these structures is itself *de dicto* in content — *route this through
arrangements that do not depend on anyone's having thought of everything* — and substantive only
in what it costs. I withdraw the claim that it is a first-order moral task "rather than a matter
of administration", which overstated the case and contradicted the thesis. The accurate claim is
narrower and still worth making: because *de dicto* duties are the only ones that bind unaware
agents, and because their satisfaction can only be verified from outside, the design of the
verifying structures is where a frontier requirement acquires determinate content. That makes
institutional design a locus of moral requirement rather than merely of institutional
convenience.

*A prediction, unchecked.* The account predicts that duties to consider specific items should
appear exactly where a domain has been routinised enough for the items to be listed — checklists,
failure-mode catalogues, standard protocols — and should be absent at the frontier, where only
procedural duties can be stated. I have not checked this and it is not evidence for anything until
someone does. What would falsify it is a mature technical domain whose codified duties remained
purely procedural, or a genuinely novel one in which specific consider-this duties were stated in
advance and turned out to be well chosen.

So the conclusion is double. The epistemic-condition literature has a blind spot of precisely the
kind it studies: it has theorised failures of belief while subsuming failures of repertoire. And
once repertoire failures are admitted, morality turns out to be able to speak at the frontier only
in the *de dicto* voice — which is why, at the frontier, it must speak through institutions or not
at all.

**A note on scope.** The argument is silent on several large questions: whether the deliberative
ought is prior to the objective one, whether accountability blame requires desert, how to
individuate a "domain" for purposes of the residue, and what an agent owes *after* her awareness
grows. The last is a real gap: the decision-theoretic literature on awareness growth bears
directly on it, and the source I most wanted for it was not retrievable (see the note on sources).
Each of these would change the tone of §10 but not, I think, the argument of §§4–7.
