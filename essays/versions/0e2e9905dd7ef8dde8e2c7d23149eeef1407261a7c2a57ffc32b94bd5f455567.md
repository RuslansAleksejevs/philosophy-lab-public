# What the Envoy Can Still Choose: Delegated Speech in the Zi Gao Episode

A state appoints an envoy to carry out a difficult mission. He fears both failure and success: failure may bring punishment; success may bring its own harm. He did not choose the mission, but he will choose some of the words with which it is carried out. What remains his responsibility?

In the Zi Gao episode of *Zhuangzi* 4, Confucius first describes service to the ruler as unavoidable, whatever the business. He then gives the envoy precise rules for speaking: do not inflate the message with extra feeling, do not alter the instructions, and do not press for a settlement. The passage explains why. Exaggeration undermines belief; words can set anger in motion; anger can produce conflict; and an envoy's unauthorized intervention can imperil negotiations. The advice sounds mainly prudent. Its stated concern is keeping the messenger whole and avoiding a failed or unstable agreement, not judging the ruler's ends.

That prudential reading is right, but incomplete. The order of the advice draws a useful distinction between authority over the mission and control over a delegated contribution to it. Zi Gao is told that he cannot select his assignment. Yet the story treats some communicative choices as his: he can add affective emphasis, depart from instructions, or urge completion. He can therefore avoid making a dangerous situation worse in these specific ways, even if he cannot choose the state's purpose. This is a narrow form of responsibility for one's discretionary contribution. It is not a theory of guilt, a right to refuse an unjust order, or a moral defense of obedience. The envoy's faithful transmission may limit the harm he adds; it cannot by itself make the mission's end right or erase his participation in it.

## Two kinds of control

The episode begins with Zi Gao, a minister sent by his king on a mission to Qi. He expects the receiving ruler to welcome him politely but delay action. He worries that he will suffer whether the mission succeeds or fails. The first answer is not an account of negotiation strategy. It is a claim about position: a subject's service to a ruler is an obligation from which he cannot escape, and he must serve without choosing the business. The advice is to attend to the work and forget himself. Only after this does the passage turn to the transmission of messages between states.

That sequence matters. It divides the problem into two parts. The envoy does not control whether he receives the mission or what the rulers want from it. He does control, at least in part, how he carries it. The passage does not call this a division of moral responsibility. It is my reconstruction of a distinction already visible in the scene: an assigned end lies outside Zi Gao's choice, while certain additions and deviations remain his to govern.

Both Giles and Legge preserve this sequence. Giles translates the first instruction as serving one's prince “without reference to the act but only to the service,” then turns to the danger of emotional excess in relaying messages. Legge likewise renders the minister's service as without choice of business, followed by a prohibition on transmitting the message with “any overflow of language.” The two translations thus support the same structural reading even though their vocabularies differ. Legge's introductory note separately describes the chapter's cases as duties arising from social position and relation. That note is the translator's editorial framing, not evidence of what the ancient passage itself says, but it underlines why a general theory of free political choice would be a poor description of the episode.

The messenger is not merely carrying neutral information. The passage says messages between states must be transmitted by intermediaries, and that conveying expressions of approval or anger is exceptionally difficult. If the parties are pleased, messengers tend to overstate praise; if they are angry, they tend to overstate blame. Excessive language becomes reckless and untrustworthy, leaving the messenger in danger. The rule is to transmit the standing message without expressive overflow.

The text then broadens the causal account. Competitive contests and drinking parties begin in order but can end in tricks and disorder as excitement grows. Human interactions, too, can begin sincerely and end in contempt; small matters can grow large. Words are likened to wind and waves: they can stir a situation and obscure the matter at issue. The passage connects clever, one-sided speech with anger, hostile reactions, and retaliation. It is in that chain of escalation that the next rules appear: do not move away from instructions and do not press a settlement. Going beyond the limit, changing the order, or trying to force completion can imperil negotiations. A bad settlement, once made, cannot be repaired.

These are not three interchangeable prohibitions. The first concerns emotional amplification: an envoy can make an assigned message more hostile or more flattering than it is. The second concerns authorization: an envoy can alter what the sender ordered. The third concerns pressure over outcome: an envoy can go beyond transmission to push the other party toward a conclusion. The scene therefore distinguishes what the agent may communicate from how far he may extend his authority. The envoy is a channel, but not a causally inert one. His discretion can add force, change scope, or accelerate a decision.

This is why the passage can be read as more than advice to stay safe. It identifies predictable ways an intermediary's discretionary conduct can contribute to harm. If an envoy adds hostility to an already tense message, his addition can undermine trust and provoke a response that the initial wording did not require. If he presses for agreement, he may help produce a settlement whose defects cannot later be corrected. He need not control the full outcome to have a role in bringing it about. The text's own concern for what happens after excessive speech is a reason to distinguish the mission's assigned content from the envoy's avoidable additions.

## Prudence, not absolution

A strong rival interpretation says the passage is only prudential. The messenger must preserve his own life and avoid being punished. The first instructions explicitly tell Zi Gao that he has no choice but to serve his ruler; the later rules protect the messenger and keep the negotiation from collapse. Nothing in the scene tells him to refuse an unjust mission, investigate the policy behind it, or answer for the ruler's purpose. If the teaching is prudence under compulsion, calling it an ethic of responsibility may put more into it than it contains.

This objection marks the essay's central limit, and the argument should concede most of it. The text does not formulate a modern doctrine of complicity. It gives a survival-centered explanation for disciplined speech. Nor does the passage clearly tell us what Zi Gao's mission is meant to accomplish. We should not infer a moral verdict on its unstated content, still less attribute an intention to a single historical author. The *Zhuangzi* is a layered literary work, and these scenes come to us through translations. Without access to the Chinese text in this investigation, a claim about fine-grained lexical force would be especially unwarranted.

But prudence does not cancel the distinction the passage draws. The rules identify actions that the envoy himself can avoid: exaggerating, changing instructions, and urging an outcome. The text describes these acts as causes of mistrust and risk, rather than as inevitable features of the assignment. If a person can refrain from a discretionary escalation, then he has a limited kind of control over his own contribution, even when he cannot control the larger political purpose. The point is not that the passage condemns these acts as morally blameworthy in every circumstance. It is that it does not treat them as simply the king's actions performed through an empty instrument.

Consider a minimal example. An envoy ordered to convey a demand adds a threat that his instructions did not contain. Even if he has no power to cancel the mission, the threat is his addition. If it provokes the receiving ruler, the envoy's action may contribute to an outcome without being its sole cause or its authorizing source. The episode's warning against excess makes this sort of contribution visible. A messenger who follows the wording exactly may avoid this particular form of escalation. But that fidelity cannot establish that the demand is just, or that carrying it out makes the messenger innocent. The passage's own counsel to serve regardless of the business leaves that moral question open.

This distinction avoids two opposite errors. One is to blame the envoy for every consequence of an order whose purpose he did not select. The other is to say that because he did not choose the purpose, none of his actions in carrying it out are his to answer for. The Zi Gao story supports neither total blame nor total exoneration. It isolates a smaller domain in which discretion and causal contribution remain: the envoy can refrain from making the message more extreme or from claiming authority he was not given. Whether his obedience to the mission is itself acceptable is a different question, one the advice does not answer.

## A small interpretive contribution

This reading does not reopen the broad debate about whether the *Zhuangzi* supports political withdrawal or engagement. Kwek's political reading of “In the Human World” already places its warnings in relation to officials, ministers, and the governing elite; Rathnam's 2026 book abstract presents Zhuangzi as permitting effective engagement with government while protecting life. The initial question about *xinzhai* as either abstract suspension or political technique is also too familiar: the SEP entry on Chinese mind and Lai's discussion of responsive agency already treat Yan Hui's dangerous encounter in context-sensitive terms. The narrower Zi Gao question is not a claim that scholarship has overlooked the scene. It asks what the scene's distinction between unavoidable service and constrained speech lets us say about delegated action.

The proposed answer is modest. The passage offers a way to separate authority from contribution. A superior may set an assignment's ends; the intermediary may have no recognized option to reject them. Still, the intermediary can retain discretion over how much force to add and whether to exceed the authorized remit. A reliable account of action should track both axes. Lack of control over the end does not entail lack of control over every means; control over one means does not make the agent responsible for the whole political outcome. The passage's advice makes this limited middle ground legible.

This is an interpretive reconstruction, not proof that Zhuangzi endorses a universal rule of message neutrality. “Transmit without overflow” may be advice to manage danger, and “do not press settlement” may protect the envoy from consequences of an unstable agreement. If so, the essay has identified a structure of constrained agency in the scene, not established a moral norm that applies everywhere. Indeed, a rule of exact transmission could itself help deliver a harmful policy. The story supplies no test for when an envoy should disobey, refuse, expose the mission, or protect those affected by it.

The scene's unsettling combination is precisely what makes the distinction worth preserving. Zi Gao is told that he has no choice about serving, then told to take care over choices he still makes inside that service. The passage neither gives him broad political freedom nor reduces him to a transparent conduit. Its account is narrower: a constrained intermediary can remain a source of avoidable escalation, and disciplined fidelity limits that contribution without settling the morality of the assignment. That is all the text can safely support here—and it is enough to challenge the easy excuse that delegated speech belongs wholly to the person who issued the order.
