# Off the Map: Unawareness, Obligation, and the Duties That Survive It

**Abstract.** Work on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility rests on an assumption it
has never isolated: that whatever an agent fails to be aware of is a proposition she was in a
position to entertain. Formal epistemology recognises a second failure — *unawareness*, the
absence of an item from the agent's space of possibilities — which is not a doxastic deficiency
at all. I give a criterion for the notion of representability this requires, argue that a
principle I call **Ought Implies Representability** governs the deliberative 'ought', and show
that it generates a sceptical problem exactly at the frontier where the gravest novel harms
occur. The problem is solved by observing that options are individuated more finely than
requirements: unawareness of an option rarely dissolves obligation, it *coarsens* it. Coarse
requirements turn out to be the *partial answers* that survive when a presupposition of the
fine-grained deliberative question fails — which also disarms the objection that a lapse of
obligation yields, by duality, an equally inaccessible permission. Because the only requirements
that bind a genuinely unaware agent are *de dicto*, and because compliance with a *de dicto*
requirement is not first-personally verifiable, frontier ethics must be institutional rather than
substantive. A residue remains in which nothing binds; this is tragedy rather than fault.

---

## 1. Two failures

Nadia is a hospital pharmacist. Consider two ways her work can go wrong.

**Sign.** She reconstitutes a drug in the usual way. The vial carries a label: *do not warm above
25°C*. She does not read it, warms the solution, and a patient is harmed. Nadia did not know that
warming was dangerous. But the thought *this might need to be kept cool* was available to her;
the evidence was in her hand; had she raised the question she would have known how to settle it,
and would have settled it correctly.

**Tubing.** In a different decade she prepares neonatal infusions using the standard giving set.
The set is licensed, and its material has passed the toxicological screen of the day: the
protocol exposes test animals to the highest dose the assay will carry, finds no observable
effect, and divides by a safety factor of one hundred to fix the human limit.[^1] Years later it
emerges that a plasticiser in the tubing harms neonates — but only at low chronic exposure, and
only during a narrow developmental window, so that the high-dose screen was not a weak test of
the hazard but a test of something else. Nobody in 1975 — not Nadia, not the manufacturer, not
the regulator, not the toxicologist who designed the screen — entertains the possibility that a
substance harmless at high acute dose might be harmful at low chronic dose. Their science has no
place for it: the governing assumption, which every calculation in the field embodies, is that
effect is monotone in dose.

The cases differ in a way that matters. In *Sign* there is an untaken precaution. In *Tubing* the
precaution was taken; what failed was the precaution's design, which presupposed a carving of the
possibilities from which the actual hazard was absent. Nadia's reasoning is impeccable given her
repertoire, and so is everyone else's. The repertoire is the problem.

English describes both by saying Nadia was "unaware" of something, and the literature on the
epistemic condition for moral responsibility follows English. That literature is built around
failures of the first kind. Its central puzzle is the regress generated by tracing: if
blameworthy ignorant wrongdoing requires culpable ignorance, and culpable ignorance requires a
culpable "benighting act", and culpability for that act requires awareness in turn, then, as
Rosen puts it, "the only possible locus of original responsibility is an akratic act" (Rosen
2004: 307, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). The responses to the regress — weakened internalism,
epistemic-vice views, quality-of-will views, capacitarianism — disagree about almost everything
except the shape of the failure they theorise.

*Tubing* has a different shape; the difference is structural rather than one of degree; and
seeing it changes what we should say about obligation, not merely about blame.

## 2. The Propositional Assumption

As Yates's survey records, "recent theorists of the epistemic condition take true belief to be
necessary and sufficient for awareness, and they identify ignorance as the lack of true belief,
the opposite of awareness (Peels 2010; Rosen 2008; Rudy-Hiller 2017)" (Yates n.d., §1). Awareness
is thus *defined* as a doxastic state. Call the resulting frame:

> **The Propositional Assumption (PA).** The relevant epistemic failures are failures with
> respect to contents the agent could have entertained: false belief, missing belief, suspension.

Two clarifications about the charge. PA is a frame, not a doctrine anyone defends. And the
literature does not *lack* a place for unawareness: its official definition of ignorance is
negative, so the unaware agent trivially counts as ignorant. The complaint is that unawareness is
*subsumed* rather than distinguished, and that theories built on the subsumption go wrong when
the subsumed case is the actual one.

They do, across the map. *Volitionists* require an occurrent *de dicto* belief that the act is
wrong, culpable only where ignorance traces to an act performed "in full awareness" (Rudy-Hiller
2022, §2): both the missing belief and the benighting act are specified by contents the agent
could have entertained. *Weakened internalists* substitute dispositional belief, "disposed to be
occurrent under certain conditions" (Yates n.d., §1) — which presupposes that the content is in
the repertoire, waiting. *Epistemic-vice* theorists locate culpability in dogmatism and
incuriosity, which explain failures to respond to challenges the agent encountered; a challenge
one cannot formulate is not a challenge one dismisses. *Quality-of-will* theorists allow that an
act can express contempt without any *de dicto* moral belief, but the expression runs through the
agent's take on what she is doing, and Talbert and Harman accordingly retain a *de re* awareness
requirement (Talbert 2013: 242; 2017a: 53; Harman 2011: 465; 2015: 67, cited in Rudy-Hiller 2022,
§3.3). Angela Smith's answerability view is the strongest candidate here, since on it failures of
factual awareness "sometimes reveal inadequate moral concern and thus are blameworthy"
(Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.3, on A. Smith 2005, 2017); Yates classes her under the analysis of ill
will as "objectionable evaluative attitudes" (Yates n.d., §2.c.i). But an attitude is expressed
toward something the agent's mind has hold of.

*Capacitarians* come closest, since they deny that awareness is required. Their disjunctive
condition is that agents "should and could be aware of [relevant considerations] given the
available evidence, the opportunity to adequately process it, and their cognitive capacities"
(Sher 2009: 87, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.4). But evidence is evidence *for* something: a
body of signals counts as available evidence only relative to hypotheses the agent can raise. The
low-dose studies of the 1990s are not evidence available to Nadia in 1975 in any sense that could
ground a should-have-known claim, though the molecules were there.

Finally, the requirement most obviously relevant to *Tubing* — "awareness of alternatives" — is
formulated by Levy as the demand that agents have "epistemic access to a range of alternatives",
so that "they can only appropriately be blamed for performing an action if they believed that
alternatives were available to them, and understood the significance of these alternatives" (Levy
2011: 111, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §1.1). That is a belief condition on an alternative already
in view. It says nothing about alternatives not in view at all.

## 3. Unawareness, formally

The distinction I need is standard elsewhere. In Fagin and Halpern's logic of general awareness,
awareness is a syntactic operator independent of knowledge: "implicit knowledge is the standard
(S5) notion of knowledge; explicit knowledge amounts to implicit knowledge and awareness"
(Halpern and Rêgo 2006: 2). Semantically, *A<sub>i</sub>φ* holds at a state iff φ belongs to the
set of formulas the agent is aware of there, and explicit knowledge is *X<sub>i</sub>φ ⇔
K<sub>i</sub>φ ∧ A<sub>i</sub>φ* (2006: §2). In decision theory the same notion is glossed as a
"lack of conception rather than a lack of information" (Schipper 2014: 1, quoted in Roussos
forthcoming, §1). Awareness and belief are thus orthogonal: unawareness is not a false belief, a
missing belief or a suspension; it is not a position in doxastic space but absence from the space.

Two features do the work that follows. They concern the agent's access to her own condition, and
they pull in opposite directions.

> **Opacity.** No agent explicitly knows, of any particular content, that she is unaware of it.

> **Transparency.** An agent may nevertheless know that there is something she is unaware of,
> without any witness to the claim.

Halpern and Rêgo state Opacity for models where awareness is "generated by primitive
propositions" — an agent is aware of a formula iff aware of every primitive proposition in it —
in which "it is impossible for an agent to know that he is unaware of a specific fact" (2006: 2).
That class will not serve, because they themselves observe that in it, under quantification, "the
formula A<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x) is valid, which does not seem to be reasonable in many
applications" (2006: 7): *de dicto* awareness of one's own unawareness would be a triviality
rather than something an agent can lack. I therefore state Opacity for the class they recommend
instead, in which awareness is only *weakly* generated by primitive propositions. It survives
there.[^2]

Transparency is their positive result: *K<sub>i</sub>(∃x(A<sub>j</sub>x ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>x))* "is
consistent and … can be true at state *s* even though there might be no formula ψ … such that
*K<sub>i</sub>((A<sub>j</sub>ψ ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ))*" (2006: §3); the self-directed form
*X<sub>i</sub>(∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x)*, "which says that agent *i* (explicitly) knows that there
exists a formula of which he is not aware", is also in the language (2006: 2). Their illustration
is exact for my purposes: a physician referring a patient to an oncologist "knows that an
oncologist is aware of things that could help the patient's treatment of which he is not aware",
yet "is unlikely to know which specific thing he is unaware of … (if he knew which one it was, he
would not be unaware of it!)" (2006: 2).

One caution before I lean on this. The bare self-directed form is cheap. Quantification here
ranges over the quantifier-free sentences of the language (2006: §3), an infinite set, so for any
agent whose awareness set is a proper subset of it throughout the states she considers possible,
*K<sub>i</sub>∃x¬A<sub>i</sub>x* holds automatically. What the formalism delivers is a
*consistency* result — no logical barrier to knowing one's repertoire is incomplete without
knowing where — and not the substantive premise an agent needs, which is that she is missing
something *relevant here*. That premise is not a theorem; it has to come from induction on the
history of her field, and §6 is where I get it. The formalism licenses the shape of the duty; it
does not supply its occasion.

Opacity and Transparency are compatible, and their joint truth is what this essay turns on. An
agent can know, with full justification, that her repertoire is incomplete, while being
constitutively unable to say where. She is not someone who knows there is a sign she has not
read. She is someone who knows only that there are kinds of sign.

## 4. What "representable" means

Almost every verdict below turns on where the boundary of representability falls, so the notion
cannot be left to intuition.

> **Representability.** An act-description φ is *representable* for *S* at *t* iff *shall I φ?*
> is a question *S* can pose and treat as answerable: iff, from her present repertoire, she can
> say what would count as settling it.

> **The Revision Criterion.** A gap in *S*'s option space is a failure of representability rather
> than of attention iff closing it requires *awareness revision* — introducing a new proposition,
> drawing a new distinction, or correcting a logical relation *S* treats as fixed — rather than a
> redistribution of confidence over propositions *S* already recognises.

The vocabulary is Roussos's. He separates awareness revision, "the formation of a new subjective
awareness state, or space of subjective possibilities", from belief extension and belief
revision, the last being the only one that "involves … evidence about what is the case" (Roussos
forthcoming, §1). Within awareness revision he distinguishes *expansion* (recognising that one's
set of possibilities was not exhaustive), *clarification* (recognising that its members were not
mutually exclusive) and *refinement* (introducing a finer partition inside a recognised one) (§2).

Two readings must be excluded, and the criterion excludes both.

The **thin** reading: φ is representable if the agent has the concepts in φ and could combine
them. On that reading *Tubing* evaporates — Nadia has TUBING, PLASTIC, DISSOLVE, CONTAMINATE,
DOSE and EFFECT, so *might something come out of the tubing?* is within combinatorial reach, and
the case is ordinary ignorance with a heavy evidential burden. I grant the inference and deny the
premise. Possessing the constituents of a question is not possessing the question, because
concepts are held under commitments about how they combine. *Might a dose too low to register in
the screen matter more than a dose high enough to register?* is not a recombination of DOSE and
EFFECT; it is the denial of a relation under which Nadia's field holds them. This is Roussos's
clarification case. Naledi could combine RAIN and SUN before her sun shower; what she could not
do was entertain their conjunction, since for her "RAIN and SUN were genuinely exclusive and
exhaustive options before". Clarification, he insists, is not reducible to recombination, because
it "involves changes to one's conceptual understanding such that one better understands what is
necessarily false of the world" (§2).

The **thick** reading: φ is representable only if the question would actually arise for her. That
collapses representability into salience and excuses everyone who simply failed to think of
something — including Nadia in *Sign*, for whom *is this heat-sensitive?* did not arise either.
The criterion blocks it, because the test is answerability, not occurrence. Nadia in *Sign* can
say exactly what would settle her question: read the label; her failure is inattention. Nadia in
*Tubing* cannot, because the settling procedure available to her — the screen — has the
assumption at issue built into its design.

Three consequences. First, representability is relative to a repertoire and hence to an agent and
a time; every verdict below is conditional on a repertoire ascription, and I will say which.
Second, the formal models do not supply the criterion: they individuate awareness relative to a
fixed language, and my phenomenon is the growth of the language. Third, the three kinds of
awareness growth are not normatively on a par. Refinement gaps usually leave a coarser
representable requirement standing — if the missing option is *use glass tubing*, the coarser
*find out whether the tubing matters* is available, and §7 will bind the agent to it. Expansion
and clarification gaps often leave nothing coarser. *Tubing* is built as a clarification case for
exactly this reason: the coarse requirement was discharged.

## 5. Ought implies representability

> **Ought Implies Representability (OIR).** If *S* deliberatively ought to φ, then φ is
> representable for *S* at the time of deliberation.

"Ought" here is the *deliberative* ought — the one that answers the question an agent asks in
deliberation and grounds accountability when she does not comply — not the objective ought that
ranks acts by their upshots, which is addressed to no one.[^3] And OIR constrains requirement,
not evaluation: *Tubing* is a bad outcome and the glass line would have been better.

The case for OIR is that a requirement failing it cannot play the role requirements play. A
requirement can be put to the agent; she can comply with it *as such*; its violation licenses the
demand *why did you not?* An unrepresentable requirement fails all three. It cannot be put to
her, since putting it to her would consist in making her aware of it. She cannot comply as such,
since compliance would be accidental. And *why did you not use a glass-lined line?* has, in 1975,
no answer that is not a report of the state of the art — no answer that is about her.

This argument is the guidance argument for perspectivism about the deliberative ought, and I
claim no credit for its form. The literature runs it via credit: on Lord's thesis, "if you
deliberatively ought to φ, then it must be possible that you perform this required action in a
way that is creditable to you" (Lord 2015, 2017b, reported in Vollmer 2026, §2), and
Lasonen-Aarnio's feasibilism rests on the premise that "it cannot be by mere luck that one
complies with the deliberative norm" (ibid.). My three clauses are that argument's three moments.
What I add is a new way for its antecedent to fail. The failures that literature discusses are of
two other kinds — ignorance of a fact, and inability. Vollmer's organising case is a doctor
"unable to give drug A" who does not know it (2026, §2, reporting Way and Whiting 2017). That an
option might be fully within an agent's power, fully consistent with her values, and simply not
in her space does not appear; where Vollmer touches something adjacent — plans so complicated one
"cannot even grasp" them — he notes that "in those circumstances, it is usually transparent to
the subject that they are unable to intend such an ungraspable plan" (2026, §3). Opacity says
that for unawareness this is precisely what is not so.

Three distinctness claims, of descending strength.

**OIR is not *ought implies can*.** Nadia *can* use a glass-lined line: she has the hands, the
training and the opportunity, and suitable glassware is in the cupboard. On any
ability-plus-opportunity analysis the antecedent of OIC is satisfied, so OIC imposes no bar. What
she lacks is not the ability to perform the act but the ability to pose it. One could read "can"
as *can do intentionally, as the upshot of deliberation*; I grant that this covers the case, and
note only that it is not the reading at work in debates about determinism and alternate
possibilities, and that its consequences have not been drawn.

**OIR is weaker than Kiesewetter's principle, and that is its advantage.** Kiesewetter holds
"that a subject only deliberatively ought to φ if they are in a position to justifiably believe
that they ought to φ" (Vollmer 2026, §3). An agent who cannot represent φ is in no position to
justifiably believe she ought to φ, so the principle entails OIR. I accept the entailment and
deny that it makes OIR redundant. Kiesewetter's is an epistemic-access condition on the
*normative* fact, and is controversial for a familiar reason: it appears to excuse the agent who
has the option in view but cannot see why it is required, making moral ignorance exculpating. OIR
takes no stand there. It can be held by someone who thinks moral ignorance never excuses, since
it requires access to the *option*, not to the *reason*. A principle with a weaker antecedent,
which suffices for §§6–10, is not made idle by being entailed.

**OIR is distinct in content from Levy's rationality condition — but here the claim is weaker
than I would like.** Levy requires that conforming to a standard be "something they can do
rationally (and not merely by chance or accident)", the relevant rationality concerning "what I
can decide to do as the result of engaging in reasoning" (Levy 2009: 735, quoted in Rudy-Hiller
2022, §3.2). Read as stated, that covers Nadia: she cannot decide to use glass tubing as the
result of reasoning, since the option never enters her reasoning. So the two are not
extensionally distinct here, and I withdraw any suggestion that they are. What survives is that
Levy's condition has two independent sources of failure and he develops one. Mr Potter can
represent the option of reforming his business and cannot see the reason; Nadia can see the
reason — *do not poison infants* is fully intelligible to her, and she is moved by it — and
cannot represent the option. Normative and representational access come apart in both directions.

## 6. The frontier problem

OIR looks harmless until combined with Opacity and a claim about frequency.

Stanford has argued for a New Induction: that "we have, throughout the history of scientific
inquiry and in virtually every scientific field, repeatedly occupied an epistemic position in
which we could conceive of only one or a few theories that were well confirmed by the available
evidence, while subsequent inquiry would routinely (if not invariably) reveal further, radically
distinct alternatives as well confirmed by the previously available evidence as those we were
inclined to accept [on the strength of that evidence]" (Stanford 2006: 19, quoted in Stanford
2023, §3.3). I do not need, and do not endorse, his conclusion about theories. I need a practical
analogue, and it is weaker than his in one respect: Stanford needs the unconceived alternatives
to have been *as well confirmed* as the accepted theory, whereas I need only that some unconceived
option would have been *better* than the one taken.

Since it is a premise and not a theorem, here is the argument. Technique is cumulative, and grows
by conceptual as well as material innovation. Its growth is not predictable from inside, since an
option one could predict would already be in the space — Opacity in practical form. So at any
time the option set a field recognises is a proper subset of what will later be recognised, and
no one inside can say which part is missing. That much I take to be close to undeniable. What I
assume rather than show is the *distributional* claim that the omitted options are
disproportionately valuable where technique is newest; my ground is that novelty is where the
smallest fraction of the space has been explored, and I flag the assumption rather than defend it.

Put the three together:

1. (OIR) Unrepresentable acts are not deliberatively required.
2. (Opacity) An agent is never in a position to identify a specific gap in her repertoire.
3. (New Induction, practical form) Repertoire gaps are normal rather than exotic, and densest
   where technique is newest.

The conclusion is that obligation *at the grain of options* lapses at the frontier, that it
lapses invisibly, and that it lapses in inverse proportion to the novelty and hence the scale of
possible harm.

The italicised qualification matters, and it blocks a dismissal. One might say that nobody thinks
obligation is individuated as finely as options, so the crisis is staged. But the standard account
of the deliberative ought makes its verdicts option-shaped by construction. On the two-stage
picture, "deliberation always proceeds in these two steps, one being concerned with finding out
which options are open, the other with determining which of these options is the best" (Hedden
2012, 2015, reported in Vollmer 2026, §5); and the central deliberative question is "Which of my
options ought I to do?", to which "a complete answer … should include a specific course of
action" (Vollmer 2026, §4). If that is what the deliberative ought answers, the frontier problem
is internal to the account. It is also worse-behaved than the volitionist regress in one respect:
the regress threatens to make blame rare, whereas this threatens to make *requirement* absent,
and absent where we most want to say something.

Nor is this the familiar moral luck, though I must correct an earlier misclassification of my
own. Repertoire luck is not a rival to *constitutive* luck, "luck in who one is, or in the traits
and dispositions that one has" (Nelkin 2025, §1). Being a pharmacist in 1975 rather than 1995 is
circumstantial luck: "luck in the circumstances in which one finds oneself", the category of the
Nazi-collaborator cases (ibid.). So the claim must be narrower: repertoire luck is a species of
circumstantial luck with a distinctive feature. The standard reply to circumstantial luck is
counterfactual — ask what the comfortably situated agent would have done in the bad
circumstances, holding her dispositions fixed — and that test is here not merely hard to run but
ill-posed. Dispositions to deliberate are dispositions to respond to *represented* options, so
there is no varying the repertoire while holding the deliberative dispositions fixed. Asking what
Nadia would have done had she been aware is asking about an agent whose deliberative question is
not hers. I offer this as a structural observation, not a proof; a workable counterfactual would
blunt it.

## 7. The grain of obligation

The frontier problem rests on equating two things that should be kept apart: the individuation of
options and the individuation of requirements. Options are fine-grained act-types indexed to
technique — *use a glass-lined line*, *stage the rollout in three cohorts*, *chelate the
effluent*. Requirements need not be. Nothing in the concept of a requirement demands that its
content be an available act-type.

> **Coarsening.** If *S* is unaware of option *o*, *S* may nevertheless be bound by a requirement
> *R* such that (a) *R* is representable for *S*; (b) *o* would have been a means of complying
> with *R*; and (c) *S* is in a position to comply with *R* knowingly — there is some strategy,
> representable to *S*, whose execution constitutes compliance with *R*. Where such an *R*
> exists, unawareness of *o* does not unbind: what *S* is required to do is *R*.

Condition (c) is not decoration; the principle without it is invalid. Without (c), *do not expose
patients to avoidable toxic risk* satisfies (a) and (b) in every case whatever, including the
residual ones of §10, so unawareness would never unbind and the residue would be empty. Condition
(c) also blocks a cheaper trick: if representability were assessed on the *form of words*,
coarsening would always succeed, since *do not wrong anyone* is representable by every competent
agent. It is (c) that makes representability attach to the requirement rather than the sentence.
And (c) keeps §5 honest. If a requirement must be put-able, compliable-with as such, and
answerable, then a coarse requirement whose only means of compliance is unrepresentable fails
those tests exactly as the fine one does. Consistency demands (c).

**(i) Outcome constraints, which do not survive.** *Do not expose patients to avoidable toxic
risk* is representable and intelligible to Nadia, and the glass line would have complied. But no
strategy representable to her has execution constituting compliance: the constraint tells her to
avoid something she cannot pick out. It fails (c). I therefore take outcome constraints off the
requirement side altogether; their role is evaluative. They locate the objective wrong, explain
why recall, compensation and regret are in order, and say nothing about what anyone
deliberatively ought to have done. This is a real cost — coarsening does not by itself restore
accountability anywhere — and it is the price of the schema's being valid.

**(ii) *De dicto* procedural requirements, which do survive.** *Given that you know there is
something you do not know, proceed in ways that tolerate being wrong.* Stage the introduction.
Prefer reversible interventions. Instrument the outcome so surprises surface. Monitor for effects
you have not hypothesised. Do not scale until the small case has run long enough for the unknown
to show itself. These have contents of the form *I know there is something I am missing*, which
Transparency shows face no formal barrier; and each specifies a strategy whose execution *is*
compliance, so (c) is met. The duty does not ask Nadia to conceive of low-dose toxicity. It asks
her to act as someone who may be conceiving of less than there is.

This family is not the precautionary principle. That principle "is often believed to be a general
instruction to be cautious", but in its legal sense it "concerns how we should act when it is
scientifically reasonable to suspect a risk to health or the environment, but the evidence is not
strong enough to show conclusively that the risk exists" (Hansson 2022, §6.2) — a condition on a
*represented* hazard that is under-evidenced. Family (ii) operates a step earlier, where there is
no hazard hypothesis to be under-evidenced. Nor is it Guerrero's caution principle, which urges
"restraint in the face of uncertainty regarding the potential harms that our actions might bring
about" (Guerrero 2007: 79, reported in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.1) — again keyed to a represented
possibility. Family (ii) is the nearest neighbour of both and differs in taking a purely *de
dicto* trigger.

**(iii) Awareness-transferring requirements.** *Route this decision through someone whose
repertoire is not yours.* Halpern and Rêgo's own example has this form: the physician refers to
the oncologist knowing the oncologist is aware of what he is not, without knowing what that is
(2006: 2). Consultation, external review, adversarial testing and mandatory second opinions act
on knowledge of one's unawareness without converting it into knowledge of what one is unaware of.
They are representable, specifiable in advance and auditable afterwards. This family is a species
of what the literature calls duties of inquiry, which "impose obligations to undertake
information-gathering actions" as distinct from norms demanding awareness itself (Rudy-Hiller
2022, §3.4, citing Rosen 2004: 302; 2008: 600–1; H. Smith 2014; Wieland 2017a). Locating it there
also answers a standing worry about awareness norms — that "becoming aware is not something we do
but something that happens to us" (Sher 2009: 112, quoted ibid.), which leads Clarke to deny that
such norms generate obligations at all (Clarke 2014: 167, reported ibid.). Families (ii) and
(iii) demand actions, not states of mind.

### Coarse requirements as partial answers

There is a way of putting this that makes the structure visible and answers an objection I would
otherwise owe.

Take the deliberative question in Vollmer's form: *Which of my options, A, B or C, ought I to do?*
A semantic presupposition of a question is "a proposition which is entailed by every complete
answer", and "if some presupposition of a question fails, there is no complete answer to that
question" — though "questions with failed presuppositions might still have partial answers"
(Vollmer 2026, §4). Vollmer's contribution is an **ability presupposition**: the question
presupposes "that one is, in fact, referring to proper options", refined to the claim that "the
best among the supposed options in the set one is referring to is, in fact, an open option"
(2026, §5). Unawareness is the mirror-image failure, and needs a second presupposition, which is
mine:

> **The Completeness Presupposition.** *Which of my options, A, B or C, ought I to do?*
> presupposes that the best *open* option is among those referred to.

Vollmer's failure is a listed item that is not an option; mine is an option that is not listed.
Both are failures of the reference of "my options". Three things follow.

First, in *Tubing* there is no complete answer to Nadia's deliberative question — no particular
option she deliberatively ought to take. That is OIR restated in the semantics of questions
rather than asserted as a principle.

Second, the duality objection dissolves. Against verdicts of the form "there is nothing the agent
deliberatively ought to do", Blumberg and Fritz object that since "ought or requirement and may or
permission are duals, a denial of the former seems to lead to an endorsement of the latter", and
the resulting permission "is just as inaccessible to the doctor as the supposed fact that they
ought to give B" (Blumberg and Fritz 2025, reported and quoted in Vollmer 2026, §3). That has
force against a bare denial of obligation and none against presupposition failure, because the
deliberative *may*-question carries the same presupposition as the *ought*-question — as Vollmer
argues for his own case, so that neither drug "is an option that the agent deliberatively may
choose" (2026, §5). Nadia is not deliberatively permitted to use the plastic set. The question to
which a permission would be an answer has misfired.

Third, coarsening just *is* the survival of partial answers: when the presupposition fails, "some
normative guidance is still to be had" (Vollmer 2026, §4). *Do not proceed in a way that depends
on your list being complete* is a partial answer; so is *route this through someone whose list is
longer*. Families (ii) and (iii) are not a rescue bolted on to a failed theory. They are what a
deliberative question with a failed completeness presupposition leaves standing.

So when we say after a disaster *you should have thought of that*, the reproach taken literally
violates OIR and is unfair. Taken as it is almost always meant — *you should not have proceeded in
a way that made everything depend on your having thought of everything* — it is a charge under
(ii) or (iii), and it can be fair.

## 8. Pricing caution

A duty never priced is not a duty but a mood, and the charge that precaution is vacuous or
paralysing stands until the price is given. Expected value cannot give it: one cannot weigh an
unrepresented harm. So the variables must be ones available *without* representing the hazard.
Two are. **Scale of exposure** is representable: Nadia knows how many neonates are on infusions
this year whether or not she knows what is in the tubing. **Irreversibility** is representable:
she knows whether a mistake can be undone. The duty in family (ii) is to buy a margin
proportionate to exposure and irreversibility, defeasible against the representable costs of
delay.

That this is not an invention is the best evidence that these duties are real, because safety
engineering already prices caution this way. Safety factors are margins set as multiples of a
measured quantity: "in food toxicology, the highest dose allowed for human exposure has
traditionally been calculated as one hundredth of the highest dose … that gave no observable
negative effect in experimental animals" (Hansson 2022, §4). Inherent safety is preferred to
secondary prevention because "as long as the hazard still exists, it can be realized by some
unanticipated triggering event" (§4) — an argument from irreversibility that names no hazard. And
Hansson states the general point: inherent safety, safety factors and multiple barriers "all aim
at protecting us not only against risks that can be assigned meaningful probability estimates,
but also against dangers that cannot be probabilized, such as the possibility that some
unanticipated type of event gives rise to an accident"; he adds that "it remains … for
philosophers of technology to investigate the principles underlying safety engineering more in
detail" (§4). Family (ii) is an attempt to say what those principles answer to.

*Tubing* is a case where a safety factor was applied and did not help, because a multiplier on
the wrong quantity is still the wrong quantity. That is not an objection to pricing caution this
way. It is why family (ii) is plural, and why family (iii) exists.

## 9. Objections

**"Unawareness is just very deep ignorance."** I previously answered that ignorance is corrigible
by evidence and unawareness is not, since evidence is evidence for a hypothesis. That is too
strong, and the awareness literature refutes it: Roussos observes that "in most real cases, agents
undergo changes of awareness because they gain some evidence about the new possibilities", as
when "Naledi might see a weather report which assigns a 30% chance to SNOW" and thereby both
becomes aware of snow and gains evidence about it (Roussos forthcoming, §2). Anomaly-driven
concept formation is the normal history of discovery, including the discovery in *Tubing*. The
distinction survives in a different form — the Revision Criterion of §4. The contrast is not
between evidence-responsive and evidence-unresponsive failures, but between evidence that
redistributes confidence over a fixed space and evidence that prompts revision of the space.
Roussos's separation of awareness revision from belief revision draws exactly that line, and it
explains why intermediate cases exist — most real episodes involve both at once — without making
the distinction one of degree.

**"Quality of will already handles it: Nadia's repertoire reflects her cares."** I accept a
limited version: what one notices is shaped by what one attends to, attention is shaped by
concern, and some gaps really are the shadow of a defective will. The test that limits it:

> **The Shared-Gap Test (restricted).** If a gap in *technical* repertoire is shared by every
> agent within *S*'s deferential reach, whatever their evaluative orientation, then the gap
> supplies no *differential* explanation of *S*'s conduct in terms of *S*'s will.

Three amendments, each conceding something. *The reference class.* "The relevant community" is
useless unspecified: narrow it to hospital pharmacists in one decade and every gap is shared;
widen it to everyone with relevant expertise and few are. The principled class is the one family
(iii) makes normatively relevant — the agents *S* could actually have consulted. If a polymer
chemist within Nadia's reach had the concept, her gap is not shared in the sense that matters,
and her failure is a failure of family (iii), not an excuse. *The conclusion.* I no longer claim
that a shared gap is "not an expression of any particular agent's will", only that it removes the
differential explanation blame requires; that is all the argument supports and all it needs.
*The collective-vice reply, which I concede.* Where the uniformity of a gap is *explained by* the
uniformity of whom a community counts — harms to people absent from a profession, or poorly
represented in it — the gap is not independent of concern at all: it is the community's
incuriosity, and a shared vice is still each member's. No answer is available, so the test simply
does not apply to such cases, which is why it is restricted to technical repertoires in
communities of plural orientation. The concession has a consequence I welcome. If correlated
repertoires can encode correlated indifference, then composing deliberative bodies so their
repertoires are *not* correlated is itself a requirement of family (iii). Safety engineering says
the same of barriers: "if two or more barriers are sensitive to the same type of impact, then one
and the same destructive force can get rid of all of them in one swoop" (Hansson 2022, §4).
Reviewers who share a repertoire are correlated barriers.

**"OIR excuses the slaveholder who 'could not conceive' of abolition."** The slaveholder is not
unaware of the relevant *options*. *Do not beat this person*, *do not sell this child*, *let these
people go* are representable to her; they are not technical novelties. What she may lack is the
moral concept under which those options are required — and that is ignorance, not unawareness.
Coarsening handles it in the strongest way: there is a representable requirement, *do not inflict
this suffering on this person*, which she violates and can comply with knowingly, so (c) is met.
The volitionist worry that she cannot *see* the requirement as a requirement is a serious worry
about moral ignorance on which OIR takes no stand — as §5 noted, one reason to prefer OIR to a
full epistemic-access principle. My thesis does not extend the domain of excuse by an inch in the
interpersonal case. Its domain is gaps in the repertoire of *means*, not gaps in the perception of
*claims*.[^4]

**"Does Opacity not undercut the *de dicto* duties too? Nadia might be unaware that she is in a
domain where unknown effects are possible."** Yes. That is the residue, and I do not think it can
be argued away.

## 10. The residue, and why frontier ethics is institutional

Transparency says *de dicto* knowledge of one's unawareness is possible, not universal. Roussos
marks the distinction: "conscious unawareness is the state in which an agent is aware than there
is something they are unaware of", as against "the presumably more common state of unconscious
unawareness, in which the agent is unaware of their unawareness" (Roussos forthcoming, §1 n. 2).
Where an agent is unconsciously unaware at the level of the *domain* — not merely of the item,
which Opacity guarantees, but of being in the kind of situation where items go missing — families
(ii) and (iii) lapse with everything else.

> **The Residue.** Unawareness fully unbinds just where no representable requirement — not even a
> *de dicto* procedural or deferential one — was available. Here there is a wrong without a
> wrongdoer: the objective ought is violated, no deliberative ought is, and no one is accountable.

This is the correct verdict rather than a cost. It is what we say about genuinely first-of-a-kind
harms once the retrospective illusion of foresight fades: something terrible happened, it was
nobody's fault, and that does not make it less terrible. Refusing the verdict drives two
distortions — hindsight scapegoating, which manufactures a fault to match the harm, and blanket
exculpation of technical domains, which infers from *no one conceived of it* to *nothing was
required*. Both assume the grain of obligation must match the grain of the harm.

**The verdict on Nadia.** A theory should decide its own case. On the repertoire ascriptions I
have stipulated, *Nadia is in the residue and the regulator is not*. Nadia used a licensed device
by the approved protocol for its approved indication; she stood within a system that had
discharged the coarse requirement *find out whether the tubing matters*; and nothing available to
her marked neonatal infusion as a domain of live unknown effects rather than as settled practice.
There was no representable requirement she failed. The regulator's position differs, and the
difference is informational, not moral: the regulator knows the material is novel, knows the
exposed population is large and physiologically atypical, and knows the screen is a screen for
acute effects. That is conscious unawareness at the domain level, and it triggers family (ii) —
staged approval, post-market surveillance, monitoring designed to catch effects nobody
hypothesised. If those were in the regulator's institutional repertoire and went unused, the
regulator is accountable and Nadia is not.

That unawareness pushes requirement *upward* rather than away is the theory's main practical
consequence, and it needs an argument rather than an assertion.

*From Opacity to external verification.* Compliance with a *de dicto* precautionary duty is not
first-personally verifiable. To check that I have been suitably cautious about what I cannot
conceive, I would have to survey what I am failing to conceive, which Opacity forbids. I can
verify that I executed a procedure; I cannot verify that the procedure was adequate to my own
blind spots. Someone whose repertoire differs from mine can. So for this class of duty,
verification is necessarily externalised — not a psychological claim about weak-willed
individuals but a consequence of the duty's structure. (I therefore withdraw the claim, which I
cannot support, that a duty with no salient object loses every contest with a live one; family
(iii) refutes it, since *call the oncologist* has a perfectly salient object.)

*The meta-level regress, conceded and bounded.* Institutional designs are techniques with
histories. "Rotate reviewers with different repertoires" is an invention, and in some period was
outside everyone's repertoire. So OIR applies at the meta-level and the frontier problem recurs
one level up: at a true frontier, the institutional options that would compensate for unawareness
may themselves be unconceived. I accept this. What I deny is that the institutional conclusion
therefore works only where the problem was mild, because the two levels have different dynamics.
Technical options are domain-specific, so each field must build its repertoire itself.
Institutional forms — staging, blinding, independent review, monitoring, rotation — are
domain-general and transferable: invented once, they are available everywhere, so the practical
New Induction is correspondingly weak for them. Institutional innovation is rare, slow and
shared, which is exactly why the meta-level repertoire is close to saturated in a modern society
even where object-level repertoires are not. The regress is real and bites in 1750. It does not
bite now.

*What kind of duty institutional design is.* Here I choose the first horn of a dilemma I had
straddled: the duty to build these structures is itself *de dicto* in content — *route this
through arrangements that do not depend on anyone's having thought of everything* — and
substantive only in what it costs. I withdraw the claim that it is a first-order moral task
"rather than a matter of administration", which overstated the case and contradicted the thesis.
The accurate claim is narrower and still worth making: because *de dicto* duties are the only
ones that bind unaware agents, and because their satisfaction can be verified only from outside,
the design of the verifying structures is where a frontier requirement acquires determinate
content. That makes institutional design a locus of moral requirement rather than of mere
administrative convenience.

*A prediction, unchecked.* The account predicts that duties to consider specific items appear
where a domain has been routinised enough for the items to be listed — checklists, failure-mode
catalogues, standard protocols — and are absent at the frontier, where only procedural duties can
be stated. I have not checked this, and it is evidence for nothing until someone does. It would
be falsified by a mature technical domain whose codified duties remained purely procedural, or by
a genuinely novel one in which specific consider-this duties were stated in advance and turned
out to be well chosen.

The conclusion is therefore double. The epistemic-condition literature has a blind spot of
precisely the kind it studies: it has theorised failures of belief while subsuming failures of
repertoire. And once repertoire failures are admitted, morality can speak at the frontier only in
the *de dicto* voice — which is why, at the frontier, it must speak through institutions or not
at all.

**A note on scope.** The argument is silent on several large questions: whether the deliberative
ought is prior to the objective one, whether accountability blame requires desert, how to
individuate a "domain" for the purposes of the residue, and what an agent owes *after* her
awareness grows. The last is a real gap — the decision-theoretic literature on awareness dynamics
bears on it directly, and the source I most wanted for it could not be retrieved. Each would
change the tone of §10 but not, I think, the argument of §§4–7.
