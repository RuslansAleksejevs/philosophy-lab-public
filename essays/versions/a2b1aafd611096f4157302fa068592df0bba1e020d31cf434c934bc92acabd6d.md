# When Repair Becomes a Bargaining Chip

Ecological restoration can be valuable even when the destruction that occasioned it was wrong. A repaired wetland can sustain lives and relationships worth protecting; denying its value does nothing to recover the wetland that was lost. Yet a promise to repair does not automatically justify destruction in advance. The difficulty is to explain this asymmetry without making restoration morally irrelevant to prospective decisions. Sometimes the availability of effective repair really does make a harmful project permissible when it otherwise would not be.

The decisive question is often how the project and the restoration became a package. An ecological gain can depend on a destructive project because that project supplies indispensable resources. It can also depend on the project because someone withholds independently owed conservation unless permission to destroy is granted. Both gains can be genuinely additional: neither will occur if the project is refused. But their justificatory significance differs. A reliable prediction of what someone will withhold is not yet a reason to recognize that person's authority to withhold it.

I argue that evaluating biodiversity offsets requires two assessments of conditionality. One asks whether the gain would actually occur without the project. The other asks whether the agent making it conditional has adequate reasons for excluding the less destructive alternative. Failure of the second assessment need not require a public authority to refuse a package under every circumstance. It does, however, defeat the proponent's inference from a beneficial package to a justified proposal. This distinction can preserve the value of repair while preventing control over repair from manufacturing moral permission.

## The familiar distinction and its unfinished work

Separating licensing from compensation is not a new environmental principle. Edvardsson Björnberg explicitly considers that separation as a way of reconciling offsetting with recognition of nature's non-instrumental value. Her discussion also argues that public deliberation can answer some concerns about private control of public environmental goods; it does not categorically reject offsets. The relevant issue here is what makes the separation substantive rather than merely procedural. Two successive decisions can still rely on the same defective inference. An authority might first approve destruction because restoration has been promised, and then specify how restoration must occur. Naming the first decision “licensing” does not establish that the promised gain was a legitimate reason for it. [Edvardsson Björnberg 2020, §§3–4 and 7](https://doi.org/10.1111/theo.12287).

Nor is concern about conservation funding new. IUCN's 2016 policy warns against dependence on destructive projects and against displacement of conservation budgets. Maron and colleagues analyze the interaction of offset credits with conservation commitments, including incentives to worsen the baseline against which gains are assessed. These are direct predecessors of the present argument. My narrower concern is a case where the causal accounting is perfectly honest: the gain really will not occur without the destructive project, and everyone knows why. The question is whether that accurate counterfactual suffices to make the gain a legitimate justification for permission. [IUCN 2016, §10.2](https://iucn.org/sites/default/files/2022-06/iucn_biodiversity_offsets_policy_jan_29_2016_0.pdf); [Maron et al. 2016, “Counterfactual scenarios”](https://doi.org/10.1111/conl.12222).

Consider a deliberately simplified case. A regional conservation agency owes restoration of a degraded marsh under an independently justified public plan. Funds are available and no emergency competes for them. The agency now proposes a recreational development that will destroy a separate habitat. It announces that it will restore the marsh only if the development is approved. Suppose the restoration would create greater ecological benefits than the development would destroy, even after accounting for timing, risk, location, affected organisms and human relationships. Assume the comparison is sound; measurement failure is not the objection.

If the agency's announcement is credible, approving the package produces a better ecological outcome than the agency will otherwise bring about. The gain is causally additional. Nevertheless, the agency cannot defend its proposal merely by pointing to that gain. It already owes the restoration and can deliver it without the destruction. Its announcement makes conservation conditional; it does not explain why conservation should be conditional. The relevant alternative to its proposal is not only the outcome it threatens to choose. It is also the less destructive course for which it remains responsible.

The case is intentionally clear about an antecedent duty. It establishes that even complete ecological accounting cannot perform every task required of moral justification. The argument does not depend on a restored marsh being less natural, authentic or valuable than an untouched one. Indeed, it grants the restoration's full stipulated value. What remains defective is the proposed use of control over that value.

## Why conditionality needs justification

The underlying principle is modest: an agent cannot normally justify avoidable harm solely by making fulfillment of an independently binding duty conditional on permission to impose that harm. This is not a principle against every beneficial bargain. It applies when the reason offered for the harmful package is created by the agent's refusal to do what the agent independently owes and can do without that harm.

Three considerations support it. First, a duty directs conduct among alternatives available to its bearer. An agent cannot erase an alternative from moral assessment merely by declaring an intention not to choose it. Otherwise, the most recalcitrant duty bearer could claim the widest permissions. The agency that reliably refuses restoration would gain a stronger case for development than the equally capable agency that honors its plan. Reliability in refusing duty would become an asset in acquiring permission.

Second, that result reverses the direction of justification. The agency should explain why the conservation duty can be suspended. Instead it cites the consequences of its own unjustified suspension as the reason to accept destruction. Its argument contains a practical circularity: destruction must be permitted because restoration will otherwise be withheld, while withholding restoration is defended only by the demand that destruction be permitted. An independently justified constraint on resources could break this circle. An unsupported refusal cannot.

Third, the principle respects the different standpoints involved. The agency chooses whether to impose the condition; an external authority may only choose how to respond to it. The authority's restricted options can make acceptance reasonable without validating the agency's prior restriction of those options. Conflating the two standpoints allows an agent to borrow the predicament it creates as its own justification.

This last point matters when ecological stakes are urgent. Suppose refusal would leave the degraded marsh exposed to imminent irreversible loss, and the authority has no timely means of enforcing the agency's duty. Acceptance might then be the least bad available response. It would be a mistake to sacrifice the marsh merely to keep a moral distinction tidy. But it would be equally mistaken to infer that the agency was entitled to make the offer. One decision can be justified as a response to another person's wrongful conduct. The response does not retrospectively make that conduct right.

Thus the proposed test is not an automatic veto on every tainted package. It identifies whose action is being evaluated and which alternatives that actor can justifiably exclude. An authority deciding under pressure should consider actual consequences. An agency defending the condition it imposed must answer for the fuller set of choices under its control. Institutions should also avoid treating emergency acceptance as a precedent validating such conditions, since that would reward their deliberate creation.

## Beyond broken commitments

An objector may concede the marsh case but consider it peripheral. Proper offset systems often deny credit for measures already required. Why turn a familiar prohibition on double counting into a philosophical contribution?

Because causal additionality and justified conditionality remain different even where no counted gain is already required by law. A legal system might impose no restoration duty while the agency still has a clear moral obligation under a fair allocation of conservation responsibilities. Conversely, the existence of an aspiration in a plan does not establish that its immediate implementation is required. The philosophical question is not settled by classifying a gain as additional under a regulatory definition. It concerns the reasons for withholding the gain, including but not exhausted by prior commitments.

The distinction also limits the criticism. Imagine a private association owns land suitable for restoration but has no special duty to restore it now. Funds adequate for restoration could instead support equally urgent conservation elsewhere. Its refusal to restore this particular site without new revenue need not be wrongful. Merely having the physical ability to restore does not make unconditional restoration obligatory. A critic who silently assumes unlimited duties whenever resources exist would make almost every environmental bargain coercive. That is not my claim.

The test asks for adequate reasons, not unconditional generosity. Such reasons can include fair limits on what a particular actor owes, competing responsibilities, and the opportunity costs of transferring scarce funds. They must be assessed without using the proposed permission itself as their sole support. A business need not prove that it would donate all prospective profits to conservation if its project were refused. It must establish whatever substantive case for its land use is required, and cannot substitute ecological gains extracted through an unjustified withholding strategy for that case.

Outside clear duties the result will sometimes remain indeterminate. There is no neutral formula that determines everyone's fair conservation share. That is a limitation of the test, but not a reason to replace it with a causal metric. Honest accounting can tell us what an actor is prepared to do. It cannot, by itself, tell us what the actor owes or which burdens others should accept.

## When a package is justified

A stronger objection points to projects whose ecological acceptability genuinely depends on restoration. Suppose a flood barrier is necessary to protect a settlement, all reasonable less damaging designs have been considered, and its construction will temporarily disturb a habitat. Reliable restoration reduces the eventual damage sufficiently to make the project proportionate. Must the barrier be justified as if restoration were impossible?

No. That would confuse independence of justification with isolation of consequences. One should assess the actual package, including reliable repair. The relevant independence concerns the reasons for its conditional structure. In this case the disturbance is bound to a defensible protective purpose, and repair is an appropriate response to the disturbance. The project does not rely on an unjustified refusal to conserve an unrelated site. Repair properly changes the decision because it changes the harm imposed by a project with a defensible purpose and design.

A commercial development can also survive the test. Suppose it supplies benefits sufficient to justify some ecological burden, avoids feasible less destructive locations, and generates funds that genuinely would not otherwise be available for the required restoration. Fair claims of affected people and nonhuman interests have been considered. The fact that conservation funding depends on development is then a reason for scrutiny, not a demonstration of wrongdoing. New production can create resources; it need not merely hold preexisting resources out of reach.

Even here, generating revenue is not enough on its own. Almost any profitable destruction can finance some good. The proponent must explain why imposing this burden is justified relative to feasible alternatives for meeting the underlying need. Calling the benefit an offset should not exempt it from that question. If an equally effective project at a less damaging site can finance the same restoration at reasonable cost, the destructive package still needs a reason for excluding it.

This is where the test improves on a flat demand to decide permission before considering repair. That demand is either too weak or too strong. It is too weak if the licensing stage simply accepts the offered package as the only relevant option. It is too strong if it forbids accounting for repair even when repair makes a necessary project proportionate. The better requirement is to justify the exclusions that make the package appear necessary. Do not ignore restoration. Ask why it must accompany this destruction.

The scope of “reasonable cost” is openly normative. A tiny additional expense should not automatically count as a disabling constraint; neither should every large expense be ignored because nature matters. The test relocates disagreement to a question that the gain-loss ledger cannot answer: what sacrifices may fairly be required of this actor to avoid this ecological burden? It does not pretend to settle that disagreement through arithmetic.

## Does the argument value nature only indirectly?

The preceding reasoning could also apply to hospitals, cultural institutions or public housing. Does that make it merely an account of bargaining, with nature incidental?

Its general form is not a defect. A moral principle can expose a distinctive environmental problem without being unique to environmental ethics. Biodiversity offsetting explicitly connects permission to impose ecological losses with provision of conservation gains. Consequently, it creates a setting where control over a benefit can be mistaken for an entitlement to impose its price. The argument identifies a vulnerability in that justificatory structure.

Still, some independent account of environmental reasons is required. The argument assumes that avoidable destruction supplies a reason against action and that conservation duties can sometimes be binding. These assumptions can be supported by concern for sentient inhabitants, future people, ecological relationships or non-instrumental value in nature. The argument does not decide among those foundations. It shows that granting favorable ecological outcomes does not remove questions of responsibility on any view that recognizes at least some duties not reducible to whatever package a proponent happens to offer.

A strict maximizing consequentialist might deny the distinction at the level of ultimate rightness. If accepting the package produces the best outcome, that theorist may judge it right regardless of how it was assembled. Yet even that position must evaluate the agency's full options rather than only the authority's restricted ones. In the marsh example, restoration without destruction is available to the agency and better than its package. Distinguishing actors prevents a truncated consequentialist comparison as well as a deontological mistake. Disagreement survives if the destructive package truly maximizes value across all options open to its proponent. The argument offers no proof that such a package must be rejected.

This limited agreement is useful. The strongest claim defended here is not that valuable things can never be exchanged, that restoration cannot repair, or that every offset is a moral failure. It is that ecological additionality is insufficient to establish justified conditionality. That insufficiency survives disagreement over the ultimate source of environmental value.

## Repair after wrongdoing

Return finally to an agency that has already destroyed the habitat without justification. It may now owe restoration wherever the best feasible remedy can be provided. Fulfilling that duty matters. The creatures and people benefiting from the repaired environment should not lose protection because the repair originated in wrongdoing.

Nor must restoration restore everything to count as fulfilling a specific duty. A duty can require the best feasible remedy when literal reversal is impossible. Completing that remedy does not show that no wrong occurred or that all other obligations have vanished. An account that allows meaningful repair without moral erasure avoids both fatalism about damaged nature and indulgence toward destructive actors.

What the agency cannot do is reason backward: because restoration is now the appropriate remedy, its earlier promise of restoration must have supplied adequate permission. The duty arose within a changed situation for which the agency bears responsibility. A response warranted after harm need not justify the deliberate production of the situation requiring it.

The practical lesson is a question for every proposed ecological bargain: why is this gain available only with this loss? Sometimes the answer identifies genuine constraints and proportionate accommodation. Sometimes it identifies an actor's attempt to turn control over conservation into authority to destroy. Good restoration deserves credit in both cases as an ecological achievement. Only the former answer can, without further justification, support the bargain's moral legitimacy.
