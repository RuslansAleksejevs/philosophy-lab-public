# A New Goal Does Not Settle an Old Loss

A nature reserve may have to stop protecting the kind of place it was created to protect. Suppose a freshwater marsh is becoming too salty for its old plants and animals. Keeping the sea out would soon consume the reserve's entire budget, damage nearby habitat, and still fail. Helping a salt marsh develop may be the best remaining choice.

Now suppose the reserve announces that the change has been a complete conservation success. Its new salt marsh is healthy. Its new targets have been met. The original freshwater habitat no longer appears among the things against which performance is measured.

The announcement can be true about the work and false about what has been saved. This is more than a problem with cheerful language. If meeting the new target closes the institution's account of the old loss, adaptation has acquired a power it has not earned. Choosing what to protect next and deciding what has been made good are different decisions.

I defend a limited principle: when a conservation body replaces a justified goal because it has become unattainable, achieving the replacement goal does not by itself settle obligations arising from the loss of the original object. To count new benefits as making good an old loss, the body needs a further argument about what was owed, to whom or for what, and how the benefit answers it. The principle permits large ecological changes. It restricts the moral conclusions drawn from success after a change of goal.

## Why changing the goal can be right

Conservation need not preserve a frozen picture. Organisms move, populations change, and places develop. A goal such as keeping exactly the same number of every species forever would be a poor goal even without human pressure. A defensible goal might instead protect a breeding population, room for a river to move, or a community's continuing relationship with a place. Each leaves room for change.

Even such flexible goals can become impossible. It would be wrong to sacrifice every nearby habitat merely to delay the disappearance of one. It can also be wrong to force animals to endure severe conditions for the sake of maintaining a familiar species list. The reasons for conservation must guide the response when older methods stop serving them.

The resist–accept–direct framework makes a useful practical distinction. Managers can try to retain familiar conditions, allow transformation, or guide it toward selected new conditions (Schuurman et al. 2022, section “Responding to ecological trajectories”). Nothing in this distinction requires treating the resulting choices as morally equivalent. It identifies choices that a changing world makes necessary.

My argument concerns a question left open by a choice among these responses. Once managers reasonably choose a new future, what are they entitled to say about the loss that brought them there? They may say that continued resistance would waste resources. They may say that the new habitat has great value. Neither statement establishes that nothing remains to answer for.

Consider two imagined reserves. Their freshwater habitats have disappeared, and their new salt marshes are equally healthy. In the first, a natural geological event caused the loss. In the second, avoidable industrial interference caused an otherwise identical change. Suppose the same future management is best in both. That agreement does not make their histories morally alike. There may be an obligation to repair harm or contribute resources in the second case that does not exist in the first. A method that evaluates only the newly adopted ecological target cannot register that difference.

This does not show that the second reserve's managers caused the harm. Managers may be exemplary rescuers. It shows that a successful rescue is not a complete account of the situation rescued.

## The missing argument

The principle rests on three claims.

First, a justified conservation goal identifies something that counts as a reason for action. It might identify a good valuable for its own sake, a means to other goods, or something an institution has been entrusted to protect. The particular justification matters. A reserve devoted to flood protection does not undertake exactly the same task as one established to protect the last breeding site of a bird.

Second, losing the practical ability to protect something does not establish that the original reason was mistaken. The bird may still matter after its habitat becomes uninhabitable. The flood protection may still be needed after the old marsh stops supplying it. Failure of a means and failure of a justification are distinct possibilities.

Third, an institution's permission to choose a feasible response comes from reasons about that response. Those reasons do not automatically give it permission to treat a different obligation as discharged. A hospital may rightly change from curing a patient to relieving pain. Success in the second task does not establish that an earlier avoidable injury has been remedied. The same logical gap occurs in conservation. It does not depend on equating a marsh with a patient.

Together, these claims block an inference: the old goal is no longer feasible; the new goal is justified; the new goal has been achieved; therefore the old loss has been made good. The premises concern what can and should be done now. The conclusion concerns whether a prior claim has been answered. It needs another premise connecting the particular achievement to that claim.

Sometimes that connection is available. If a marsh was protected solely because it filtered a town's water, another equally reliable and accessible water source might fully answer that particular loss. If the original undertaking concerned a bird's regional survival, a thriving population elsewhere could meet it. In these cases, a changed local target may serve an unchanged underlying purpose.

But a connection cannot be supplied just by broadening the description. Replacing “this breeding population” with “some biodiversity” may make a score rise while removing the very feature that gave the original goal its point. A manager must explain why the broader description captures what mattered. Administrative convenience is insufficient.

The concern is especially sharp when the same body both chooses its new target and declares all earlier obligations fulfilled. Its power to manage land is then being treated as a power to decide which losses count. Yet those affected by the loss may never have granted that second power. Even where a single public body properly holds both responsibilities, it must exercise them through different arguments. Combining offices does not close a gap in justification.

## What is inherited, and what is added

The idea that substitution can leave a loss unanswered is familiar. Elliot's stated thesis in the abstract of “Faking Nature” is that a natural area's origin can contribute value which engineering cannot restore (1982). My argument requires neither that all restoration is deficient nor that human involvement makes a place less valuable. It applies even where no value depends on an untouched past.

Page and Heyward distinguish successful adaptation from compensation for disruption, and distinguish things that serve people's ends from things that form part of those ends. They also discuss losses that compensation cannot fully address (2017, pp. 359–364). Their discussion of reasonable expectations explains why foreseeing a loss need not make it acceptable (pp. 366–367). These are substantial predecessors, not problems first noticed here.

The proposed addition concerns the authority involved in revising a conservation goal. A body's authority to steer ecological change does not, merely by being exercised successfully, authorize it to settle claims generated by that change. This point links a practical decision about future habitat to a demand for a separate justification of closure. It also applies where the object of protection is a nonhuman population, rather than a resource valued within a person's life.

That last application needs care. A population does not submit an invoice, and this essay does not establish that every ecosystem has rights. The narrower point is conditional. If an institution has a justified duty to protect a population, its failure to do so cannot be treated as irrelevant solely because it has selected a different beneficiary. The basis for the duty may be animal welfare, species value, or a legitimate public commitment. These bases will yield different further requirements. None is replaced by the bare fact that a new target has been met.

## What should change in practice?

Return to the imagined marsh. The reserve should describe its present plan using the best feasible ecological goal. It should also retain a separate account of the significant goods lost, why protection failed, and what duties remain. This account is not a second set of impossible performance targets. Managers should not be assessed as incompetent for failing to restore conditions beyond their control.

The distinction instead changes three decisions.

First, it changes the description of what a project buys. Suppose a firm responsible for accelerating salinization funds the new salt marsh. The funding deserves credit for what it achieves. Whether it also settles the firm's obligation depends on the loss being addressed. A healthy new habitat may answer some ecological damage while doing nothing for a community's lost freshwater use. The firm cannot obtain release from the latter merely because it financed the reserve's preferred future.

Second, it changes who must be heard. Experts who know how to establish a salt marsh may lack the authority to decide that losing a particular practice, place, or protected population has been adequately answered. Affected people need a way to contest the proposed substitution. Interests of nonhuman life need representation tied to the reasons that justified protecting them. Representation does not confer an unlimited veto. It makes visible a question that a revised species list cannot answer.

Third, it changes the comparison between otherwise attractive projects. Imagine two plans that create equally healthy salt marshes at equal cost. One also keeps open a route by which the threatened freshwater species can reach suitable habitat elsewhere. The other offers additional viewing platforms instead. If protecting that species was part of the reserve's justified undertaking, the first plan has a reason in its favor that cannot be erased by rewriting the goal as visitor enjoyment. The reason might be outweighed by other needs. It must first be acknowledged.

These requirements can be met without establishing a permanent new bureaucracy. A public explanation could distinguish the new ecological objective, the losses that remain, and the grounds for claiming that any particular duty has ended. The moral work lies in the distinctions and supporting reasons, not in the number of forms completed.

## Objections and limits

One objection is that conservation already struggles with nostalgia. Insisting on an account of past losses could make managers spend scarce resources on vanished conditions. New ecosystems would always appear inferior because they came later.

The principle does not assign value simply for being old. Some earlier conservation goals were unjust, poorly informed, or ecologically harmful. A reserve may have been created through wrongful exclusion. A species target may have rested on a mistaken classification. Evidence of these failures can defeat the original justification. In that case, abandoning the goal need not leave a claim to its fulfillment.

What matters is why the goal changes. Learning that an aim was wrong differs from learning that it can no longer be achieved. Only the second, by itself, leaves its justification intact. This makes the principle a check against nostalgia as well as against convenient forgetting: anyone invoking an old goal must still defend it.

A second objection concerns double counting. If new habitat already benefits wildlife, why demand more resources under the heading of compensation? That seems to charge twice for one loss.

Nothing here requires two payments. A single action can serve adaptation and answer an earlier duty. The demand is to identify which duty it answers and to what extent. If a new wetland supplies all the lost flood protection to the same people, the relevant claim may be fully met. If it protects different people, further distributive reasoning is needed. Separating questions prevents both giving credit where it is unearned and withholding credit where it is deserved.

A harder objection says that even a justified conservation goal creates no obligation after its object is gone. Duties concern possible actions. If restoration is impossible, there is nothing left to do except pursue the best available future. An account of loss adds moral language to a decision already complete.

The objection is right about the original action. No one can now preserve a population that has become extinct. A duty to perform that impossible act cannot remain an ordinary demand for compliance. But there can still be possible actions whose reasons depend on the failure: assisting surviving related populations, supplying lost services, explaining negligence, or changing the conditions that produced it. Whether any is owed requires argument. Impossibility of the original action does not settle that question either way.

Nor must every irreparable loss generate an endless debt. A responsible body may have done everything reasonably required. A fair agreement may settle a claim without pretending the world has been restored. A natural loss may leave grief but no party obligated to compensate. The principle prohibits automatic closure through a new target, not closure itself.

Finally, perhaps this is simply sensible accounting, without philosophical significance. A sufficiently broad assessment could include future benefits, historical losses, and responsibility in one calculation. Why insist on two questions?

One calculation is acceptable if it preserves their different grounds. The danger is not numerical combination as such. It is allowing a change in what managers seek to change what others may claim, without an argument connecting the two. A benefit to one population does not automatically answer a duty to another. An ecological gain does not automatically release a responsible party. Even a theory that ultimately permits these exchanges must justify them. The proposed distinction identifies where that justification is owed.

There is a practical reason to make this point explicit. Institutions need attainable goals. Their reports and funding will understandably follow those goals. Without a separate judgment about unsettled losses, the very process that makes conservation realistic can gradually remove the grounds on which its failures remain answerable. No deliberate deception is needed. The old object merely disappears from the measure of success.

Conservation in a changing world must be able to choose a new future. It should be equally able to say that this future is worth protecting and that reaching it has not made every earlier loss good. Keeping those statements together is a condition of honest success.
