# What Did You Forgive?

Leah tells her friend Sam that she forgives him for sharing a private letter. Months later she learns that he shared it to make her lose a job. Sam says that she has already forgiven him for sending the letter. He did not send it twice. Does Leah have to take her forgiveness back before she can properly resent what he did?

The answer depends on what her forgiveness covered. A single action can wrong someone in several ways. Leah may have forgiven a breach of confidence without forgiving a deliberate attempt to damage her livelihood. But that answer creates a problem. Every event can be described in countless ways. If each new description opens a fresh grievance, forgiveness settles almost nothing. If forgiveness covers every true description of an event, it can settle much more than the victim understood or chose.

I will argue for a middle position. When a person forgives, the scope of that forgiveness includes both the wrong she understood and the relevant uncertainty she knowingly accepted. It does not automatically include every morally important feature of the event. This view explains why two people can discover the same fact about a forgiven action yet have different grounds for renewed resentment. It also explains why an offender cannot normally use information he concealed to argue that the victim forgave more than she knew.

The distinction between different aspects of one offence is already present in Geoffrey Scarre’s discussion of taking forgiveness back. The task here is to give that distinction a boundary: a way to tell a newly encountered wrong from a fresh presentation of something already forgiven. The argument concerns sincere personal forgiveness that involves relinquishing a grievance. It is not a definition of everything called forgiveness, and it does not settle whether someone must forgive in the first place.

## An act and a grievance

Resentment, as I use the word, is a response to being wronged that continues to press the wrong against the person responsible. It need not involve revenge. Someone may resent a betrayal and want an honest acknowledgment without wanting its author to suffer. Pamela Hieronymi’s account helps make this possibility clear: resentment can protest the meaning of a wrongful event rather than seek harm to its author. Her discussion also explains why giving up resentment need not mean deciding that the action was acceptable. [Hieronymi, preprint pp. 23–25](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0cm3336m/qt0cm3336m_noSplash_b7bb0ca49bb703866019c79cd5dde416.pdf).

Forgiving a grievance, on the understanding used here, involves ceasing to press it in this personal way. The victim may still remember the event, judge it wrong, demand practical repair, or take precautions. A friend can forgive a broken confidence and still stop sharing secrets. Whether these attitudes amount to forgiveness can be contested in difficult cases. The clear cases suffice for the present argument: a victim can sincerely release a personal grievance without forgetting the history that gave rise to it.

Consider three later developments. First, a reminder brings back the pain. The pain need not be an accusation, and its return need not undo forgiveness. Second, the offender does something else wrong. Forgiveness for yesterday’s insult does not settle today’s. Third, the victim learns something morally important about the original event. This third case is harder because the action is old but part of the grievance is new.

Scarre makes this difficulty explicit. In one example, a woman forgives an assault upon her, then learns that it also harmed her son. He distinguishes her forgiveness regarding the injury to herself from her renewed response to the harm to him. He also allows significant corrections in a victim’s assessment to matter to whether forgiveness can be withdrawn. These are important precedents for the view developed here. [Scarre, §3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-015-9651-z).

We should not settle the matter simply by counting actions. One act of sending a letter can violate confidentiality, aid a deception, and sabotage someone’s work. Nor should we settle it simply by counting descriptions. “Sending the letter on Tuesday” and “sending the letter on the day before Wednesday” do not identify separate injuries. What matters is whether the new information changes the reason the victim has for objecting to her treatment.

A grievance therefore has more content than the identity of an action. It concerns what the offender did to the victim, and why that treatment was wrong. This need not be a theory that the victim can put into formal words. Leah can understand the difference between careless gossip and a plan to get her fired without having a philosophical account of either.

## Two discoveries that look the same

Imagine two versions of Leah’s case.

In the first, Sam tells her that he shared the letter because he thought a mutual friend already knew its contents. Leah recognizes that this was still a betrayal. She eventually forgives him. Later she discovers his deliberate plan to make her lose her job. The plan was in place when he sent the letter. Sam has done nothing new since she forgave him.

In the second version, Sam tells Leah that he acted from a motive he is ashamed to discuss. She knows that hostility about her job is a serious possibility. She says, after taking time to consider it, that she forgives the sharing even if that was his motive. She does not want to resolve that question. Later she learns that it was indeed his motive.

In both cases, Leah acquires the same fact about the same action. Yet her forgiveness has a different relation to the discovery. In the first case, the newly discovered plan was absent from what she understood herself to be forgiving. In the second, she took that possibility into account and chose not to let its truth make a difference to whether she forgave.

It would be strange to say that the second Leah had forgiven only careless gossip. She deliberately forgave more than that. It would be equally strange to say that the first Leah had necessarily forgiven the deliberate sabotage just because she could identify the act of sending. That would give the act’s unseen features a power to enlarge her forgiveness without her participation.

The difference is not whether Leah would feel more upset once the truth was known. The second Leah might find certainty much harder to bear than she expected. Nor is the difference whether she would have forgiven with complete knowledge. The first Leah might be generous enough that she would eventually forgive the deliberate plan too. What she would have done is not the same as what she did. The relevant question is what she was actually prepared to release, including which open possibilities she accepted as part of that release.

## How to draw the boundary

Three questions help determine whether a discovery falls outside earlier forgiveness.

First, does the information change the moral grievance? A different motive, a further violation of trust, or a newly revealed harm can change what the person has reason to protest. A mere change of vocabulary cannot. Learning that Sam pressed the send button with his left hand normally changes nothing. Learning that he selected a recipient who could dismiss Leah changes a great deal.

Second, was that feature already included in the victim’s understanding, either as a fact or as an accepted possibility? A victim need not know the exact extent of a loss to forgive causing it. She may know that the damage could be large and forgive it without waiting for a total. Here uncertainty is inside her forgiveness, rather than a limit upon it.

Third, what supports the claim that the uncertainty was accepted? There must be something more than the general truth that people never know everything. Relevant evidence includes what the victim understood, what the parties discussed, and what she showed herself prepared to put aside. Her words matter, but so does their setting. “I forgive everything” after a discussion of a small confidence broken is not automatically a release for an undisclosed campaign of sabotage.

These questions do not give the victim an unrestricted power to redraw the past. She might now prefer to say that she never covered a fact which she plainly addressed. The evidence can count against that claim. If she knew Sam might have sought her dismissal and deliberately forgave that possibility, the later discovery does not, by itself, put the grievance outside her earlier forgiveness.

They also do not require a catalogue of every possible consequence. Ordinary understanding works in broad terms. A person who forgives the destruction of a cherished object can understand that the loss includes being unable to use it next year. She need not list future occasions of sadness. The important distinction is between grasping a kind of injury with an uncertain extent and being unaware of a further kind of mistreatment.

That distinction can be hard to apply. An unexpected cost may be so large that it transforms the victim’s understanding of what happened. There need be no sharp numerical point at which this occurs. The absence of a mechanical rule does not make all judgments arbitrary. We can ask whether the discovery merely fills in the harm she accepted or reveals a concern that her earlier forgiveness never addressed. Reasons must be offered for either reading.

## Why forgiveness has this shape

The argument rests on two features of forgiving. It is a response to an understood wrong, and it has some stability beyond the moment of response.

The first feature rules out automatic forgiveness of everything causally attached to an action. Suppose Leah forgives the breach of confidence. Sam may receive a benefit from her release of that grievance. But he does not thereby gain authority to determine how broadly her release extends. If the identity of the physical action alone settled its scope, Sam could secure a larger release merely by ensuring that several injuries resulted from one click rather than several. The moral difference would then depend on how he packaged his conduct, not on what Leah forgave. That is the wrong person, and the wrong fact, to give control over its extent.

The second feature rules out a release confined to a frozen list of details. If forgiveness covered nothing beyond what the victim had vividly imagined, almost any later encounter with the consequences would escape it. Forgiving a broken object would not cover seeing its empty place on a shelf. Forgiving an insult would not cover remembering it in a new setting. A victim ordinarily understands that she is releasing a grievance whose familiar details may continue to affect her.

Acceptance of uncertainty connects these two features. It allows forgiveness to reach beyond exact knowledge without making it limitless. Leah can settle a grievance whose full consequences she cannot yet count. What carries it beyond present knowledge is her stance toward the uncertainty, rather than the offender’s power to point back to one physical event.

The position does not assume that forgiveness is a contract. Contracts normally seek a settled allocation of enforceable rights. Personal forgiveness often seeks a change of heart and may be private. Still, a change of heart has an object. Asking what that object was does not turn affection into commerce. We can ask what someone trusted another person to do without treating their friendship as an insurance policy. We can likewise ask what someone forgave without treating her words as a legal release.

## The offender who kept quiet

The account yields a further conclusion about concealment. An offender cannot normally claim that a victim accepted a morally important uncertainty when he induced her to think that the uncertainty did not exist.

Suppose Sam knew of his plan and concealed it by presenting a false account of the sharing. He later argues that Leah said she forgave “the sending,” not “the sending provided there was no plan to harm my career.” This argument fails. Her failure to state a qualification does not show that she accepted a possibility he deliberately removed from her view. He cannot create an appearance of a narrow wrong and then appeal to broad words spoken within that appearance.

This is not simply the claim that deceit supplies an additional grievance. It does, but imagine that Leah separately forgives the deceit. A question would remain about the original sabotage. Forgiving the lie about the letter does not by itself enlarge the forgiveness originally given for sharing it. One can release resentment about being misled while still needing to address what the misleading account kept hidden.

Nor does the conclusion depend entirely on Sam’s dishonesty. Suppose he never spoke to Leah, and she mistakenly inferred carelessness from the available evidence. The sabotage may still fall outside what she forgave. Concealment matters because it defeats a particular argument from her apparently unrestricted words. The general limit comes from the content of her forgiveness, whether her incomplete understanding resulted from manipulation or innocent uncertainty.

This also explains an asymmetry in reasonable expectations. The first Sam has poor grounds for expecting Leah to treat the sabotage as settled. The second Sam, who heard her knowingly accept that possibility, has much better grounds. Those expectations need not give him an absolute right against future resentment. They do, however, change what he can fairly say was already forgiven.

## Does this merely rename taking forgiveness back?

Monique Wonderly argues that someone can genuinely forgive and later justifiably stop forgiving the same wrong. She challenges accounts that make forgiveness impose an excessively strong commitment against later blame. Her alternative emphasizes how forgiveness and its reversal can form part of an ongoing moral relationship. [Wonderly, §§2–4](https://www.pgrim.org/philosophersannual/41articles/wonderly-forgiving.pdf).

Nothing argued here excludes that possibility. The second Leah may have further grounds for withdrawing forgiveness. Perhaps she discovers that her original willingness was shaped by pressure. Perhaps later conduct changes the meaning of the earlier act. Such possibilities require their own assessment. The narrower point is that the first Leah need not withdraw the forgiveness she actually gave merely to protest the newly revealed sabotage.

Calling the two cases different is not just a verbal preference. In a true withdrawal, the earlier forgiveness itself counts among the considerations to address. Why is it now right to return to a grievance that was released? When the grievance lies outside its scope, that special question does not arise. The victim still needs an accurate and proportionate response to the wrong. She does not also need permission to undo a release she never made.

This distinction prevents two mistakes. The offender cannot use forgiveness of one injury as a general defence against complaints about everything accomplished through the same action. The victim cannot avoid acknowledging an actual reversal merely by describing a familiar injury more vividly. Both owe attention to the earlier forgiveness as it was, including the uncertainty it embraced.

A critic might object that this account demands too much reflection from someone already hurt. But the account describes what makes a later claim sound; it is not an instruction to conduct an inquiry before saying anything kind. People often understand broad possibilities without explicitly sorting them. Where the scope was unclear, it remains unclear. We should then resist confident claims that the victim has broken faith, rather than insist that she should have prepared a more precise statement.

Another critic might say that generosity means forgiving the person as a whole. Some people do adopt such an expansive stance. This account allows that. A person may knowingly release grievances extending beyond the injuries she can name. But an exceptional breadth cannot be read into every act of forgiveness. A gift does not become more generous because its recipient describes it more broadly than its giver understood.

Forgiveness can settle something real without settling everything. Its stability depends partly on taking its scope seriously. The person who forgives need not know every consequence of an action, but neither is she bound to treat every later discovery as already addressed. We should ask what wrong she understood and what uncertainty she accepted. Those questions let us respect both the forgiveness she gave and the grievances she has yet to face.
