# Asking Makes You Answerable: The Question as a Normative Power

**Abstract.** Philosophers have lavished attention on the norms of assertion and, lately, on
the norms of inquiry. The norms of *asking* — of putting a question to another person — remain
almost untouched. I argue that asking is a normative power: it puts the addressee under a
burden of response, and — this is the thesis — it makes the asker answerable to the addressee
for what becomes of the answer. The defence turns on what answering involves: an answer is not
a commodity transferred but a judgment staked, and soliciting a staked judgment whose bearing
one has foreclosed is inducing a liability whose point one has already destroyed. I then argue
that this is not a moral side-constraint but a validity condition: where it fails, the
utterance is "an act of speech but no speech act," and the addressee owes nothing — not even
an excuse for silence. The account explains the force of the retort "then why are you asking?",
and it generalises a pattern Berenstain has described in the case of oppression — demanding
epistemic labour and then dismissing its fruits — into a diagnosis that also covers the sham
consultation, the rigged hearing, and the friend who asks advice she has no use for.

---

## 1. Then why are you asking?

Three complaints, none of which is a complaint about being lied to.

**Consultation.** A council publishes a draft scheme and invites residents to comment. The
scheme has in fact been fixed; the comments will be read, counted, and filed. Suppose,
further, that everybody involved knows this. Residents comment anyway, or bitterly decline.
Nobody has been deceived. Nobody has been coerced. And yet the exercise is a paradigm of
public bad faith.

**Advice.** You ask what I think you should do about your marriage. I give you an hour and
tell you something hard. You had already decided; you say so afterwards, cheerfully, as
though this were of no consequence. I feel used. I cannot easily say I was harmed — the
hour was freely given, and I might even have enjoyed it — and I cannot say you were
insincere, since you did want to hear me.

**Interrogation.** A courteous interlocutor asks me, in public, for evidence of a claim I
have made. I supply it. He asks again, slightly differently. This continues. He is
unfailingly polite, and he has not misrepresented anything. But whatever I produce will be
met with another question, and the eventual verdict — his and the audience's — was settled
before the first one.

In each case the natural retort is the same, and it is a curiously *constitutive*-sounding
retort rather than a moral one: **then why are you asking?** Not "you shouldn't have asked,"
which would concede that an asking occurred; but a demand to be shown that the act was one
of its kind at all.

This essay takes that retort seriously. I shall argue that asking a question of another
person is the exercise of a normative power, and that its authority is grounded in the
asker's own liability to account. Asking makes you answerable. Where the asker is not in a
position to be answerable, no question has been put, and the addressee owes no answer and no
excuse.

The neglect here is real and slightly odd. The semantics of interrogatives is a large and
sophisticated field; the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on questions is given over almost
entirely to it, and what it records about the act of asking is a pair of classificatory
remarks: that asking is "standardly regarded, e.g., by Searle (1969, 69), as a special case
of the illocutionary act of requesting," and that on Åqvist's rival proposal "to ask a
question is to command the hearer to cause the speaker to know the question's answer" (Cross
and Roelofsen 2026, §1.1). Both place asking among the directives; neither asks what the
directing costs the director. Epistemology has meanwhile turned to the norms of inquiry, but
those norms have been formulated first-personally: Friedman's zetetic instrumental principle
tells me that "if one wants to figure out Q?, then one ought to take the necessary means to
figuring out Q?" (Friedman 2020, §1; author's PDF, 2). Asking somebody is a means — usually
the cheapest one. What the inquirer owes the person they lean on is not a question that the
zetetic literature has yet had to face.

## 2. Four diagnoses, and one that gets closer than the others

Before building, clear the ground. Each of the following is a natural first thought about
what went wrong in §1. Three fail. The fourth, I shall argue, has identified the phenomenon
without yet giving the explanation I am after.

**(a) Insincerity.** The asker did not really want to know. But in all three cases the asker
may sincerely want to hear the answer. The council is genuinely curious about what residents
think; the friend genuinely wanted my view; the interlocutor may be genuinely interested in
my evidence, in the way a collector is interested in specimens. Wanting to hear is not
wanting to be *moved*. Sincerity, as Austin and Searle use it, attaches to the psychological
state the act expresses; the trouble here lies elsewhere, in what the asker will *do*, and
so a sincerity condition cannot capture it.

**(b) Deception.** The asker implied openness and lacked it. This is often true, but it is
not the wrong, because the wrong survives full disclosure. Stipulate that the council prints
"our decision is final" on the consultation document; stipulate that my friend says in
advance, "I've decided, but tell me anyway"; stipulate that my interlocutor announces that
nothing I say will change his mind. Deception is now impossible, and the exercise is worse
rather than better. A wrong that is aggravated by being announced is not a species of
deception.

**(c) Harassment or bad faith.** Claeys's recent treatment of sealioning takes this route,
and takes it well. Adopting Johnson's characterisation — sealioning "fuses persistent
questioning ... with a loudly-insisted-upon commitment to reasonable debate" (Johnson 2017,
13, quoted in Claeys 2024) — she argues that it has two goals: "the political goal of
forestalling action on the one hand, and the epistemic goal of undermining the victim's
authority on the other" (Claeys 2024). She extends the analysis, via Sartre, to the
"good-willed" questioner who is culpably ignorant of what he is doing. The account is
persuasive for its cases. But it cannot be the general account, because the council is not
harassing anybody and my friend is not in bad faith about herself; she is simply careless
about what she has done to me. We need a wrong available to the well-meaning, the courteous,
and the self-transparent.

**(d) Exploitation — and the demand-and-dismiss pattern.** The obvious thought is that
answering costs the answerer something and the asker has extracted it for nothing. Berenstain's
"epistemic exploitation" is the developed version: it "occurs when privileged persons compel
marginalized persons to produce an education or explanation about the nature of the oppression
they face," and is "a variety of epistemic oppression marked by unrecognized, uncompensated,
emotionally taxing, coerced epistemic labor" (Berenstain 2016, §2).

If cost were all there were to it, this diagnosis would not generalise, since the complaint
survives cost's absence. Suppose the answer is a single syllable cheerfully given. "Do you
mind if I bring my dog?" — "Not at all." If I had already loaded the dog into the car, I have
wronged you, and the wrong is not that I cost you a syllable. Conversely, an answer may be
very costly and not wrongfully solicited at all, if it was solicited by somebody who then let
it do its work. The complaint tracks *uptake*, not *expenditure*.

But cost is not all there is to it, and this is where I have to be careful, because Berenstain
sees the thing I am after. Unpaid labour is only the first of three features she identifies;
the others are the double bind and "the default skeptical responses from the privileged when
the marginalized do acquiesce and fulfill their demands" (§3). And in §5 she asks, in so many
words, my question: "How can we make sense of the fact that the privileged demand labor from
the marginalized and then fail to make use of the fruits of that labor?" Her description of the
pattern is exact: such demands "may appear to be invitations to participate in knowledge
production," but the privileged "demonstrate entitlement to the labor and means of production of
knowledge but fail to actually recognize the goods produced as contributions to knowledge" (§5).
She even lists "just asking a question" among the euphemisms under which the practice travels
(§2), and, quoting Dotson, locates the failure in *uptake* (Dotson 2012, 32, quoted in
Berenstain 2016, §4).

So the phenomenon is not mine to discover. What is left to do is explain it, and Berenstain's
explanation and mine are different in kind. Hers is *functional*: demand-and-dismiss persists
because it does work, upholding active ignorance and "the illusion that there simply are no
viable alternatives" (§5). That explanation is well suited to its subject, and I am not
disputing it. But it explains the practice by what it achieves for a system of domination, and
so it says nothing about the council, or my friend, or the examiner, where no system of
domination is in play and the pattern recurs anyway. My explanation is *normative*: it locates
the defect inside the act of asking itself, as the failure of a condition on a power, which is
why it reaches the cases hers is not built for — and why, as §5 will argue, it yields something
Berenstain's double bind leaves open.

What (a)–(c) share is that they treat the asking as normatively inert and look for the wrong
*around* it — in the state that precedes it, the impression it creates, the hostility it
conceals. My proposal is that the asking is not inert. It does something, and the something it
does carries its own conditions.

## 3. Asking as a normative power

A normative power is a capacity, exercised intentionally and recognised as such, to change
the normative situation by exercising it. Promising is the standard case, and the standard
gloss on it is that promising is "a special sort of power we have over our normative
circumstances, the power to invoke obligations by promissory utterance," the distinguishing
feature being that "promisors obligate themselves directly, by their own powers" rather than
via convention or engendered expectation (Habib 2022, §1). Consenting is another: my
permission alters what you may do to me.

Asking has the three marks of a power.

*First, it burdens.* After a question has been put to me, my silence needs a licence. I may
answer, or I may say "I'd rather not," or "that's not your business," or "look it up" — but
some such move is now called for, and simple non-response is a small offence that it was not
a moment earlier. Contrast being told something: I may receive an assertion in total silence
without owing anybody anything.<sup>[1]</sup> The burden is defeasible and often tiny, but it
is genuinely normative rather than merely social, as is shown by the fact that the rebuke "you
didn't answer my question" is a rebuke, and by the fact that our most formal institutions —
the subpoena, discovery, the parliamentary question — are recognisably elaborations of it
rather than exceptions to it. The classificatory tradition points the same way: asking belongs
with requests and orders, whose "essential condition has to do with the fact that the utterance
is an attempt to get the hearer to do it" (Searle 1965, 15); and Åqvist's alternative makes the
directive element starker still, since on his account asking *commands* the hearer to bring it
about that the asker knows (Cross and Roelofsen 2026, §1.1).

*Second, it authorises.* A question confers on its addressee a standing to speak on a matter
that they may not otherwise have had. "You asked" is a complete defence to the charge of
offering an unwanted opinion. This is easy to miss because the power is benign, but it is a
power: the asker opens a door, and what comes through it enters licensed.

*Third — the thesis — it makes the asker answerable.* Having summoned the answer, the asker
owes the answerer both that the answer be allowed to bear and an account, if asked, of how it
bore.

Let me make the third mark precise. Every ask is put in the service of some attitude or
decision of the asker's: what I shall call its **bearing point**. For "what time is it?" the
bearing point is my belief about the time. For an examiner's question, the bearing point is
not the examiner's belief about the subject matter — he knows the answer — but his assessment
of the candidate; Searle's distinction between "requesting information" and "requesting that
the hearer display knowledge" (Cross and Roelofsen 2026, §1.1) is a distinction between
bearing points. For "may I borrow your car?" the bearing point is my decision whether to take
it.

Then:

> **Live Dependence (LD).** At the time of asking, there are two answers within the range the
> asker takes the addressee might give such that the asker's bearing-point attitude, formed as
> the asker intends to form it, would differ between them.

> **Accounting (AC).** The asker is disposed, on the addressee's request and within reasonable
> limits, to say how the answer bore on the bearing point.

> **Standing (ST).** The asker is entitled to impose on this addressee the burden that
> answering would involve.

My claims are: LD and ST are *validity* conditions on the power — where they fail the
utterance does not exercise the power at all — while AC is an obligation *generated by* a
valid exercise, so that failing it is a breach rather than a nullity. The division tracks
Austin's, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it: a misfire is a case in which "the putative
speech act fails to be performed at all" and one has "performed an act of speech but no speech
act," whereas an abuse is a case in which the act comes off but "fails to live up to a
standard appropriate for speech acts of its kind" (Green 2020, §2.2).

Two immediate clarifications, since the thesis is easily over-read.

LD does **not** require that the asker be undecided, or unconfident, or likely to change their
mind. I may be ninety-five per cent sure and satisfy it comfortably. It requires only that
the answer be *positioned* to make a difference — that there be some live answer on receipt of
which I would go differently. Nor does AC require justification: the asker need not persuade
the answerer that the answer was rightly weighed, only be able to show that it was received as
an answer rather than as noise.

## 4. Why the asker is answerable: the argument from staked judgment

Why should soliciting an answer bind the solicitor? Commands do not bind the commander;
requests for objects do not oblige the requester to use them.

The answer lies in what an answer *is*. Consider the difference between asking you to pass the
salt and asking you where the salt is. In the first, you supply a thing, and the thing is
indifferent to your supplying it. In the second, you supply *your judgment*. You do not merely
emit a sentence; you advance a claim on your own authority and invite me to take it on your
say-so. Which is to say: you stake yourself. If you are wrong, you are on the hook in a way
no conduit is — "you told me it was in the cupboard" is a reproach, and "I don't know" is
itself a self-report that exposes you.

This gives the argument its shape.

> **P1.** In answering, one does not transfer a commodity but advances a judgment on one's own
> authority, thereby assuming a liability for it (for its truth, its competence, or its
> candour).
>
> **P2.** To ask is to induce the addressee to assume that liability.
>
> **P3.** It is wrong to induce another to assume a liability while having already foreclosed
> the benefit for the sake of which the liability is assumed, unless they freely accept this.
>
> **P4.** The benefit for the sake of which an answerer assumes the liability of answering is
> that their judgment should *count* — that it be received as a judgment and allowed to bear.
>
> **C.** So an asker who has foreclosed the answer's bearing wrongs the answerer, absent the
> answerer's release.

P3 is the moral engine, and it is not peculiar to speech. It is why soliciting tenders for a
contract already awarded is a fraud on the bidders even if their costs are trivial, and why
inviting applications to a filled post wrongs the applicants even if they enjoyed writing them.
In each case someone is induced to expose themselves — to be judged, to be on record, to be
refusable — in exchange for a consideration the inviter has already withdrawn. The wrong is
not theft of labour; it is **exposure without reception**.

P1 and P4 carry the philosophical weight, and they are what make asking unlike commanding. A
command treats the addressee as an agent whose *doing* is wanted. A question treats them as an
agent whose *take* is wanted, and a take cannot be received while its author is bypassed, in
the way a wrench can be received while its fetcher is ignored. That is the alienability
asymmetry: the deliverable of an ask is inseparable from the deliverer's standing as one who
judges. Discard it and you have not merely declined to use a good; you have solicited a person's
credit and then declined to bank it.

This is also why Berenstain's pattern is *specifically* an injury to standing as a knower
rather than a labour grievance: demanding the labour while refusing to "recognize the goods
produced as contributions to knowledge" (2016, §5) is exposure without reception, under
conditions that make the exposure far worse.

Notice too how the account explains the gradient of complaint across §1. The cost of a syllable
is nil, but the exposure is not: in answering "not at all" you have put your preference on the
record and made yourself the author of a permission. When it turns out the dog was already in
the car, what you resent is that your authorship was counterfeit.

## 5. The presupposition argument, and why the verdict is nullity

The argument of §4 shows that non-responsive asking wrongs the answerer. A second argument
shows something stronger: that the utterance fails to be an asking at all.

Second-personal address, as Darwall puts it, "presupposes" an authority: "her having the
authority is a necessary condition of the validity of the reason she purports to address and is
thus a normative felicity condition of successfully giving her addressee the reason" (Darwall
2006, ch. 1, 10–11, course PDF). What authority does asking presuppose? It cannot be authority
over the addressee's conduct in general — strangers may ask me the time. The presupposition is
narrower and more interesting:

> **The Presupposition Argument.** To ask B, rather than merely to wonder, is to treat B as
> someone whose answer can move one. To be such that no answer B could give would move one is
> to treat B as someone whose answer cannot. So one who asks while foreclosing the bearing of
> the answer occupies, simultaneously, both sides of a contradiction in the presupposition that
> made the utterance intelligible as an ask. The utterance therefore does not come off: it is an
> act of speech but no ask.

This is not a moral judgment but a structural one, and it is what the retort of §1 registers.
"Then why are you asking?" is not the complaint "you should not have asked." It is a demand for
the bearing point — a challenge to validity, of exactly the form Austinian felicity conditions
license. And the retort is available even where no one is deceived, no one is harassed, and no
one is out of pocket, which is precisely what the diagnoses of §2 could not accommodate.

The nullity verdict yields the practical payoff:

> **No Standing Without Answerability.** An invalid ask imposes no burden of response. The
> addressee owes no answer — and, what matters more, owes no excuse for not answering.

This speaks to the double bind, which both of my main interlocutors describe and neither
dissolves. Claeys's sealioned target "is left with the choice to either respond (and tire
herself needlessly) or seem either unreasonable or unwilling" (Claeys 2024). Berenstain's
version is sharper, because in her cases the second horn carries a racialised penalty: Amina,
asked by an acquaintance why she was "so rude" to a stranger who touched her hair, "knows that
if she refuses to explain herself ... she runs the risk of being painted as overly emotional,
irrational, hypersensitive, unfriendly, and aggressive," and so "faces a double bind"
(Berenstain 2016, §3). Berenstain's diagnosis is that "the privileged feel entitled to the time
and energy of the marginalized," so that failing to supply it is read as "shirking a duty"
(§3). That is exactly right, and the present account says what is wrong with the reading: there
was no duty. The entitlement the asker asserts is an entitlement they do not have, because an
ask that forecloses its own bearing point, or that is put by someone with no title to put it,
does not generate a burden of response at all. Amina is not declining to discharge an
obligation. There is no obligation, because there was no question — only an utterance shaped
like one.

I should be careful about how much this delivers, since a philosophical result is easily
oversold. The nullity verdict removes the *normative* default, not the social penalty. Amina may
still be called aggressive; that is what Berenstain's structural analysis is about, and nothing
in speech-act theory disarms it. What the verdict does is deny the penalty its justification,
which is not nothing: much of the force of "but you didn't answer" depends on the appearance of
a shirked duty, and showing that appearance to be counterfeit is a precondition of contesting
the penalty rather than absorbing it.

It is worth noting how unusual it is to find an independent institution reaching for the same
verdict. English administrative law has for forty years recognised conditions on lawful
consultation — the "Sedley criteria," endorsed by the Supreme Court in *R (Moseley) v Haringey
LBC* — of which the fourth is "that the product of consultation must be conscientiously taken
into account in finalising any statutory proposals" ([2014] UKSC 56, [25], quoting *R v Brent
LBC, ex p Gunning* (1985) 84 LGR 168, 189). Lord Wilson grounds these not only in better
decisions and in avoiding "the sense of injustice which the person who is the subject of the
decision will otherwise feel," but in "a third purpose, reflective of the democratic principle"
([24]). And the verdict on a consultation whose framing had foreclosed the live alternatives was
not that it was defective: it was that "in reality, therefore, there was no consultation on the
fundamental basis of the scheme" ([42]). That is the nullity verdict, reached by judges who were
not doing speech-act theory. I offer it not as an argument — courts can be wrong, and legal
validity is not normative validity — but as evidence that the structure I am describing is one
reflective practice has already found it necessary to mark.

## 6. Two further conditions, and a taxonomy of failures

LD and AC concern the *asker's* relation to the answer. ST concerns the asker's title to
impose the burden in the first place, and it is needed for a class of cases LD cannot reach.

Searle's preparatory condition on requests already gestures at it: "if I make a request to
someone to do something which it is obvious that he is already doing or is about to do, then my
request is pointless and to that extent defective," an instance of the general requirement that
"the act must have a point" (Searle 1965, 12). Generalise: the burden of answering must be
one the asker is entitled to impose, given who they are, who the addressee is, how available the
answer is elsewhere, and how often it has already been given. Standing is defeated by
repetition, by ready public availability, and by the asker's having no title to this person's
labour in particular. Add a companion condition — call it **Fair Terms** — that the ask not be
so structured that every available response is costly to the addressee.

With four conditions we can sort the pathologies, and the sorting is the account's main
explanatory dividend.

| Pathology | Condition that fails | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sham consultation; the rigged hearing; the interview whose verdict is fixed | Live Dependence | nullity: no question was put |
| "We've taken your views on board" and nothing further, ever | Accounting | breach of an obligation owed to the answerer |
| Sealioning | Standing, Fair Terms | nullity: no question was put |
| Epistemic exploitation (demand-and-dismiss) | Standing *and* Live Dependence, plus Fair Terms via the double bind | nullity, overdetermined |
| "I'm just asking whether X" | not an ask at all | assertion in interrogative costume |

Two rows deserve comment.

The fourth row is where the framework earns its keep against a rival explanation. Berenstain's
cases fail more than one condition at once, and that is a substantive claim rather than a
hedge. They fail Standing, because the asker has no title to a tenth explanation of what is
readily available; they fail Live Dependence, because the "default skepticism" she describes
(2016, §3) *is* the foreclosure of the bearing point — the answer will be discounted whatever it
says; and the double bind is a Fair Terms failure, since both horns are costly. The
overdetermination explains something her functional account does not need to explain but which
matters practically: why these demands are so hard to refuse cleanly, and why refusing one
ground at a time ("I've answered that before"; "you're not really listening") never settles the
matter. Each refusal answers one defect and leaves the others standing.

The last row the framework diagnoses more sharply than the literature has. What is objectionable
about "just asking questions" is usually described as insinuation, or the laundering of a claim
one will not defend. True, but incomplete. Assertion and asking each have a price. The price of
assertion is answerability for the truth of what one says; the price of asking is answerability
for what one does with the answer. The JAQ-er wants the illocutionary benefit of assertion — a
content placed on the table, in play, needing rebuttal — while paying neither price: not the
first, because he did not assert; and not the second, because the bearing point is not his own
belief but the audience's impression, to which his interlocutor's judgment is admitted only as
raw material and never as judgment. Call this **bearing-point drift**: the advertised bearing
point is inert, and the operative one is a place where the answerer's standing as a judge has no
purchase at all. Berenstain describes precisely this use of an interlocutor as material —
epistemic products are treated as "fodder for skeptical responses" and "harvest[ed] for content
that can be developed into straw arguments and rejected out of hand" (2016, §5). The manoeuvre
is a double evasion, and its two halves are what make it so hard to name in real time.

The conditions also let us say something the harassment literature cannot easily say about
Claeys's courteous, genuinely open questioner. If he really satisfies LD and AC, my account does
not convict him of sham; it convicts him under ST, since he is not entitled to this person's
tenth explanation of what a search would settle, nor entitled to it in a forum where every
answer costs her. That is a better description of his fault than self-deception, because it can
be shown to him without telling him that he does not know his own mind.

## 7. Objections

**(i) This is Gricean implicature, not a normative power.** The suggestion would be that asking
merely *implicates* openness, as asserting implicates belief, and that the implicature is
cancellable.

The test for implicature is cancellability, and the phenomena fail it in a distinctive way.
"What do you think? — though I should say I'm fairly settled" is perfectly acceptable, but it
does not cancel LD; being fairly settled is compatible with being movable. Now cancel LD itself:
"What do you think? — though nothing you say will make any difference to anything I believe or
do." This does not produce a felicitous, implicature-free question. It produces the retort of
§1. Compare "I promise to come, though I have no intention of coming," which is not a cancelled
implicature but something closer to an absurdity. Cancelling a genuine implicature makes an
utterance more informative; cancelling LD makes the utterance unintelligible as an ask. That is
the signature of a constitutive condition.

**(ii) Over-demandingness.** If every question puts me on the hook, people will ask less, and
asking is epistemically precious.

Three replies. First, the obligation is graded and usually trivial to discharge: for most asks
AC costs a sentence, and for many it is waived by default. Second, the counterfactual is wrong.
The alternative to answerable asking is not more genuine asking but more sham asking, and sham
asking is self-consuming: where consultation is known to be ornamental, people stop responding.
The credit of the practice is a common resource and non-responsive askers deplete it — the
mechanism the *Stanford Encyclopedia* notes for promising, where "a pattern of abuses of speech
act institutions might deprive a person of an ability to perform speech acts" (Green 2020, §9).
Third, powers-based accounts have a relief valve: the obligation is owed *to* the addressee and
so is theirs to waive. "Ask away — you needn't tell me what you do with it" is a real and common
release, available precisely because the obligation is bipolar.

**(iii) The examiner, the lawyer, the therapist.** Plenty of legitimate questioners know the
answer and will not be moved by it.

The bearing-point apparatus handles these without special pleading. The examiner's bearing point
is the grade; his question satisfies LD robustly, and an examiner whose grade was fixed in
advance is running precisely a sham, which is the right result. Cross-examination is a case where
the bearing point is the tribunal's finding rather than the questioner's belief, and the
institution is transparent about this — which is why the witness is not wronged, and also why we
speak of a "kangaroo court" when the finding is fixed. The therapist's bearing point is her
understanding of the patient. In each case the ask survives because there is somewhere the answer
genuinely lands; it is the *absence* of such a place, not the presence of a settled belief, that
voids.

**(iv) Isn't Live Dependence just the "good faith" condition already on offer?** Claeys quotes
Sullivan and colleagues: one argues in good faith when "one is willing to change their views
considering some conceivable evidence or argument that one's opponent might offer" (Claeys 2024,
quoting Sullivan et al. 2020). The resemblance is real and I do not want to overstate the
distance. But three things are added here. Their formulation is a rough gloss on a virtue of
debaters; mine is a validity condition on a normative power, and that difference is what delivers
the nullity verdict and hence the permission to refuse. Their formulation indexes openness to the
asker's *views*; mine indexes it to a bearing point that is often not a view at all, which is why
mine handles examiners and consent. And theirs has no counterpart of AC, which is where much of
the ordinary wrongdoing in this area actually lies: most bad asking is not rigged, it is
unaccounted for.

**(v) Broadcast and machine asks.** A public call for comment is addressed to no one in
particular; and I ask search engines things all day.

Broadcast asks generate the obligation jointly rather than severally, which is why institutions
that take them seriously invent joint discharges — the published response to comments, the
editor's report. The machine case is more instructive: querying a database generates no
obligation of uptake, and the account says why without legislating about machine minds. The
obligation tracks the exposure of an agent who stakes a judgment; where nobody is exposed,
nothing is owed. A theory locating the wrong in wasted labour or in deception would have to
strain here, whereas the staked-judgment account predicts the asymmetry directly.

## 8. The consent ladder

The account may look most doubtful at the low end and most obvious at the high end. Making the
gradient explicit is therefore worth doing, because the high end is common ground and my claim
is that it is continuous with the rest. Asks form a ladder by how much of the addressee's agency
they recruit.

At the bottom are **evidential asks**: "what time is it?" The addressee is wanted as a source.
Exposure is slight and the uptake obligation correspondingly slight — but not zero, which is why
asking the time while knowing it is faintly objectionable rather than merely odd.

In the middle are **advisory asks**: "what do you think we should do?" Here the addressee is
recruited as a *participant*, their judgment solicited as judgment and their standing staked far
more heavily. This is where consultation and peer review live, and where Berenstain's cases sit
— which is why the injury in them is an injury to standing as a knower.

At the top are **permissive asks**: "may I?" Here the answer is not information but the exercise
of the addressee's own normative power, and here everyone already agrees with my conclusion: one
who asks permission and proceeds regardless has not merely ignored an answer; the asking was a
nullity and the proceeding is a violation. Nobody thinks the wrong there is wasted breath, or
deception, or discourtesy.

So the top of the ladder is not a special case but the clearest view of a structure present all
the way down. What makes ignoring consent a nullity is that the point of soliciting it was to
let the other's will bear; what makes ignoring advice a weaker wrong of the same family is that
the point of soliciting it was to let the other's judgment bear. The difference is in degree of
recruitment, not in kind. Anyone who accepts the verdict at the top owes an explanation of why
the structure vanishes one rung down, and I do not think there is one.

## 9. What follows

Three consequences worth flagging, briefly.

*For the ethics of institutions.* The standard defence of consultation is instrumental: it
produces better decisions. That defence is insufficient even when true, since it leaves the
consultee's complaint unexplained — an ignored consultee whose view was wrong has still been
wronged — and it predicts the wrong remedy. If the point were information, the remedy for bad
consultation would be better information-gathering; if the point is answerability, the remedy is
publication of how the responses bore. That is a testable difference in institutional design,
and it is the one the *Gunning* fourth criterion already implements.

*For zetetic epistemology.* If ZIP tells the inquirer to take the necessary means to answering
her question, and asking others is the cheapest means, then ZIP unqualified licenses free-riding
on other people's epistemic labour. Friedman's project needs, and does not yet contain, a
constraint on the recruitment of other inquirers. The norms of asking are a candidate, and they
have the right shape: they do not forbid leaning on others, they price it. Berenstain's work is,
among other things, a demonstration of what the unpriced version costs, and of who pays.

*For the second-personal framework.* Darwall's circle runs from authority to accountability:
"the authority to demand implies, not just a reason for the addressee to comply, but also his
being responsible for doing so," and these notions "comprise an interdefinable circle" (Darwall
2006, ch. 1, 17, course PDF). As stated, the circle makes the *addressee* accountable. Asking
shows that for at least one power the implication also runs the other way: the authority to
summon an answer is conditional on the summoner's liability to account for it. If that holds for
asking, it is worth asking — and I mean the question seriously — which other second-personal
powers are held on similar terms. Demanding an apology, calling someone to account, issuing a
reproach: each solicits from another an exercise of agency that exposes them. Perhaps powers to
recruit another's agency are, as a class, held in trust for the exercise they recruit.
