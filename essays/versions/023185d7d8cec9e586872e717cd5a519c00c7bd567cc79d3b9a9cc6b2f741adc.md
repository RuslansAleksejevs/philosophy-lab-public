# Difference without Reconstruction

Remembering a painful afternoon need not hurt. Remembering an intensely coloured sunset need not reproduce its intensity. This difference between having an experience and remembering it raises a problem for a familiar philosophical procedure. To investigate what shapes experience, we often compare how things seemed before and after acquiring a recognitional ability or understanding a sentence. But the earlier experience is gone. If we compare a current experience with a diminished recollection, perhaps the contrast comes from remembering rather than from the change we wanted to investigate.

That is a serious evidential problem. It does not, however, establish that recollection must reproduce an experience's phenomenal character before it can inform us about phenomenal differences. I will defend a narrower requirement and draw out its asymmetric consequences. A procedure that transforms whole experiences, preserving no independently identifiable phenomenal component, can still license an inference from a difference between its outputs to a difference between its inputs. The same procedure may license no inference from matching outputs to matching inputs.

The argument is a reply to a claimed requirement on phenomenal comparison, not a demonstration that human memory meets the replacement requirement. It establishes a consistency result relative to the premises considered: holistic transformation and loss of phenomenal information do not by themselves defeat every contrast. The conditions under which a real recollection supplies evidence remain demanding.

## The objection and its surviving force

Nourbakhshi's discussion of phenomenal contrast supplies the precise target. He argues that recollection and imagination cannot simply replace perception: their own phenomenal differences can explain an apparent contrast. Sections 7–8 consider relevant partial similarities and a response that separates a state's contribution from its content's contribution. He objects that phenomenal parts are interdependent and cannot safely be subtracted; introspection also cannot reliably identify the proposed remainder. The paper advances a strong requirement of full phenomenal reflection, while its conclusion also allows a weaker reading on which distortion supplies a rival explanation (Nourbakhshi 2023, §§4, 6–9).

My reply addresses the strong requirement. The weaker objection survives. A person may remember two phenomenally identical experiences differently, and a theorist must not ignore that possibility. Nor will I claim originality for the general suggestion that relevant similarity can suffice: that response is already considered in the paper. The question is whether a comparison can work without reconstructing the original experience or subtracting an invariant part from a transformed one.

There are two importantly different procedures. One compares a present perception directly with a recollection of an earlier perception. Another compares two recollections obtained through an appropriately matched procedure. My direct result concerns the second. The first involves different routes to the compared states and requires an additional justification. Calling both states representations of experiences does not make their differences informative in the same way.

## What a comparison must preserve

Begin with episodes rather than with a theory of memory. Let (P(e)) denote the phenomenal type of an original episode (e): how the episode felt, considered as a whole. Let (R(e)) denote the phenomenal type of the recollective output obtained from it under a specified comparison procedure. Using phenomenal types leaves open what constitutes those types and whether their differences are sensory, affective, or cognitive.

The crucial condition is:

> For the episodes covered by the comparison, if (P(e_1)=P(e_2)), then (R(e_1)=R(e_2)).

Call this the condition against manufactured differences. Phenomenally identical targets must not acquire different output phenomenal types merely because they pass through the procedure. By contraposition, if the condition holds and the outputs differ, the original phenomenal types differed.

This formulation makes the substantive assumption visible. It is not enough that the mechanism producing memories is deterministic. Two episodes may have different physical histories, associated beliefs, or retrieval circumstances while sharing a phenomenal type. A deterministic mechanism can respond differently to those other differences. Determinism about complete histories therefore does not establish the displayed condition.

When the condition does hold, the output can be treated as a function of the original phenomenal type, (R(e)=F(P(e))), over the comparison's domain. The function need not be the identity. More surprisingly, it need not be injective: it can send different original types to the same output. All that follows directly from unequal outputs is unequal inputs. That elementary point will do the argumentative work; the mathematics itself is not a new discovery.

Consider a finite model:

| Original phenomenal type | Recollective phenomenal type |
| --- | --- |
| a | u |
| b | u |
| c | v |
| d | v |

Here a, b, c, and d are distinct whole phenomenal types. The two recollective types, u and v, are distinct from one another and from every original type. Thus no recollection reproduces any original phenomenal type. Some information is also lost: the procedure does not preserve the difference between a and b, or between c and d.

Nevertheless, if one output is u and the other is v, the originals differed. One came from the a/b group and the other from the c/d group. The conclusion does not depend on knowing which member of each group occurred. It does not depend on recovering any part of the original experience. It is compatible with each output being a thoroughly altered way of experiencing.

This is a consistency model of a restricted inferential possibility. The letters do not demonstrate that a human memory system realizes this pattern, and they do not provide a complete metaphysics of phenomenal types. They show that the failure of full reconstruction does not logically entail the failure of difference detection. Anyone seeking to derive that entailment needs an additional premise that excludes such a pattern.

## Why holism does not close this route

The model has not divided experience into a content component and a memory-mode component. It has not removed the latter and compared what remains. Its inputs and outputs are whole phenomenal types throughout.

Suppose every phenomenal part of a recollection depends on its whole character. Suppose, further, that moving from perception to recollection changes the contribution of every part. Neither supposition says that two identical original phenomenal types must sometimes produce different recollective phenomenal types. Neither says that all original types must become the same recollective type. The table requires neither such manufactured difference nor such total collapse.

Holism concerns dependence within phenomenal wholes. The condition against manufactured differences concerns a relation between the types of wholes at different stages. Those claims can coexist. It would take a further argument to show that interdependence within a recollection makes the relevant relation between original and recollective types unavailable. Simply insisting that there are no detachable phenomenal components does not supply that argument.

An objector might say that the table covertly preserves a component after all: membership in the a/b group rather than the c/d group. But a partition of possible whole states is not thereby a part of each state. We can classify whole melodies as familiar or unfamiliar without committing ourselves to a separable familiarity sound within each melody. Likewise, the model's grouping is a relation used to describe the transformation, not an item phenomenally present inside every experience. Calling every preserved distinction a phenomenal component would erase the distinction between structural preservation and preservation of parts.

The response therefore grants what the subtractive reply disputes. The original phenomenal whole need never appear again, and there need be no unchanged phenomenal remainder to inspect. The comparison can still carry information about whether two original wholes differed.

## A difference is easier to preserve than a verdict of sameness

Return to two outputs that both have type u. Their inputs might have been a and a. They might instead have been a and b. The outputs alone do not settle which. Matching recollections therefore do not entail matching original phenomenal types under the weak condition.

To license that converse inference, the transformation would also have to avoid erasing original differences: distinct inputs would need distinct outputs. That is injectivity. Full phenomenal reconstruction would be stronger still, since an injective transformation may systematically alter every type while keeping them distinct.

Three requirements should consequently be kept apart. Avoiding manufactured differences can license an inference from output difference to original difference. Avoiding erased differences can additionally license an inference from output equality to original equality. Reproducing the original phenomenal type is unnecessary for either implication.

This asymmetry has a methodological consequence. A critic of a positive recollective contrast should ask whether the procedure can create the observed difference from phenomenally identical targets. Showing that it can forget some other differences does not answer that question. Conversely, a defender who relies on the absence of a recollective contrast must explain why a real original difference would not have disappeared. A general assurance that memory is often useful does not discharge either burden.

The distinction also prevents vividness from serving as a universal credential. A vivid reconstruction could incorporate information acquired later and thereby introduce a difference absent from the original experience. A faint or extensively transformed recollection could, in principle, preserve the division needed for a particular contrast. What matters is the direction of the error relevant to the proposed inference, not resemblance considered without reference to that inference.

None of this makes matching outputs evidentially worthless. With further assumptions about which inputs are likely, they might increase the probability that the inputs matched. The claim is that equality does not follow from the weak condition alone. A distinction between entailment and probabilistic evidence matters as much here as a distinction between the two directions of preservation.

## The assumption real memories must earn

Actual recollection is not plausibly modelled, without qualification, as depending only on original phenomenal type. A more informative description is (R(e)=G(P(e),z(e))), where (z(e)) includes other influences: retrieval cues, elapsed time, associated beliefs, expectations, and attention. The same function G can yield different outputs from identical P values when z differs.

Imagine someone recalling an encounter with a tree before learning its classification and recalling another encounter afterwards. The second memory may come with a sense of familiarity because the person now believes that recognition occurred then. The first may be reconstructed as confused for the complementary reason. Those changes could create different present recollections even if the original visual phenomenology was identical. Merely observing that both outputs are memories produced by the same person would not eliminate this explanation.

The proposed condition therefore must not be treated as a verbal way of certifying whatever contrast interests us. Its justification must address alternative sources of output difference. Depending on the claim, independently supported restrictions on the comparison procedure, on retrieval conditions, or on the kinds of distortion might help. This essay establishes no such calibration. In difficult learning cases, the beliefs that accompany learning may be precisely what prevents the procedure from being appropriately matched.

There is also a complication from noise. Suppose phenomenally identical targets produce the same probability distribution of recollective outputs. It does not follow that two particular outputs will match. Two draws from one distribution can differ. An observed difference therefore cannot be handled by simply applying the deterministic contraposition to a probabilistic memory mechanism. A noisy comparison needs an error model specifying how likely the observation is under identical targets and under the relevant alternatives. The finite model provides no numerical probability for that task.

These concessions preserve the weaker memory objection. Distortion remains a possible explanation of apparent contrast. What changes is its form. It is a challenge to the justification of a particular inference, rather than a consequence of the mere fact that recollection feels different from perception.

## Three objections to the limited result

First, have I assumed the very reliability that needs to be proved? For a practical vindication of human memory, yes: the condition would need defence. But the present target is the alleged necessity of full phenomenal reconstruction. To defeat an inference from non-reconstruction and holism to invalidity, a countermodel may grant a weaker preservation condition and show its compatibility with those premises. It need not establish that actual recollection satisfies it. The result is conditional by design.

This would be an empty manoeuvre if the weaker condition merely restated full reconstruction. The finite model shows that it does not. It allows every output to differ from its target and allows genuine distinctions to disappear. Nor does it simply assume every desired philosophical conclusion: it licenses only the existence of some original phenomenal difference, leaving its nature unexplained.

Second, is this still a phenomenal contrast method, or a new inferential method borrowing its name? There is a legitimate terminological choice here. If a phenomenal contrast method is defined as placing the original phenomenal types before the subject again, the proposal does not preserve that definition. But a restriction built into a definition cannot establish that indirect comparisons are useless for the original philosophical purpose. The relevant question is whether present evidence can support a difference between earlier experiences. Treating recollections as evidence, rather than as reenactments, preserves that question while changing how it is answered.

The change does have a cost. A method attractive for its apparently immediate evidence acquires an explicit dependence on assumptions about a comparison procedure. I do not claim that the revised method retains every advantage of an immediate contrast. Making that cost visible is preferable to either assuming faithful reenactment or declaring all indirect comparison impossible.

Third, even if the earlier experiences differed, why think the difference concerned the feature being investigated? There is no general reason. A perceptual change, an emotional response, and a cognitive change can accompany one another. Fulkerson and Cohen argue that access to a psychological contrast does not establish its phenomenal status; downstream changes also complicate identifying the target difference (2025, §§2–4). My model does not answer their objection by labelling its input set phenomenal. Applied to real data, that description itself requires support.

Even after a phenomenal difference has been established, locating it and explaining it remain further steps. Chudnoff's distinction between phenomenal presence, irreducibility, and independence illustrates why such stronger conclusions cannot be collapsed together (2015, §1). A recollective difference satisfying the proposed condition would establish none of those theses about cognition on its own. In the tree example, it would not show that the original visual experience represented a kind property. Attention or affect might still provide the better explanation.

## What the correction achieves

The lesson is a change in the question asked of imperfect memory. Must it reproduce what it represents? For a contrast of the restricted kind examined here, no. It must preserve the implication the argument uses, and the two directions of implication have different requirements.

A transformation may change every phenomenal type and erase distinctions without manufacturing new differences from identical inputs. Holism does not by itself rule that out. Where the relevant condition can be justified, unequal outputs can support unequal original phenomenal types; equal outputs require more to establish sameness. Where the condition cannot be justified, the contrast remains insecure.

This does not rescue a particular argument for rich perceptual content or cognitive phenomenology. It separates a supposed logical obstruction from the evidential work a successful argument would still have to do. Failure to reenact an experience is not yet failure to learn how it differed from another.
