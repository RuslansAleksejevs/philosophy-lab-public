# What Asking Does: Requests and the Second Dimension of Practical Reason

*Draft 001 — initial manuscript for critical review*

## 1. A small puzzle about asking

A colleague stops you in the corridor and says: "Could you look at my draft this weekend?" Two things are true of this little event, and they do not sit together comfortably.

The first is that the request is cheap to refuse. You may say, "Sorry, I'm swamped" — or even "Sorry, I'd rather not" — and you have done nothing wrong. You need not show that your weekend plans outweigh your colleague's need. You need not weigh anything at all. Almost any genuine reason will do.

The second is that the request is expensive to ignore. If you look through your colleague as though nothing had been said, and walk on, you have wronged them — and you have wronged them even if, had you considered the request, you would have been entitled to refuse. The failure is not that you gave insufficient weight to a consideration. It is that you did not take the matter up at all.

Put the two together and a structural oddity emerges. Whatever a request contributes to your practical situation is so slight that it can be defeated by nearly anything; yet it is so insistent that it cannot be passed over in silence. Considerations that can be defeated by nearly anything are normally the very considerations we are permitted to ignore. Requests are not.

This essay argues that the oddity is real and that it tells us something about the structure of practical reason. My thesis has three parts.

**(T1) The negative claim.** The act of requesting adds no weight — or no weight worth the name — to the first-order balance of reasons bearing on the requested action. Whatever favours your reading the draft (your colleague's need, your friendship, the good of the profession) would favour it just as strongly if the request had never been made.

**(T2) The positive claim.** Requesting is nonetheless the exercise of a genuine normative power. It operates on a second dimension: it places an option on the addressee's *deliberative agenda* and renders the addressee *answerable* for not taking it. The addressee must now either comply or act on a reason not to comply that they could avow to the requester.

**(T3) The taxonomic claim.** Reasons of this sort — call them *inclusionary reasons* — are the positive counterpart of Joseph Raz's exclusionary reasons. Raz defined second-order reasons as reasons to act, or to refrain from acting, for a reason; he developed only the refraining half. Requests occupy the slot his own definition left open.

If this is right, then the long-running dispute about whether requests "really" create reasons has been conducted on the wrong axis. Both sides have asked whether a request adds anything to the scales. The answer, I shall argue, is no — and this is compatible with requesting being one of the most important normative powers we have, because some of morality's work is done not by adding weights but by settling what must be weighed at all.

## 2. The state of play, and a shared assumption

The contemporary literature on requests is organised around a single question: when I ask you to φ, does my asking *create* a reason for you to φ, or does it merely *reveal* a reason you already had?

David Enoch's influential answer is that requests exhibit "robust reason-giving," to be distinguished both from merely epistemic reason-giving (informing you of reasons you already have) and from merely triggering reason-giving (rendering true the antecedent of a conditional you were already under). On Enoch's account, for A robustly to give B a reason by requesting, A must intend to give B a reason to φ, intend B to recognise this intention, and intend B's reason to depend appropriately on B's recognition of A's communicated intention.[^1]

James Lewis adds a condition: the request creates a reason only if the addressee "places sufficient discretionary value" in the requester. This, Lewis argues, explains the discretionary character of requests — their leaving it up to the addressee — which he thinks Enoch's account cannot. For Lewis a request is "an attempt by an addressor to create and communicate a non-obligatory reason for the addressee(s) to perform an action."[^2]

Peter Schaber grounds the power in value: we have the normative ability to create reasons by requesting because it is valuable, both to requesters and to requestees, that people should have it, and the power extends only to respectful requests. Schaber then says something that will matter below: "The reasons requests create have always the same weight." Apparent differences in the force of a partner's request and a neighbour's request he traces to the different weights of the *request-independent* reasons in play (Schaber 2021, §4).

Against all of these stands Daniel Weltman's defence of the epistemic account, on which a request "does not create any reasons and thus does not have any special normative power"; it gives reasons only by revealing normatively relevant information. Weltman's conclusion is deliberately deflationary: "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking" (Weltman 2023, §6).

What unites these positions is an assumption about where the action is. All of them ask whether the request contributes something to the *first-order balance* — to the set of considerations that determine whether the addressee ought to φ. Enoch, Lewis, Schaber and Gläser say it does, in various ways; Weltman says it does not.

The assumption surfaces most clearly at the one point where the alternative comes into view and is waved away. Weltman considers N. G. Laskowski and Kenneth Silver's suggestion that a request can direct the addressee's *attention* to reasons already possessed. He replies that in such cases "there is nothing normatively special about the request. The only normative strength is in the reasons that are attended to (which already exist) and the reasons to attend to those reasons (which also exist prior to the request)." In a footnote he concedes the possibility only to dismiss it: a request "may technically have normative power insofar as you have more reason to attend to a reason brought to your attention, because (for instance) it would be rude to ignore someone's having brought it to your attention. But this normative power has nothing to do with the request being a request rather than any other action" (Weltman 2023, §5.2 and n. 40).

That footnote is where I want to plant a flag. I shall argue that the attention-directing effect of a request is not a psychological by-product with a normative shadow, but the *whole* of the request's distinctive normative work; and that it has everything to do with the request being a request rather than any other action.

There is an older witness for the thought that asking is not reducible to the operations philosophers usually analyse. Thomas Reid divided the powers of the mind into the solitary and the social, the latter being "such operations as necessarily suppose an intercourse with some other intelligent being." His list of social operations is telling: "when he asks information, or receives it; when he bears testimony, or receives the testimony of another; when he asks a favour, or accepts one; when he gives a command to his servant, or receives one from a superior; when he plights his faith in a promise or contract." These, he insists, "suppose understanding and will; but they suppose something more which is neither understanding nor will; that is, society with other intelligent beings." And: "To ask a question is as simple an operation as to judge or to reason; yet it is neither judgment nor reasoning, nor simple apprehension, nor is it any composition of these." Reid's complaint was that in the standard divisions of mental operations "the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain."[^3]

Reid's point was about the philosophy of mind. Mine is its normative analogue. In the standard divisions of practical reasons — weights on a balance, defeaters, cancellers, enablers — the social operations have again been thrown behind the curtain, because the only place the theory has for them is the balance, and the balance is not where they live.

## 3. Five things a theory of requests must explain

Before arguing, let me assemble the data. I take these to be pre-theoretical observations about the practice, not tendentious descriptions.

**(D1) Cheap defeat.** A request can be refused for reasons that do not remotely match its apparent importance. "I have plans" is enough, and one need not say what they are. Refusal does not require a showing that the competing reason is weightier. This is what Lewis calls discretion, and what Lance and Kukla mark by saying that "it is essential to the notion of a request that this reason is not an obligation."[^4]

**(D2) Costly silence.** Requests must be taken up. Ignoring a request — treating it as though it had not been made — is a distinctive failure, and it is a failure *even when refusal would have been permissible*. Lance and Kukla make a closely related observation: treating a request "as though it was just evidence of the speaker's psychological desires" is a way of *defying* it.[^5] One can defy a request without disobeying it, which is already a hint that requests make demands that are not demands to act.

**(D3) Repetition does not accumulate.** If A asks S to φ, and then asks again, and then again, S's reason to φ does not triple. If anything it degrades: repeated asking becomes nagging, and nagging is worse for the requester's cause than asking once. Ordinary weighted reasons do not behave like this. Ten people's needs aggregate, at least on most views; ten askings do not.

**(D4) The residue of refusal is an explanation, not a repair.** If I break a promise I owe apology and often compensation. If I refuse a request I owe, at most, an account of myself — and often not even that, when the account is obvious. Nothing is owed by way of repair, because nothing has been taken. What refusal leaves behind is a *liability to answer*, and that liability is discharged by speech, not by restitution.

**(D5) The silent sufferer.** Suppose someone is drowning and cannot call out. Your reason to save them is not one jot weaker than it would be if they could shout "Help!" Their calling out does not increase the moral urgency of their rescue. This generalises. Take any case in which refusing a request seems seriously wrong, and remove the request while holding fixed the underlying facts and your knowledge of them: the wrong of not acting remains, undiminished. What weight there is belongs to the need, the friendship, the prior favour — never to the asking.

D5 is the datum that supports (T1), and it deserves emphasis because it is so easily missed. When we feel that "you can't just refuse — she *asked* you," we are usually registering that what she asked for matters, or that her asking revealed how much it matters, or that our relationship makes her interests weigh with us. Strip all of that out — hold the addressee's information and the underlying stakes fixed — and the residual contribution of the bare act of asking to the *weight* of the case for complying is, so far as I can tell, nil.

## 4. The Attention–Weight Dilemma

Call any view a **Weight Model** if it holds that the distinctive contribution of a request is a first-order pro tanto reason of some positive weight *w* to do the requested act. Enoch, Lewis, Schaber and Cupit are all committed to some version of this, since all hold that requests add a reason to the addressee's case for φ-ing. The epistemic account denies that the request contributes anything of its own; I treat it separately in §6. The Weight Model faces a dilemma.

**Horn 1: substantial weight.** Suppose *w* is non-negligible — comparable, say, to a modest inconvenience. Then D1 fails. If my colleague's request has the weight of a modest inconvenience, I cannot rightly refuse it merely because I would prefer to see a film, unless seeing the film is worth more than a modest inconvenience, which it typically is not. Yet I can refuse, and rightly. Making *w* large enough to explain why requests grip us at all makes it too large to explain why they release us so easily.

**Horn 2: negligible weight.** So suppose *w* is very small — an epsilon, less than that of any ordinary competing consideration. This is the natural retreat, and something like it is suggested by Schaber's claim that request-created reasons always have the same, modest, constant weight while the real variation lies in request-independent reasons. It preserves D1 elegantly: any genuine competing reason defeats an epsilon.

But now consider two principles about deliberation that are hard to deny:

> **Idleness.** A consideration C is *idle* in a deliberative context D if the balance of reasons in D is the same whether or not C is included.
>
> **Permitted Inattention.** A deliberator is not open to criticism for failing to attend to a consideration she reasonably takes to be idle.

Permitted Inattention is not a concession to human frailty; it is a condition of deliberation being possible at all. Every choice sits in a field of vanishingly small considerations — the penny on the pavement, the marginal aesthetic disvalue of the chair's angle — and a deliberator who attended to all of them would never act. Attention is a scarce resource, and rationality does not require spending it where it cannot change the outcome.

An epsilon-weight reason is idle in every context in which some other, weightier consideration is present and the remaining considerations do not exactly balance. So Permitted Inattention entails that in the overwhelming majority of cases — every case in which you already have some ordinary reason not to comply — you are permitted to give the request no attention whatever.

That contradicts D2. Suppose I am on a hard deadline, which is plainly a sufficient reason to decline to read my colleague's draft. On the epsilon model, I know in advance that the request cannot alter what I ought to do. I may therefore, without criticism, not attend to it: I may let the message sit unopened and unanswered, because I already know its content could not move me. But this is precisely what one may *not* do. Something is owed to the request that is not owed to a penny on the pavement.

So: if *w* is big enough that requests are not rationally ignorable, requests are not cheaply refusable; if *w* is small enough that requests are cheaply refusable, they are rationally ignorable. Either way the Weight Model loses a datum. Call this the **Attention–Weight Dilemma**.

Three replies deserve answers.

*First reply: variable weight.* "The weight of a request varies with the relationship, the stakes, the standing of the requester." Certainly it does — but this does not help. The dilemma is generated pointwise. Fix any case; assign whatever weight you like; the case will fall on one horn or the other. And in the cases where requests are most clearly cheap to refuse — low-stakes requests among equals — they remain just as clearly impossible to ignore. Variation across cases cannot resolve a tension that arises within each case.

*Second reply: the duty to consider is a separate duty.* "Your D2 is explained by a general duty of courtesy or respect, which has nothing to do with the request's weight." This reply concedes the substance. For notice that the duty is *triggered by, directed at, and specific to* the request: it is a duty to take up *this* proposal and answer *this* person. Whatever generates it, the request is what switches it on and fixes its content. That is second-order normative work, and once we admit it, we have the two-dimensional picture I am recommending. The only remaining question is whether there is *also* a first-order weight — and D5 suggests there is not.

*Third reply: the weight is not idle because it can break ties.* "An epsilon reason is not idle in every context; where the other reasons are balanced, it decides." True, and this is why the epsilon model gets D1 right. But tie-breaking contexts are rare and, more to the point, not identifiable in advance. If the request's only possible contribution were to break ties, a deliberator who knows she has a decisive reason to refuse would still be permitted to ignore it, and that is the datum at issue. The reply preserves the model's verdicts in a narrow class of cases at the cost of leaving the main phenomenon unexplained.

## 5. Inclusionary reasons

The dilemma is a reason to look for a second dimension. Raz's framework already contains one, and — this is the taxonomic point — already contains an unoccupied position within it.

Raz's definition is general: "A second-order reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason."[^6] The definition names a genus with two species, and Raz develops only one of them. Exclusionary reasons *remove* considerations from deliberation: the soldier's order, the promise, the authoritative directive all work by taking certain otherwise-relevant reasons off the table rather than by outweighing them. Raz's theory of authority is built on this: an authoritative directive pre-empts rather than competes.

The other species has no settled name. Let us call a second-order reason to act for a reason — equivalently, a reason to give a consideration deliberative uptake — an **inclusionary reason**.[^7]

My proposal is that requesting is the paradigmatic way of creating one. More precisely:

> **The Agenda Thesis.** By requesting that S φ, A (if A has the standing to do so) brings it about that:
> (i) *Agenda*: φ-ing enters S's deliberative agenda — S is required to consider φ-ing, under the description "what A has asked of me"; and
> (ii) *Answerability*: S must either φ, or else not-φ for a reason that S could avow to A.

Two features of this deserve comment.

**The burden is one of production, not of persuasion.** Lawyers distinguish the burden of producing some evidence on an issue from the burden of persuading the tribunal on it; a presumption typically shifts the former without touching the latter.[^8] The structure of a request is the same. Before the request, S owed A nothing on the question whether to φ — the question was not S's to answer. After it, S must put something forward. But what S must put forward need only be a reason, not a *weightier* reason. This is exactly the shape of D1 and D2 taken together: a burden is easy to discharge (D1) and impossible to ignore (D2), because discharging and ignoring are different relations to it.

**The avowability condition does real work.** The reason S acts on must be one S could state to A as an account of the refusal. This is not a demand for actual speech — often it would be absurd to explain oneself out loud — but for a certain relation between the agent's actual motive and the requester. It rules out pretexts: if S refuses because S dislikes A but says "I'm busy," the burden is not discharged, because the reason S acted on is not the one S has offered A. It also rules out acting on no reason at all: a coin flip, or sheer inattention, leaves the burden undischarged even if some excellent reason to refuse happened to be available. Weight models cannot register this difference, because weights attach to the objective situation, not to what the agent acts on. Burdens are discharged by agents, not by facts.

The Agenda Thesis explains the rest of the data as structural consequences.

*Non-aggregation (D3).* Burdens do not stack. Once a question is on the agenda it cannot be put on the agenda again; once S is answerable, S cannot become more answerable by being asked twice. Repetition is normatively idle — which is why it reads as pressure rather than as reason-giving, and why it irritates. Weight models must either predict accumulation, which is false, or postulate a diminishing-returns function, which is ad hoc. The burden model predicts idempotence outright.

*Residue (D4).* If what a request does is shift a burden, then the natural residue of non-compliance is discharge of the burden — an account — rather than repair. Nothing was owed and withheld; a question was opened and must be closed. And it is closed by speech.

*No weight (T1, D5).* The Agenda Thesis says nothing about the strength of the case for φ-ing, which is exactly right. The drowning swimmer's cry puts rescuing her on your agenda; but it was on your agenda anyway, because emergencies conscript attention without anyone's help, and the weight of the reason to rescue comes entirely from the peril.

*Standing.* Not everyone may put anything on anyone's agenda. A stranger may ask me for directions but not for my afternoon; an employer may ask for overtime but not for my political opinions. Where standing is absent, the characteristic wrong is not that the request gives a reason that is then outweighed. It is that the requester *has imposed an agenda item they had no right to impose* — has conscripted attention, and demanded an accounting, without title. This is why intrusive requests feel like impositions rather than like temptations, and why the natural response is often "you have no business asking," a challenge to standing rather than a weighing. It also gives a home to Laskowski and Silver's observation that one can wrong a person precisely by requesting something of them: on the present account, requesting is always the exercise of a power over another's deliberation, and powers can be abused.[^9]

## 6. Objections

**(1) The reduction objection.** "A reason to consider φ-ing is just a first-order reason to perform the act of considering. Your second dimension collapses into the first."

This is the most serious reply, but it relocates the phenomenon rather than eliminating it. Grant that considering is an act with its own reasons. Then the epistemic account's deflationary claim — that requests are normatively inert — is abandoned, since the request now creates a reason for an act, namely considering. And the Weight Model's claim is abandoned too, since the reason created is not a reason to do what was requested.

Moreover, the relocated reason behaves oddly for a first-order reason. It is not defeated by the things that defeat the reason to comply. Being on a deadline is a sufficient reason not to read the draft; it is not a sufficient reason not to answer. The costs of considering are almost always trivially small, yet the requirement is not remotely proportionate to them: it persists where considering is costly, and where the outcome is foreordained. First-order reasons to perform an act are sensitive to the costs and benefits of that act. This requirement is not; it tracks the requester's standing, not the economics of attention.

Finally, the reduction cannot deliver Answerability. "You have reason to think about it" is not "you must comply unless you have a reason not to." The second is a conditional requirement on the requested act itself, and no amount of theorising about the act of considering will generate it.

**(2) The permissiveness objection.** "If any reason discharges the burden, then bigotry discharges it: 'I won't help because I dislike people like you' is a reason, and it is avowable by the bigot."

The objection conflates discharging a burden with acting well. A burden of production is discharged by producing something of the right *type*; it does not certify the quality of what is produced. The bigot discharges the request-burden and does something monstrous for independent reasons — reasons that have nothing to do with requests and everything to do with contempt. Compare a defendant who meets a burden of production with vile testimony: he has met the burden and disgraced himself. What the Agenda Thesis claims is only that the specific wrong of *ignoring* a person is avoided by engaging with them, which seems right. The bigot who refuses to your face wrongs you differently, and in one respect less thoroughly, than the bigot who declines to register that you spoke.

**(3) The gravity objection.** "Surely refusing some requests is gravely wrong. You cannot explain this if requests have no weight."

I can, and D5 shows how. In every such case the gravity is traceable to the content of the request and the situation of the requester, not to the act of asking. Apply the test: delete the request, hold the facts and the addressee's knowledge fixed, and ask whether the wrong of not acting shrinks. It does not. What deleting the request removes is the addressee's *answerability*, not the weight of the case against them. They may still act wrongly; they no longer owe anyone an account.

**(4) The trivialisation objection.** "On your view requests barely matter."

On the contrary. Consider how fiercely we guard the power at issue. We install do-not-disturb settings, unsubscribe links, gatekeepers, office hours, and elaborate conventions of indirection whose function is to let a request be withdrawn before it lands. We say "don't ask me that," and "I'm not going to ask, but —," precisely in order to prevent an agenda item from being created.[^10] None of this makes sense if what a request does is add a small weight to someone's scales; small weights are not worth defending against. All of it makes sense if a request conscripts attention and creates an obligation to answer. Attention is the scarcest thing we have, and a power to commandeer it is not a small power.

**(5) The conventionalist objection.** "All this is etiquette. Different cultures handle requests differently."

Conventions certainly determine *how* requests are made, who may make them of whom, and what counts as an adequate answer. But conventions of this kind are implementations, not grounds. The phenomenon they implement is the one Reid identified: a social operation that necessarily supposes intercourse with another intelligent being, and which is complete only when it is taken up by another mind. To ask and not to be attended to is not a defective transaction; it is a failed one. That is why being ignored insults in a way that being refused does not.

**(6) "Isn't this the epistemic account with extra steps?"**

No, and here is the test that separates them.

> **The Eavesdrop Test.** Case (a): A asks S to help with a move on Saturday. Case (b): S overhears A telling a third party, "I desperately need help on Saturday, and I'd love it if S offered — but I can't bring myself to ask." A does not know S overheard.

By construction, (b) gives S *more* information than (a): S learns A's need, A's desire that S help, and A's estimate of the relationship. On the epistemic account, which holds that requests are normative only by revealing information, (b) should be at least as normatively potent as (a). And on the Weight Model, the difference between (a) and (b) should be a difference in how strongly S is favoured toward helping.

But the observed difference is neither. In (a), S must respond: help, or decline and say something. Silence is not an option. In (b), S may say nothing at all. S may quietly decide not to help and never mention it, and S has not wronged A by failing to answer, however much S may be criticisable for stinginess. The difference is not in how much S has reason to help. It is in whether silence is permissible — that is, in whether S is *answerable to A* on the question.

One might object that (b) contains the extra information that A chose not to ask, which cancels the reason. So vary the case: A does not ask because it never occurs to A that S is available, or because A is shy, or because A is unconscious. Informational parity can be approached as closely as we like. The asymmetry in answerability survives intact, because it was never informational. It was the difference between a consideration being *available* to S and a claim being *addressed* to S.

This, I think, is the kernel of truth in the tradition that treats second-personal address as sui generis. What address does is not to add to the balance but to make the addressee accountable on a question. The Agenda Thesis is one way of saying precisely what that amounts to.

## 7. Beyond requests

If inclusionary reasons exist, requests will not be the only source of them. Three extensions suggest the account earns its keep.

**Questions.** Reid put asking a question and asking a favour on the same list, and rightly. A question creates an obligation to answer that is cheap to discharge ("I don't know"; "I'd rather not say") and impossible to ignore. Refusing to answer is permissible; failing to register that one was asked is not. That is the same profile, and it is unexplained by any view on which a question merely supplies the addressee with a reason to utter a true sentence.

**Procedural rights.** Legal standing, the right to be heard, the right to petition: these are powers to put a claim on an institution's agenda. They are not powers to win. It is a familiar puzzle why such rights should matter independently of outcomes — why a hearing one was always going to lose is nonetheless something one was owed. The two-dimensional picture answers it. The right to be heard is an inclusionary power: it makes an institution answerable on a question, and being answerable is a distinct normative status from being obliged to decide in one's favour. On a one-dimensional view of practical reason, procedural rights can only look like instruments for producing better outcomes, which is why one-dimensional views keep failing to account for them.

**Protest.** Much political speech is not an attempt to shift anyone's balance of reasons; the reasons are usually already there and already known. It is an attempt to force a question onto an agenda and to make someone answerable for how they respond. The characteristic complaint of the unheard is not "you weighed us too lightly" but "you did not even consider us." That complaint is unintelligible if weight is the only normative dimension, and straightforward if it is not.

## 8. Conclusion

The literature on requests has been asking whether asking adds anything to the scales. I have argued that it does not, and that this is not a deflationary conclusion but a redirection. What asking does is to put something on the agenda and to make the addressee answerable for it: it creates an inclusionary reason, the positive species of the second-order genus that Raz defined and then left half-explored.

The view earns its place by explaining a profile no weight-based account can capture: cheap refusal together with costly silence, non-accumulation under repetition, an explanatory rather than reparative residue, and the sharp asymmetry between overhearing a need and being asked. It also makes sense of what we in fact protect. We do not build elaborate defences against small increments to our reasons. We build them against having our attention conscripted and our silence made culpable.

If there is a general moral, it is this. A theory of practical reason that recognises only weights will keep mislocating the phenomena of second-personal life, because the most important thing another person can do to you, short of obliging you, is not to tip your scales but to make you answer. Reid thought the social operations of mind had been thrown behind the curtain. So, I think, have the social operations of practical reason.
