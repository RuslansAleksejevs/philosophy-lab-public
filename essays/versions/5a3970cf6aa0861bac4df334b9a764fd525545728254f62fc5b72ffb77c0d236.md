# What Introspection Preserves

You direct attention to a faint bodily sensation and now discern a definite pressure. Was that pressure already part of your experience? An affirmative answer is tempting: attention apparently revealed what was there. But another possibility is that attention helped produce the definite phenomenal character you now report. The report may be entirely correct about the present without settling the past. Conversely, a sensation that disappears under scrutiny need not have been absent before scrutiny began.

This familiar difficulty is often treated as a reason to mistrust introspection or as a problem to be solved by making introspection less intrusive. Both responses overlook a useful intermediate possibility. An intervention can change its target while preserving enough about it to support a particular conclusion. We need not establish that introspection leaves experience unchanged. We need to establish that it does not make the specific kind of change that would invalidate the inference we want to draw.

I defend a directional account of this requirement. A positive report about an attended experience supports a positive claim about the earlier experience only insofar as the transition is constrained against producing the reported feature from its absence. A negative report supports a negative claim about the earlier experience only insofar as the transition is constrained against eliminating that feature. These are different conditions. A method can satisfy one without satisfying the other. The distinction explains why repeated introspective agreement may improve confidence in a present report while leaving its retrospective application unsupported. It also shows how a locally limited form of first-person authority can survive interference without deciding the wider question of introspection's reliability.

## 1. Accurate about what?

Consider three claims about the pressure example:

1. There is a definite pressure in my experience now.
2. There was a definite pressure in my experience just before I attended to it.
3. Attending to the sensation preserved whether there was a definite pressure.

The first claim concerns the attended experience. The second concerns an earlier experience. The third concerns their relation. Even granting the first claim maximal authority does not grant the third. Nor does the first entail the second. A theorist who treats the subject as an impeccable observer of her current experience can still question what her observation establishes about the experience preceding scrutiny.

This is not the discovery that introspection can interfere with experience. Spener distinguishes introspective access from the method in which it is employed, and explains why reports of experience altered by attention cannot automatically characterize experience outside that setting. Her discussion supplies the starting point here, particularly the distinction between natural and forced episodes (Spener 2022, §§1, 2.2–3). The proposed advance is a more selective account of the support needed to cross that boundary: preserving every feature is unnecessary, and even preserving the truth value of one feature may demand more than the intended inference requires.

The target must first be specified accurately. “Pressure” might mean any felt pressure, a pressure above a specified apparent intensity, or a pressure with a determinate spatial boundary. An intervention can preserve the first while creating the third. It is therefore unhelpful to ask simply whether attention changes the sensation. Likewise, “before introspection” must designate an interval rather than an indefinitely extended background. The difference between an experience immediately before an attentional shift and the experience of the previous hour is not just an increase in difficulty; the relevant transitions differ.

My argument concerns an inference from a report of a present attended condition to a claim about an earlier condition. It does not assume that every introspective judgment works this way. Someone may remember an experience directly, or register an ongoing experience without deliberately attending to it. If a report is genuinely about the past from the outset, its evidential status depends on that retrospective capacity. It should not be reconstructed as a present report merely to make it vulnerable to the argument.

## 2. Two histories, one truthful report

Let F be a specified phenomenal feature. Suppose a subject accurately reports F after directing attention toward her experience. Two histories can produce this situation. In the first, F was present and the transition retained it. In the second, F was absent and the transition produced it. If the present report is equally expected under both histories, the report alone does not discriminate between them.

The point is epistemic, not a claim that both histories are always equally plausible. Prior knowledge may overwhelmingly favor preservation. A familiar constant sensation might have a well-supported history of surviving comparable attentional changes. But this support comes from more than the accuracy of the present report. One cannot establish preservation simply by insisting that the subject is right about what she now experiences. The two histories grant precisely that correctness.

It helps to separate observation from transition. Write F-before and F-after for the presence of the same specified feature at the two times. The following implication blocks the production history:

If F-after, then F-before.

Call this the nonproduction condition. It does not say that nothing changes. It allows the transition to remove F, to alter other features, or to increase the intensity of F while keeping it within the category under consideration. What it excludes is a transition from the absence of F to its presence. Given an accurate positive report and this condition, a positive retrospective inference follows.

The condition needed for a negative retrospective inference runs the other way:

If F-before, then F-after.

Call this the nonelimination condition. If the subject accurately reports that F is absent afterward, and the transition could not have removed it, F was absent beforehand. This condition permits production, alteration of other features, and any changes that leave F present. It excludes only a transition from presence to absence.

These elementary implications matter because ordinary assurances about attention often point in just one direction. Suppose someone argues that directing attention to a feature can enhance it but cannot suppress it. Even if this were established, it would support a negative retrospective inference, not the positive inference commonly associated with attention's revealing power. Failure to find the feature would count against its prior presence, provided failure to report is an accurate report of absence. Finding the feature would remain compatible with its having been produced. Conversely, if attention can suppress a feature but cannot produce it, finding it would support its prior presence, whereas failing to find it would be inconclusive.

There is no paradox. Turning on a device that can only add a signal makes a remaining absence informative about the earlier absence. It does not make a resulting presence informative about the earlier presence. What is distinctive about introspection is not the logic but the temptation to bypass it by appealing to first-person privilege. Privilege over the output does not determine the direction of the transformation.

Full preservation combines nonproduction and nonelimination. It licenses both positive and negative transfers. But asking for full preservation when only one transfer is at issue sets an unnecessarily demanding standard. Equally, citing a change in experience without identifying its direction can be an insufficient objection. The relevant question is whether the change opens the particular alternative history that would defeat the conclusion.

These formulations describe strict inferences. Real evidence will usually justify only probabilistic versions: production is unlikely here, or elimination is rare under these conditions. An accurate positive report then supports earlier presence to a degree determined by both the transition probabilities and the prior alternatives. Calling production unlikely is not enough if earlier absence is overwhelmingly more probable. The directional framework identifies the relevant dependencies; it does not supply their numerical values or turn default trust into certainty.

## 3. Why looking again can miss the problem

Repeated scrutiny seems an obvious remedy for introspective uncertainty. A subject attends, reports a determinate pressure, attends again, and gives the same report. This can strengthen confidence that she is not simply misspeaking or making a transient classificatory mistake. Yet it need not strengthen the inference about what preceded the first act of attention.

Imagine a process that turns an initially diffuse sensation into a determinate one. Once established, the determinate sensation persists under further attention. Every subsequent report is accurate and stable. The initial transition nevertheless produced the feature being investigated. Repetition samples the persistence of the transformed condition. It does not repeat the transition from the original condition.

Now compare a process that merely preserves an already determinate sensation. After the first attentional shift, these two processes may yield the same entire sequence of reports. Even unlimited agreement within that sequence cannot, by itself, identify which initial transition occurred. This is not the claim that repetition never helps. It helps if the competing histories make different predictions about later scrutiny. The point is that persistence after a first intervention and preservation through that intervention are different propositions.

There are consequently two distinct forms of calibration. We may calibrate the judgment: does the subject discriminate and describe the experience she has while reporting? Or we may calibrate the transfer: does that reporting procedure preserve the feature relevant to claims about the prior condition? Success at the former does not constitute success at the latter. This remains true if discrimination becomes extremely fine or subjects acquire a shared vocabulary. Such achievements may make reports of transformed experiences extraordinarily accurate.

Training can also supply transfer evidence. Perhaps variations in the timing or kind of attentional demand produce patterns that one transition model predicts and another does not. But the evidential gain then comes from discriminating the models, not from agreement as such. Similarly, returning repeatedly to a genuinely comparable initial condition differs from continuing to inspect an already modified condition. The distinction should guide interpretation even when neither form of evidence is easy to obtain.

This argument also limits a familiar skeptical response. Schwitzgebel emphasizes both error and indecision in introspection and describes the instability of experiences under reflection (2008, §§XII–XIII). The present concern can remain even after those difficulties are provisionally removed. A perfectly stable, correctly classified experience can be the wrong temporal target. Conversely, temporal transfer can be warranted despite instability when the instability does not threaten the feature or direction at issue. Reliability of observation and legitimacy of retrospective use should therefore receive separate verdicts.

## 4. What a cue establishes

A dispute about reports of briefly presented arrays illustrates the stakes. A subject may be able to identify selected elements after receiving a cue even though she cannot report all the elements together. One interpretation is that the later report retrieves phenomenal detail already present. Another allows the cue to help generate a more determinate experience from information that was previously represented without that phenomenal detail. Sergent and Rees explicitly propose attentional modification as an alternative to pure readout, while distinguishing access to coarse attributes from detailed reports (2007, p. 524).

The directional account specifies the extra burden involved in moving from successful cued identification to prior phenomenal detail. The relevant feature must be the experienced identity or detail in question, rather than the mere presence of stimulus information somewhere in the system. Evidence that information survived the display does not, without further premises, establish that its phenomenal form survived unchanged. A process can preserve information while altering whether or how that information is experienced.

Assume for argument that the subject's cued report accurately describes the phenomenal detail she then has. To infer that the same detail was experienced before cueing, one must have reason against its production during the intervening process. It is insufficient to say that the cue could not insert the correct letter's identity because the cue did not contain that identity. The production hypothesis need not say that the cue supplies the information. The cue might alter the use of information already stored. Producing a phenomenal feature and producing the underlying information are different possibilities.

This is not a refutation of phenomenal overflow, the thesis that phenomenal consciousness can exceed cognitive access. A larger body of evidence may support the relevant nonproduction assumption or a theory under which prior phenomenal detail is the best explanation. Nor does the possibility of production establish that the earlier representation was unconscious. The earlier experience might contain coarse features while lacking the later determinate feature. The conclusion is instead a constraint on one evidential step: correctness after cueing cannot do all the work required to identify experience before cueing.

The same constraint applies to the opposing inference. If a subject cannot report a feature after a demanding introspective task, earlier absence does not follow unless the procedure preserved that feature and the reporting process was sufficiently sensitive. Those who invoke the constructive effects of attention against positive reports cannot simply forget its potentially suppressive effects when interpreting negative ones. There is no uniform burden favoring a richer or sparser account of consciousness. The burden follows the direction of each proposed inference.

## 5. Is the requirement too demanding?

One objection says that ordinary knowledge never requires an explicit theory of how observation preserves its object. I can know that a cup was on the table before I looked without establishing a formal nonproduction condition. Why should introspection face a stricter test?

It should not. The conditions above describe what supports an inference, not what every knower must articulate. Ordinary background knowledge often excludes the relevant production history well enough. Nor does merely imagining that attention creates a feature defeat everyday knowledge. A live concern requires some reason to take that transition seriously: the nature of the task, evidence of attentional alteration, a credible competing explanation, or a conclusion that explicitly concerns the very dimension attention is being used to sharpen. In a dispute where that concern is already at issue, repeating the current report leaves the dispute untouched. The account locates a burden of response without imposing universal prior certification.

A second objection appeals to direct acquaintance. Perhaps the phenomenal feature is itself involved in the introspective judgment, leaving no room for error about it. Grant this for the sake of argument. It secures a relation between the judgment and its present constituent or object. It does not establish that the same kind of feature occurred before the judgment-generating activity began. A theory could secure direct access to an earlier experience as well, but that would be an additional account of retrospection. The directional argument is compatible with very strong current authority; that compatibility is part of its significance.

A third objection asks whether calibration is circular. To learn that attention does not produce F, must we not compare experience before attention with experience afterward, thus attending to the supposedly unattended experience? Some proposed calibrations will indeed reproduce the original difficulty. The answer is not to declare any agreement sufficient. It is to distinguish observations that depend on the disputed transition from constraints that discriminate between transition models. Retrospective judgments, spontaneous reports, task variations, and independently supported mechanisms may contribute, but none is automatically decisive merely because it bears a different label. Their evidential dependencies must be examined.

The account allows an uncomfortable result: for some features in some conditions, available evidence may not discriminate between production and preservation. That is a limit on knowledge of the earlier feature, not proof that it was absent, proof that it was present, or proof that present introspection fails. Recognizing that local indeterminacy is preferable to allowing confidence in one question to answer another.

Finally, one might object that the proposed framework only restates the obvious fact that inferences need premises. In one sense it does: its logic is deliberately elementary. But the philosophical use is more precise than a demand for “further evidence.” It identifies two independently defeasible premises, assigns each to a different polarity of report, and explains why an apparently stronger introspective procedure can leave the crucial premise untouched. It thereby changes what counts as a relevant defense or criticism. Demonstrating some modulation is not always a sufficient criticism; demonstrating repeated accuracy is not always a sufficient defense.

## 6. The scope of first-person authority

Introspection can provide evidence whose strength varies with the conclusion drawn from it. The same accurate report may settle whether a feature is present during scrutiny, support a claim that the feature can occur, and remain neutral about whether it occurred before scrutiny. There is no contradiction in these different assessments. They concern different targets and different inferential routes.

The distinction offers a disciplined middle position between treating introspection as a transparent window onto ordinary experience and dismissing it whenever it changes what it examines. An intervention need not preserve an entire experience to preserve something worth knowing. It may license a positive inference while blocking a negative one, or the reverse. And a limitation on temporal transfer need not be a limitation on the subject's knowledge of what she experiences now.

When an introspective report is contested, the first question should therefore be what it is being asked to establish. If the conclusion concerns experience before attention, its defender must identify why the relevant transition could not, or probably did not, create the misleading appearance of continuity. Its critic must identify a change capable of defeating that particular inference. Both sides should resist treating truthful reporting, stable reporting, and preservation of the original target as interchangeable achievements. Knowing exactly what introspection preserves is a more useful ambition than demanding that it preserve everything.
