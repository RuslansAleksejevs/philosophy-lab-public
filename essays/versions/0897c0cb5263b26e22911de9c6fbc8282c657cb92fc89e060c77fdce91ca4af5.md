# Asking Makes You Answerable: The Question as a Normative Power

**Abstract.** The norms of assertion have been studied intensively, and the norms of inquiry
lately too. The norms of *asking* — of putting a question to another person — remain almost
untouched. I argue that asking is a normative power: it puts the addressee under a burden of
response, and it makes the asker answerable to the addressee for what becomes of the answer.
The defence turns on what answering involves. An answer is not a commodity transferred but a
judgment staked, and soliciting a staked judgment whose bearing one has foreclosed is inducing
a liability whose point one has destroyed. I then argue that this is not a moral
side-constraint but a validity condition: where it fails the utterance is "an act of speech but
no speech act," and the addressee owes nothing, not even an excuse for silence. The account
explains the retort "then why are you asking?", and it generalises a pattern Berenstain has
described in the case of oppression — demanding epistemic labour and dismissing its fruits —
into a diagnosis that also covers the sham consultation, the rigged hearing, and the friend who
asks for advice she has no use for.

---

## 1. Then why are you asking?

Three complaints, none of them a complaint about being lied to.

**Consultation.** A council publishes a draft scheme and invites residents to comment. The
scheme has in fact been fixed; the comments will be read, counted and filed. Suppose everybody
involved knows this. Residents comment anyway, or bitterly decline. Nobody has been deceived or
coerced, and yet the exercise is a paradigm of public bad faith.

**Advice.** You ask what I think you should do about your marriage. I give you an hour and tell
you something hard. You had already decided, and say so afterwards, cheerfully, as though this
were of no consequence. I feel used. I cannot easily say I was harmed — the hour was freely
given and I may even have enjoyed it — and I cannot say you were insincere, since you did want
to hear me.

**Interrogation.** A courteous interlocutor asks me in public for evidence of a claim I have
made. I supply it. He asks again, slightly differently. This continues. He is unfailingly
polite and has misrepresented nothing. But whatever I produce will be met with another
question, and the eventual verdict — his and the audience's — was settled before the first one.

In each case the natural retort is the same, and it sounds *constitutive* rather than moral:
**then why are you asking?** Not "you shouldn't have asked," which would concede that an asking
occurred, but a demand to be shown that the act was one of its kind at all.

This essay takes that retort seriously. Asking a question of another person is the exercise of
a normative power, and its authority is grounded in the asker's own liability to account.
Asking makes you answerable. Where the asker is not in a position to be answerable, no question
has been put, and the addressee owes no answer and no excuse.

The neglect here is real and slightly odd. The semantics of interrogatives is a large and
sophisticated field; the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on questions is given over almost
entirely to it, and what it records about the act of asking is a pair of classificatory
remarks: that asking is "standardly regarded, e.g., by Searle (1969, 69), as a special case of
the illocutionary act of requesting," and that on Åqvist's rival proposal "to ask a question is
to command the hearer to cause the speaker to know the question's answer" (Cross and Roelofsen
2026, §1.1). Both place asking among the directives; neither asks what the directing costs the
director. Epistemology has meanwhile turned to the norms of inquiry, but those norms are
first-personal: Friedman's zetetic instrumental principle says that "if one wants to figure out
Q?, then one ought to take the necessary means to figuring out Q?" (Friedman 2020, §1; author's
PDF, 2). Asking somebody is a means, usually the cheapest. What the inquirer owes the person
they lean on is not a question the zetetic literature has yet had to face.

## 2. Four diagnoses, and one that gets closer

Three natural first thoughts about §1 fail. The fourth has identified the phenomenon without
giving the explanation I am after.

**(a) Insincerity.** The asker did not really want to know. But in all three cases the asker may
sincerely want to hear the answer. The council is genuinely curious; the friend genuinely wanted
my view; the interlocutor may be interested in my evidence as a collector is interested in
specimens. Wanting to hear is not wanting to be *moved*. Sincerity, as Austin and Searle use it,
attaches to the psychological state the act expresses, and the trouble here lies in what the
asker will *do*.

**(b) Deception.** The asker implied openness and lacked it. Often true, but not the wrong,
because the wrong survives full disclosure. Let the council print "our decision is final" on the
document; let my friend say in advance "I've decided, but tell me anyway"; let my interlocutor
announce that nothing I say will change his mind. Deception is now impossible and the exercise
is worse, not better. A wrong aggravated by being announced is not a species of deception.

**(c) Harassment or bad faith.** Claeys's treatment of sealioning takes this route and takes it
well. Adopting Johnson's characterisation — sealioning "fuses persistent questioning ... with a
loudly-insisted-upon commitment to reasonable debate" (Johnson 2017, 13, quoted in Claeys 2024)
— she argues it has two goals, "the political goal of forestalling action on the one hand, and
the epistemic goal of undermining the victim's authority on the other," and extends the analysis
via Sartre to the "good-willed" questioner who is culpably ignorant of what he is doing (Claeys
2024). Persuasive for its cases; but the council harasses nobody, and my friend is not in bad
faith about herself, merely careless about what she has done to me. We need a wrong available to
the well-meaning, the courteous and the self-transparent.

**(d) Exploitation, and the demand-and-dismiss pattern.** The obvious thought is that answering
costs something and the asker has extracted it for nothing. Berenstain's "epistemic exploitation"
is the developed version: it "occurs when privileged persons compel marginalized persons to
produce an education or explanation about the nature of the oppression they face," and is "a
variety of epistemic oppression marked by unrecognized, uncompensated, emotionally taxing,
coerced epistemic labor" (Berenstain 2016, §2).

Cost alone would not generalise, since the complaint survives its absence. Suppose the answer is
a single syllable cheerfully given: "Do you mind if I bring my dog?" — "Not at all." If I had
already loaded the dog into the car I have wronged you, and not by costing you a syllable.
Conversely a costly answer may be solicited perfectly properly, by somebody who then lets it do
its work. The complaint tracks *uptake*, not *expenditure*.

But cost is not all there is to Berenstain, and here I must be careful, because she sees the
thing I am after. Unpaid labour is the first of three features she names; the others are the
double bind and "the default skeptical responses from the privileged when the marginalized do
acquiesce and fulfill their demands" (§3). And in §5 she asks my question almost verbatim: "How
can we make sense of the fact that the privileged demand labor from the marginalized and then
fail to make use of the fruits of that labor?" Her description is exact — such demands "may
appear to be invitations to participate in knowledge production," yet the privileged
"demonstrate entitlement to the labor and means of production of knowledge but fail to actually
recognize the goods produced as contributions to knowledge" (§5). She lists "just asking a
question" among the euphemisms the practice travels under (§2) and, quoting Dotson, locates the
failure in *uptake* (Dotson 2012, 32, quoted in Berenstain 2016, §4).

So the phenomenon is not mine to discover; what is left is to explain it, and our explanations
differ in kind. Hers is *functional*: demand-and-dismiss persists because it does work, upholding
active ignorance and "the illusion that there simply are no viable alternatives" (§5). That suits
its subject and I am not disputing it. But it explains the practice by what it achieves for a
system of domination, and so says nothing about the council, my friend, or the examiner, where
no domination is in play and the pattern recurs anyway. My explanation is *normative*: the defect
lies inside the act of asking, as the failure of a condition on a power. That is why it reaches
cases hers is not built for, and why, as §5 argues, it yields something her double bind leaves
open.

What (a)–(c) share is that they treat the asking as normatively inert and look for the wrong
*around* it. My proposal is that the asking is not inert.

## 3. Asking as a normative power

A normative power is a capacity, exercised intentionally and recognised as such, to change the
normative situation by exercising it. Promising is the standard case: it is "a special sort of
power we have over our normative circumstances, the power to invoke obligations by promissory
utterance," the distinguishing feature being that "promisors obligate themselves directly, by
their own powers" rather than via convention or engendered expectation (Habib 2022, §1).
Consenting is another: my permission alters what you may do to me.

Asking has three marks of a power.

*First, it burdens.* Once a question has been put to me, my silence needs a licence. I may
answer, or say "I'd rather not," or "that's not your business," or "look it up" — but some such
move is now called for, and bare non-response is a small offence that it was not a moment
earlier. Contrast being told something: I may receive an assertion in total silence without
owing anybody anything.<sup>[1]</sup> The burden is defeasible and often tiny, but normative
rather than merely social, as is shown by the fact that "you didn't answer my question" is a
rebuke, and that our most formal institutions — the subpoena, discovery, the parliamentary
question — are recognisably elaborations of it rather than exceptions to it. The classificatory
tradition agrees: asking belongs with requests and orders, whose "essential condition has to do
with the fact that the utterance is an attempt to get the hearer to do it" (Searle 1965, 15);
and on Åqvist's account asking *commands* the hearer to bring it about that the asker knows.

*Second, it authorises.* A question confers on its addressee a standing to speak that they may
not otherwise have had. "You asked" is a complete defence to the charge of offering an unwanted
opinion. The asker opens a door, and what comes through it enters licensed.

*Third — the thesis — it makes the asker answerable.* Having summoned the answer, the asker owes
the answerer both that the answer be allowed to bear, and an account, if asked, of how it bore.

To make the third mark precise: every ask is put in the service of some attitude or decision of
the asker's, which I shall call its **bearing point**. For "what time is it?" the bearing point
is my belief about the time. For an examiner's question it is not his belief about the subject
matter — he knows the answer — but his assessment of the candidate; Searle's distinction between
"requesting information" and "requesting that the hearer display knowledge" (Cross and Roelofsen
2026, §1.1) is a distinction between bearing points. For "may I borrow your car?" it is my
decision whether to take it.

> **Live Dependence (LD).** At the time of asking, there are two answers within the range the
> asker takes the addressee might give such that the asker's bearing-point attitude, formed as
> the asker intends to form it, would differ between them.

> **Accounting (AC).** The asker is disposed, on the addressee's request and within reasonable
> limits, to say how the answer bore on the bearing point.

> **Standing (ST).** The asker is entitled to impose on this addressee the burden that answering
> would involve.

LD and ST are *validity* conditions on the power: where they fail, the utterance does not
exercise it. AC is an obligation *generated by* a valid exercise, so failing it is a breach
rather than a nullity. The division tracks Austin's, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* has it: a
misfire is a case in which "the putative speech act fails to be performed at all" and one has
"performed an act of speech but no speech act," whereas an abuse is a case in which the act
comes off but "fails to live up to a standard appropriate for speech acts of its kind" (Green
2020, §2.2).

Two clarifications, since the thesis is easily over-read. LD does **not** require that the asker
be undecided, unconfident, or likely to change their mind; I may be ninety-five per cent sure
and satisfy it comfortably. It requires only that the answer be *positioned* to make a
difference. And AC does not require justification: the asker need not persuade the answerer that
the answer was rightly weighed, only be able to show it was received as an answer rather than as
noise.

## 4. Why the asker is answerable: the argument from staked judgment

Why should soliciting an answer bind the solicitor? Commands do not bind the commander, and
requests for objects do not oblige the requester to use them.

The answer lies in what an answer *is*. Compare asking you to pass the salt with asking you
where the salt is. In the first you supply a thing, and the thing is indifferent to your
supplying it. In the second you supply *your judgment*: you advance a claim on your own
authority and invite me to take it on your say-so. You stake yourself. If you are wrong you are
on the hook as no conduit is — "you told me it was in the cupboard" is a reproach — and even "I
don't know" is a self-report that exposes you.

> **P1.** In answering, one does not transfer a commodity but advances a judgment on one's own
> authority, thereby assuming a liability for it (for its truth, its competence, or its candour).
>
> **P2.** To ask is to induce the addressee to assume that liability.
>
> **P3.** It is wrong to induce another to assume a liability while having already foreclosed the
> benefit for the sake of which the liability is assumed, unless they freely accept this.
>
> **P4.** The benefit for the sake of which an answerer assumes that liability is that their
> judgment should *count* — be received as a judgment and allowed to bear.
>
> **C.** So an asker who has foreclosed the answer's bearing wrongs the answerer, absent the
> answerer's release.

P3 is the moral engine and it is not peculiar to speech. It is why soliciting tenders for a
contract already awarded is a fraud on the bidders even if their costs are trivial, and why
inviting applications to a filled post wrongs the applicants even if they enjoyed writing them.
In each case someone is induced to expose themselves — to be judged, to be on record, to be
refusable — in exchange for a consideration the inviter has already withdrawn. The wrong is not
theft of labour; it is **exposure without reception**.

P1 and P4 carry the weight, and they are what make asking unlike commanding. A command treats
the addressee as an agent whose *doing* is wanted. A question treats them as an agent whose
*take* is wanted, and a take cannot be received while its author is bypassed, in the way a
wrench can be received while its fetcher is ignored. The deliverable of an ask is inseparable
from the deliverer's standing as one who judges; discard it and you have not merely declined to
use a good, you have solicited a person's credit and declined to bank it. This is also why
Berenstain's pattern is specifically an injury to standing as a knower rather than a labour
grievance: demanding the labour while refusing to "recognize the goods produced as contributions
to knowledge" (2016, §5) is exposure without reception, under conditions that make the exposure
far worse.

It explains the gradient of complaint in §1, too. The cost of a syllable is nil, but the
exposure is not: in answering "not at all" you made yourself the author of a permission. When it
emerges that the dog was already in the car, what you resent is that your authorship was
counterfeit.

## 5. The presupposition argument, and why the verdict is nullity

§4 shows that non-responsive asking wrongs the answerer. A second argument shows that the
utterance fails to be an asking at all.

Second-personal address, Darwall says, "presupposes" an authority: "her having the authority is a
necessary condition of the validity of the reason she purports to address and is thus a normative
felicity condition of successfully giving her addressee the reason" (Darwall 2006, ch. 1, 10–11,
course PDF). What authority does asking presuppose? Not authority over the addressee's conduct in
general — strangers may ask me the time. Something narrower:

> **The Presupposition Argument.** To ask B rather than merely to wonder is to treat B as someone
> whose answer can move one. To be such that no answer B could give would move one is to treat B
> as someone whose answer cannot. One who asks while foreclosing the answer's bearing therefore
> occupies both sides of a contradiction in the presupposition that made the utterance
> intelligible as an ask. It does not come off: it is an act of speech but no ask.

This is a structural verdict, not a moral one, and it is what the retort of §1 registers. "Then
why are you asking?" is not "you should not have asked"; it is a demand for the bearing point, a
challenge to validity of exactly the form Austinian felicity conditions license. And it is
available where nobody is deceived, harassed, or out of pocket — which is what the diagnoses of
§2 could not accommodate.

> **No Standing Without Answerability.** An invalid ask imposes no burden of response. The
> addressee owes no answer — and, what matters more, owes no excuse for not answering.

This speaks to the double bind, which both my main interlocutors describe and neither dissolves.
Claeys's target "is left with the choice to either respond (and tire herself needlessly) or seem
either unreasonable or unwilling" (Claeys 2024). Berenstain's version is sharper, since the
second horn carries a racialised penalty: Amina, asked by an acquaintance why she was "so rude"
to a stranger who touched her hair, "knows that if she refuses to explain herself ... she runs
the risk of being painted as overly emotional, irrational, hypersensitive, unfriendly, and
aggressive," and so "faces a double bind" (Berenstain 2016, §3). Berenstain's diagnosis is that
"the privileged feel entitled to the time and energy of the marginalized," so that failing to
supply it is read as "shirking a duty" (§3). Just so — and the present account says what is
wrong with that reading. There was no duty. An ask that forecloses its own bearing point, or
that is put by someone with no title to put it, generates no burden of response. Amina is not
declining to discharge an obligation; there is no obligation, because there was no question,
only an utterance shaped like one.

I should not oversell this. The nullity verdict removes the *normative* default, not the social
penalty. Amina may still be called aggressive; that is what Berenstain's structural analysis is
about, and nothing in speech-act theory disarms it. What the verdict does is deny the penalty its
justification, which is not nothing: much of the force of "but you didn't answer" depends on the
appearance of a shirked duty, and showing that appearance counterfeit is a precondition of
contesting the penalty rather than absorbing it.

It is worth noting how unusual it is to find an independent institution reaching the same
verdict. English administrative law has for forty years recognised conditions on lawful
consultation — the "Sedley criteria," endorsed by the Supreme Court in *R (Moseley) v Haringey
LBC* — of which the fourth is "that the product of consultation must be conscientiously taken
into account in finalising any statutory proposals" ([2014] UKSC 56, [25], quoting *R v Brent
LBC, ex p Gunning* (1985) 84 LGR 168, 189). And the verdict on a consultation whose framing had
foreclosed the live alternatives was not that it was defective: it was that "in reality,
therefore, there was no consultation on the fundamental basis of the scheme" ([42]). That is the
nullity verdict, reached by judges who were not doing speech-act theory. I offer it not as an
argument — courts can be wrong, and legal validity is not normative validity — but as evidence
that reflective practice has already found this structure necessary to mark.

## 6. Standing, and a taxonomy of failures

LD and AC concern the asker's relation to the answer. ST concerns their title to impose the
burden at all, and it reaches cases LD cannot. Searle's preparatory condition on requests
gestures at it: "if I make a request to someone to do something which it is obvious that he is
already doing or is about to do, then my request is pointless and to that extent defective," an
instance of the requirement that "the act must have a point" (Searle 1965, 12). Generalise: the
burden of answering must be one the asker is entitled to impose, given who they are, who the
addressee is, how available the answer is elsewhere, and how often it has been given already.
Standing is defeated by repetition, by ready availability, and by the asker's having no title to
this person's labour in particular. Add a companion condition, **Fair Terms**: the ask must not
be so structured that every available response is costly.

| Pathology | Condition that fails | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sham consultation; rigged hearing; interview whose verdict is fixed | Live Dependence | nullity |
| "We've taken your views on board," and nothing further, ever | Accounting | breach of an obligation owed |
| Sealioning | Standing, Fair Terms | nullity |
| Epistemic exploitation (demand-and-dismiss) | Standing *and* Live Dependence, plus Fair Terms | nullity, overdetermined |
| "I'm just asking whether X" | not an ask at all | assertion in interrogative costume |

Two rows need comment. The fourth is where the framework earns its keep against Berenstain's
rival explanation, because her cases fail several conditions at once, and that is a substantive
claim rather than a hedge. They fail Standing, since the asker has no title to a tenth
explanation of what is readily available; they fail Live Dependence, because the "default
skepticism" she describes (2016, §3) *is* foreclosure of the bearing point, the answer being
discounted whatever it says; and the double bind is a Fair Terms failure, both horns being
costly. The overdetermination explains something practical her functional account does not need
to: why such demands are so hard to refuse cleanly, and why answering one defect at a time
("I've said this before"; "you're not really listening") never settles the matter.

The last row the framework diagnoses more sharply than the literature has. What is objectionable
about "just asking questions" is usually described as insinuation, or as laundering a claim one
will not defend. True but incomplete. Assertion and asking each have a price: the price of
assertion is answerability for the truth of what one says, and the price of asking is
answerability for what one does with the answer. The JAQ-er wants the illocutionary benefit of
assertion — a content on the table, in play, needing rebuttal — while paying neither: not the
first, because he did not assert; not the second, because the bearing point is not his belief but
the audience's impression, to which his interlocutor's judgment is admitted only as raw material.
Call this **bearing-point drift**. Berenstain describes exactly this use of an interlocutor as
material: epistemic products treated as "fodder for skeptical responses" and "harvest[ed] for
content that can be developed into straw arguments and rejected out of hand" (2016, §5). The
manoeuvre is a double evasion, and its two halves are what make it so hard to name in real time.

The conditions also let us say something the harassment literature cannot easily say about
Claeys's courteous, genuinely open questioner. If he satisfies LD and AC, my account does not
convict him of sham; it convicts him under ST, since he is not entitled to this person's tenth
explanation, nor to it in a forum where every answer costs her. That is a better description of
his fault than self-deception: it can be shown to him without telling him he does not know his
own mind.

## 7. Objections

**(i) This is Gricean implicature, not a normative power.** The thought would be that asking
merely *implicates* openness, as asserting implicates belief, and that the implicature is
cancellable. The test is cancellability, and the phenomena fail it distinctively. "What do you
think? — though I should say I'm fairly settled" is fine, but does not cancel LD; being fairly
settled is compatible with being movable. Now cancel LD itself: "What do you think? — though
nothing you say will make any difference to anything I believe or do." This does not yield a
felicitous, implicature-free question. It yields the retort of §1. Compare "I promise to come,
though I have no intention of coming," which is not a cancelled implicature but closer to an
absurdity. Cancelling a genuine implicature makes an utterance more informative; cancelling LD
makes it unintelligible as an ask. That is the signature of a constitutive condition.

**(ii) Over-demandingness.** If every question puts me on the hook, people will ask less, and
asking is epistemically precious. Three replies. The obligation is graded and usually trivial: AC
costs a sentence, and is often waived by default. The counterfactual is wrong — the alternative
to answerable asking is not more genuine asking but more sham asking, which is self-consuming,
since where consultation is known to be ornamental people stop responding. The credit of the
practice is a common resource and non-responsive askers deplete it, the mechanism the *Stanford
Encyclopedia* notes for promising, where "a pattern of abuses of speech act institutions might
deprive a person of an ability to perform speech acts" (Green 2020, §9). And powers-based
accounts have a relief valve: the obligation is owed *to* the addressee and so is theirs to
waive.

**(iii) The examiner, the lawyer, the therapist.** Plenty of legitimate questioners know the
answer and will not be moved by it. The bearing-point apparatus handles these without special
pleading. The examiner's bearing point is the grade, so his question satisfies LD robustly — and
an examiner whose grade was fixed in advance is running a sham, which is the right result.
Cross-examination has the tribunal's finding as its bearing point rather than the questioner's
belief, and the institution is transparent about this, which is why the witness is not wronged
and why we speak of a kangaroo court when the finding is fixed. In each case the ask survives
because the answer lands somewhere. It is the absence of such a place, not the presence of a
settled belief, that voids.

**(iv) Isn't Live Dependence the "good faith" condition already on offer?** Claeys quotes
Sullivan and colleagues: one argues in good faith when "one is willing to change their views
considering some conceivable evidence or argument that one's opponent might offer" (Claeys 2024,
quoting Sullivan et al. 2020). The resemblance is real and I do not want to overstate the
distance. But theirs is a rough gloss on a virtue of debaters, mine a validity condition on a
power, and that is what delivers the nullity verdict and the permission to refuse. Theirs indexes
openness to the asker's *views*; mine to a bearing point that is often not a view at all, which
is why mine handles examiners and consent. And theirs has no counterpart of AC, which is where
most ordinary wrongdoing here lies: bad asking is usually not rigged, merely unaccounted for.

**(v) Broadcast and machine asks.** Broadcast asks generate the obligation jointly rather than
severally, which is why institutions that take them seriously invent joint discharges — the
published response to comments, the editor's report. Querying a database generates no obligation
at all, and the account says why without legislating about machine minds: the obligation tracks
the exposure of an agent who stakes a judgment, and where nobody is exposed nothing is owed.

## 8. The consent ladder

The account looks most doubtful at the low end and most obvious at the high end, so the gradient
is worth making explicit: the high end is common ground, and my claim is that it is continuous
with the rest. Asks form a ladder by how much of the addressee's agency they recruit. At the
bottom are **evidential asks** — "what time is it?" — where the addressee is wanted as a source,
exposure is slight, and the obligation is correspondingly slight, though not zero, which is why
asking the time while knowing it is faintly objectionable rather than merely odd. In the middle
are **advisory asks** — "what do you think we should do?" — where the addressee is recruited as a
*participant*, their judgment solicited as judgment and their standing staked far more heavily.
This is where consultation and peer review live, and where Berenstain's cases sit, which is why
the injury in them is an injury to standing as a knower.

At the top are **permissive asks**: "may I?" Here the answer is not information but the exercise
of the addressee's own normative power, and here everyone already agrees with me: one who asks
permission and proceeds regardless has not merely ignored an answer — the asking was a nullity
and the proceeding a violation. Nobody thinks the wrong there is wasted breath, or discourtesy.

So the top of the ladder is not a special case but the clearest view of a structure present all
the way down. What makes ignoring consent a nullity is that the point of soliciting it was to let
the other's will bear; what makes ignoring advice a weaker wrong of the same family is that the
point of soliciting it was to let the other's judgment bear. The difference is in degree of
recruitment, not in kind, and anyone who accepts the verdict at the top owes an explanation of
why the structure vanishes one rung down. I do not think there is one.

## 9. What follows

*For institutions.* The standard defence of consultation is that it produces better decisions.
That is insufficient even when true, since it leaves the consultee's complaint unexplained — an
ignored consultee whose view was wrong has still been wronged — and it predicts the wrong remedy.
If the point were information, the remedy for bad consultation would be better
information-gathering; if the point is answerability, the remedy is publication of how the
responses bore. That is a testable difference in institutional design, and the one the *Gunning*
fourth criterion already implements.

*For zetetic epistemology.* If ZIP tells the inquirer to take the necessary means to answering her
question, and asking others is the cheapest means, then ZIP unqualified licenses free-riding on
other people's epistemic labour. Friedman's project needs, and does not contain, a constraint on
the recruitment of other inquirers. The norms of asking are a candidate with the right shape:
they do not forbid leaning on others, they price it. Berenstain's work is a demonstration of what
the unpriced version costs and of who pays.

*For the second-personal framework.* Darwall's circle runs from authority to accountability: "the
authority to demand implies, not just a reason for the addressee to comply, but also his being
responsible for doing so," and these notions "comprise an interdefinable circle" (Darwall 2006,
ch. 1, 17, course PDF). As stated, the circle makes the *addressee* accountable. Asking shows
that for at least one power the implication runs the other way too: the authority to summon an
answer is conditional on the summoner's liability to account for it. It is then worth asking —
and I mean the question seriously — which other second-personal powers are held on similar
terms. Demanding an apology, calling someone to account, issuing a reproach: each solicits from
another an exercise of agency that exposes them. Perhaps powers to recruit another's agency are,
as a class, held in trust for the exercise they recruit.
