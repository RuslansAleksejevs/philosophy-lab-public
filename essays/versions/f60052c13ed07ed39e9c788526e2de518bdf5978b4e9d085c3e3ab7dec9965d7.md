# What a Second Look Can Refute

Mira leaves a film bored. Most of its scenes consist of a woman entering a room, arranging a few objects, and leaving again. A friend who knows the director's work asks Mira to watch it once more. Stop waiting for the story to advance, he suggests. Watch how the woman's repeated gestures change as the rooms become emptier. Mira does so. She notices small hesitations she missed before and can explain their place in the film. She remains unmoved. Her friend replies that this shows she still has not learned how to watch it.

Perhaps he is right about the film. Perhaps Mira is still missing something. But those possibilities do not settle whether he is entitled to ask for a third viewing on the same terms. His advice has encountered a difficulty even if the film has not.

I shall defend a distinction between the authority to commend a work and the authority to renew a particular person's investment in it. After a serious attempt at following critical advice, disappointment can count against the proposed route into a work without counting decisively against the work's value. The critic cannot dismiss this local evidence merely by appealing to the very expertise that the route was supposed to help the audience acquire. A renewed demand needs a response to what happened: some evidence of progress, a reason to expect this delay, a relevant change in approach, or another ground for thinking that continuing will help. The work's excellence, repeated as though nothing had been tried, cannot do all this work.

The claim concerns a particular use of criticism. A critic can share enthusiasm, suggest an experiment, or explain why she herself keeps returning. None of these acts must be accompanied by a defense against every possible disappointment. My target is the stronger claim made when she treats another person's refusal to continue as evidence of aesthetic negligence or defective judgment. In that case, she is claiming authority over how that person should direct attention. That authority is more limited than her authority to judge the work.

There is good reason to take such authority seriously. First responses to art can be confused. We can listen for the wrong things, demand effects a work never seeks, or overlook the relation between its parts. Hume gives practice, comparison and repeated consideration central roles in developing taste. His account of a second reading also allows it to expose defects obscured by an initially pleasing surface. Increased attention need not produce increased praise. These points support instruction without making conversion its only possible success. [Hume, “Of the Standard of Taste,” §§18–23.](https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/st)

Nor is the distinction between guiding attention and imposing a verdict new. Parker gives aesthetic authority a role in encouraging attentive examination while opposing its replacement of sincere appreciation. He also emphasizes that understanding an art can require learning what to seek in it. More recently, Flisi argues that experts should offer advice that develops recipients' own aesthetic understanding, rather than exercise authority over the judgments they eventually make. [Parker, *The Principles of Aesthetics*, chapter VII;](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6366/pg6366.html) [Flisi, *Aethics*, p.24.](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/bitstreams/b320d692-16ff-4078-9da0-1c57bc3ae338/download)

Yet endorsing independent judgment at the end leaves a problem in the middle. How can someone who is still learning contest an expert's demand for more learning? If the answer is that she must first acquire the expert's understanding, independence can become a destination whose gate is permanently controlled by the guide. We need to distinguish the competence required to assess a work from the competence required to report what following a particular piece of advice has accomplished.

Consider three claims Mira's friend might make. The film has considerable artistic value. Mira could, under suitable circumstances, come to appreciate that value. Watching it again with attention to the gestures is a promising next step for her. These claims concern different things. The first concerns the film; the second concerns Mira's possibilities; the third concerns a proposed encounter. Even if the first two are true, the third can be false. An excellent work may need a different introduction. A capable learner may be tired of an unhelpful exercise. An insightful critic may be a poor guide for this person.

This is not just the familiar point that experts sometimes teach badly. It changes the evidential role of the learner's response. If every report of boredom is treated as a verdict on the film, the expert can reasonably answer that the learner is not yet qualified to deliver it. If the report bears on a proposed encounter, however, that answer can miss its object. Mira need not know everything the film can offer in order to know that tracking the gestures has not brought the kind of engagement her friend anticipated. Her report is evidence about an event in which she participated, not a rival claim to comprehensive expertise.

The distinction also prevents a second mistake. A failed attempt does not establish that Mira cannot appreciate the film. The route may have failed, or it may need longer. We should resist the movement from unsuccessful advice to a diagnosis of the person just as firmly as the movement from an unsuccessful encounter to a condemnation of the artwork. What has been tested is smaller than either the work or the learner's eventual capacity.

What, then, entitles a critic to renew the demand? Begin with a simple consideration about advice. A recommendation based on a person's present relation to an activity must remain responsive to evidence about that relation. Otherwise its apparent personalization contributes nothing. The adviser is merely recommending the activity to anyone who has not yet accepted it. That can still express a defensible general judgment, but it cannot by itself explain why this person, after this attempt, should undertake this next step.

Aesthetic advice adds a complication. The learner's present sense of what counts as progress may itself need education. Mira initially thinks that a film becomes interesting when more happens. The critic may rightly ask her to suspend that expectation. If every stage had to satisfy it, his advice could never teach her anything. Responsiveness therefore cannot mean that advice survives only while it produces rewards recognizable under the audience's existing preferences.

But suspending a preference is different from suspending the evidential force of everything the learner can report. Mira may be mistaken about what makes a film interesting while accurately reporting that she can now distinguish gestures she previously conflated. She may accurately report that they have begun to seem anxious rather than merely slow. She may also report that the suggested way of attending makes the film feel increasingly predictable. These are possible developments within the encounter. Their significance can be discussed without requiring her to know the correct final verdict.

The critic's task is to show why some feature of this developing relation supports another attempt. He might point out that a gesture Mira calls predictable changes function in the final scene. He might recognize her new sense of anxiety as an opening worth following. He might explain that tracking individual gestures was only preparation for seeing a pattern across several viewings, and give reasons for expecting the preparation to matter. Or he might conclude that concentrating on the gestures has made her watch too mechanically and suggest an unstructured viewing instead.

Any of these responses could be mistaken. They nevertheless keep the recommendation answerable to the encounter. By contrast, “You would appreciate it if you were attending properly” does not answer Mira when attending properly is identified solely by whether she appreciates it. It restates the desired endpoint. It gives no basis for judging whether another performance of the recommended activity will take her closer to it.

The relevant requirement is consequently not a guarantee of success. It is a requirement against explaining every failure of advice through the absence of the success it promised. Once someone has made a serious attempt at a proposed approach, an expert who criticizes her refusal to continue must address the attempt. If the only response is that competent viewers value the work, the expert has supplied evidence for the wrong claim. She has defended the work's standing while leaving the renewed recommendation unsupported.

This does not entail that every disappointing experience must lower one's estimate of a route. An experience might be exactly what a well-supported recommendation led one to expect. Learning to hear a complex passage can involve several unrewarding attempts. What matters is that an explanation of delay have some basis beyond its usefulness in protecting the recommendation. Previous experience helping comparable listeners, features of the work that can only become apparent later, and demonstrated changes in the learner's perception can all supply such a basis. A prediction can also be revised after an unexpected result. The correction is legitimate if it explains something about this encounter; surprise does not automatically discredit an adviser.

There is a further distinction between changing one's confidence and changing what one may demand. A critic may remain highly confident that a route will eventually work while becoming less entitled to fault the learner for declining it. The expected benefit may be uncertain in duration and costly to pursue. More importantly, the critic may be unable to give the learner access to the grounds of that confidence. Initially, a trusted judgment can reasonably support an experiment. After sustained unsuccessful cooperation, invoking that same judgment can still explain the critic's hope without settling whether the learner is unreasonable to stop. Trust makes exploration possible; it does not make every renewed request compulsory.

One might object that this leaves the novice in charge of deciding whether she has made a serious attempt. She can claim to have done everything asked while retaining the very habits the critic wanted her to abandon. An inattentive viewer can notice a recurring object without seeing its dramatic role. An exercise designed to transform perception may be superficially completed while its point is missed.

This is a real limitation, but it supports examination of the attempt rather than immunity from it. The critic can ask Mira to describe what changed, compare two scenes, or explain why she now finds a sequence predictable. Her answers may reveal that she was watching messages on her phone, failed to recognize the repeated setting, or continued to regard every pause as dead time between plot events. Any of these could support a diagnosis more specific than deficient taste. It could also show why another attempt would differ from the last.

Conversely, Mira might offer a detailed, sensitive description that preserves her negative response. Her friend should allow this as a possible result of the inquiry. If no description could count as competent except one ending in admiration, his procedure could not distinguish increased perception from increased willingness to agree. He would have built the verdict into the test of whether the learner had followed his instructions.

There need be no public checklist that settles every dispute about adequate attention. A learner can be mistaken about her own experience; a critic can notice avoidance that she misses. The point is comparative. We often possess more shared evidence about what someone tried and learned than about what the whole work is worth. The absence of an infallible test does not erase that difference. Where the adequacy of the attempt remains unsettled, the critic may suspend judgment. Uncertainty does not entitle him to assign all remaining error to Mira.

The strongest challenge comes from art whose rewards arrive late, or whose value cannot be understood through the audience's present outlook. A listener may need to learn an unfamiliar musical tradition before a piece becomes intelligible. The proposed education may take months. Judging it after a few disappointing sessions can seem like pulling up a plant to check whether its roots have grown. Does demanding a response to failure make transformative attention impossible?

Callard's account of aspiration explains why learning to value something need not begin with an adequate grasp of its value. She also describes reliance on a mentor as directed toward eventual independence, and explicitly allows rational criticism of an aspiration after a period of practice, including advice to give it up. Her discussion invokes expertise, familiarity with the aspirant and evidence from trials or similar attempts. The possibility of justified perseverance and justified abandonment is therefore already part of this account. [Callard, “Proleptic Reasons,” pp.129–131, 137–138, 151–153.](https://humanities-web.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/philosophy/prod/2019-06/Callard%20Proleptic%20Reasons%20Final%20Published%20Version%20OSME.pdf)

My proposal locates a further distinction within such guidance. Evidence may warrant giving up a particular route without warranting giving up the aspiration. A learner can withdraw from an instructor's preferred exercise while retaining an aspiration to appreciate the music. The instructor cannot answer this narrower withdrawal simply by saying that the learner does not yet know what musical understanding is worth. That may explain the need for education. It does not identify the education she should continue.

Nor must a lengthy route deliver small installments of its eventual reward. There can be good grounds for undertaking a stretch of work whose benefit cannot be assessed from inside each session. A novice can reasonably commit to a sequence and defer the question of whether it was useful until the sequence ends. During it, the commitment and the reasons for its scale can support persistence. Nothing in the account requires constant self-monitoring or a pleasure report after every ten minutes.

What it excludes is an indefinitely expandable preparatory period justified only by the fact that appreciation has not yet arrived. If three viewings become ten, and ten become a year's study, the recommendation has changed in scope. It now asks for a different investment. That change may be warranted, but the original invitation cannot silently authorize it. A critic who discovers that his advice was too slight has learned something about the route. He should be able to acknowledge that discovery without concluding that the learner has failed.

Sometimes a critic cannot explain in advance what will change, cannot find an intermediate sign of progress, and has no relevant experience with learners of this sort. She still has her own powerful experience of the work. It would be a loss if she could never say, “Please try.” She can. A work can warrant an exploratory invitation even when nobody knows a reliable path into it. The uncertainty affects what follows a refusal. The critic's inability to guide does not diminish the work, but it limits her standing to describe another person's unwillingness to keep experimenting as an aesthetic defect.

This limitation matters especially when an artwork promises to change the standards by which it will be judged. An advocate may say that dislike belongs to the audience's old outlook, so only the transformed audience can assess the enterprise. There is something right in this. We should not require new values to pass every test supplied by the old ones. Yet the possibility of transformation cannot identify which present practices deserve trust. Rival teachers and rival works can promise incompatible transformations. The mere fact that a future admirer would endorse the path cannot distinguish them: each advocate can describe a future admirer of her own.

An invitation becomes more credible when it supports capacities whose exercise does not guarantee allegiance to its source. Mira's improved attention to hesitation could enable her to admire the film. It could also enable her to explain why its repetitions feel contrived. The newly acquired capacity is useful evidence of learning partly because these possibilities remain open. If the friend recognizes the capacity only when it produces praise, he loses evidence that his guidance is educating Mira at all.

This is not an argument that every negative judgment is a sign of independence or progress. Contrarianism can be as empty as conformity. The relevant development is a richer, more answerable relation to the work: the learner can distinguish effects, offer comparisons, locate her response, and sometimes correct her own account. Such developments can support continuing even when enjoyment declines. They can also make an informed refusal possible before comprehensive appreciation has been achieved. Independence need not appear all at once at the end of education. It can first appear as a justified objection to the way one is being taught.

The principle defended here has relatives outside aesthetics. Advice about learning a language or entering a profession should also respond to unsuccessful attempts. I do not claim that criticism obeys a wholly separate logic. Its distinctive difficulty is that an adviser can appeal both to superior judgment and to the need to transform the learner's judgment, making disagreement appear to undermine the very competence required to disagree. Separating the work, the learner and the proposed encounter prevents this maneuver from swallowing all negative evidence.

The contribution is correspondingly narrower than a theory of artistic value or a general defense of aesthetic autonomy. Neither sincere response nor established expertise alone settles the choice to persevere. We need to identify the claim that an encounter has tested. An unsuccessful second look can reveal that a particular way of directing attention has not earned its renewal, even when the artwork remains admirable and the learner's possibilities remain open. This supplies a reason to revise advice, not merely a courteous reason to tolerate resistance.

Return to Mira and her friend. He may be able to show her something new. She may agree to watch again. She may decide that the new proposal is promising but leave it for another year. Or their conversation may end with him saying that he still admires the film and no longer knows how to help her appreciate it. That last outcome need not be a defeat for criticism. It distinguishes the critic's discovery from the limits of his invitation, and leaves the next encounter open to be something other than another examination the audience can pass only by agreeing.
