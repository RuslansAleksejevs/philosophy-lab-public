# Revocable Consent and the Creation of Duties

Rowan lends Ellis a spare pump to supply a small settlement with water. The arrangement is explicitly revocable on a day's notice; Rowan makes no promise to keep lending. Ellis already owns a reserve pump. Keeping it available would involve a modest expense. Instead, Ellis sells it to fund another useful but nonurgent project. A replacement could now take two weeks to obtain. If Rowan asks for the pump back, the residents will face a serious shortage unless Rowan continues the loan.

Ellis has neither deceived Rowan into the original agreement nor decided to ignore a future refusal. Ellis would acknowledge that a refusal ends the original permission. Yet Ellis has made it much harder for Rowan to end the use permissibly. Assume that extending the loan briefly would impose little burden on Rowan, while withdrawing the pump would gravely harm innocent residents. Those facts may give Rowan a duty to allow temporary continued use. Ellis can therefore undermine Rowan's control without making Rowan's revocation invalid.

My claim is that a recipient of revocable permission can owe its giver a duty to preserve a morally available exit. More precisely, a recipient normally must not, without further authorization or adequate justification, use its discretionary control over arrangements to make ending the permitted activity subject to a substantial new moral burden when a reasonable precaution would preserve the giver's control. This is a duty about how dependence is organized before withdrawal, not a claim that dependence always invalidates consent or that its victims may ignore the needs it creates.

The contribution is a limited connection between three familiar ideas: the revocability of consent, the possibility of morally binding predicaments produced by wrongdoing, and the fair distribution of exit costs. The connection matters because it identifies a wrong that may occur before anyone refuses, without a plan to manipulate anyone, and even when the resulting duty to continue is genuine.

## 1. What revocation does, and what it does not do

Three questions should be separated. Can Rowan end the permission originally given? May Rowan permissibly make Ellis stop using the pump? Must Ellis, all things considered, stop? These questions can have different answers. A valid revocation changes the normative situation. It does not, just by itself, settle every obligation of either party.

Healey distinguishes consent's effect on a directed duty from an action's overall permissibility. He also notes that several independent duties can be owed to the same person, so removing one need not remove the others (2019, pp. 499–504). Applied across time, this distinction allows us to say that a permission has ended while independently grounded considerations still justify, or require, a closely similar course of conduct. If an emergency justifies continued use of Rowan's property, that justification need not be the original consent.

Dougherty likewise distinguishes the priority of present dissent over past consent from the priority of consent over other moral reasons. He allows, for example, that escaping grave danger can justify crossing land despite its owner's dissent. He also explains how a person can supplement revocable consent with a promise not to revoke: revocation can remain effective even though it breaks the promise (2014, pp. 27, 33–34, 38–39).

It therefore would be a mistake to infer a liberty to withdraw support from the bare existence of a power to revoke. A person can possess a power she ought not exercise. Nor does her possession of that power imply a guarantee that its exercise will achieve the practical result she wants. A consent giver is not sovereign over the needs of third parties.

This distinction sharpens the pump case. Rowan can withdraw the standing permission while acknowledging an emergency duty to provide a short extension. Or, depending on the correct account of emergency use, Rowan's revocation may stand while Ellis has a separate justification for temporarily retaining the pump. I need not settle which analysis is correct. The problem is that Rowan cannot now obtain cessation on the terms that otherwise governed the arrangement. A morally available exit means an opportunity to bring the use to an end without thereby violating a substantial independent duty. It does not mean a magical power to erase other people's interests.

Recognizing the loss of that opportunity does not yet explain why Ellis did anything wrong. People routinely and permissibly change what others ought to do. Inviting someone to dinner, beginning a friendship, or needing urgent help can create new moral demands. The question is why some ways of creating dependence wrong the person who must bear the resulting demand.

## 2. Beyond a manipulative intention

Keller's account of moral blackmail shows why the later duty cannot simply be dismissed. Someone may wrong another by arranging circumstances in which the latter becomes morally required to act as the first person wishes. Keller's examples include manipulating the obligations of professionals to vulnerable beneficiaries. The injustice of producing the predicament does not make the resulting obligation unreal (2018, sections “Moral Blackmail” and “Blackmailing a Fiduciary”).

A malicious version of Ellis fits this pattern. Ellis sells the reserve precisely to ensure that Rowan will feel unable to reclaim the loaned pump. But consider a different Ellis, who expects Rowan to remain generous and thinks only of putting an idle resource to productive use. Ellis understands that the sale removes the backup but does not aim to constrain Rowan. The need for Rowan's continued support is a foreseeable effect, not the purpose of the decision.

The second Ellis still owes Rowan an explanation. It is inadequate to say that every actual request for return will receive a respectful answer. Ellis has removed the arrangement that would make a respectful answer more than an acknowledgment of a duty whose fulfillment would now injure others. Nor can Ellis reply that the residents are better off because the proceeds bought something useful. The disputed choice was between preserving the reserve and redirecting its value. Continuing to help the residents did not require discarding the means of honoring Rowan's exit.

Compare a third case. A storm destroys the reserve despite reasonable precautions. Rowan may face exactly the same immediate reason to extend the loan. Yet Ellis need not have wronged Rowan. What Rowan owes once the emergency exists does not determine whether Ellis was entitled to create the conditions for that obligation. We need an assessment of the earlier decision that is sensitive to control, foreseeability, and alternatives.

Liberto supplies another relevant distinction. Securing promissory release can exploit a promisee's vulnerability even when the release is voluntary and effective. On an authority account, the wrong need not be promise-breaking; it can survive the successful exercise of a normative power (2016, pp. 149–150, 160–163). My concern is different: no release or renewal need be solicited or secured. A recipient can already have mistreated a consent giver by exposing her to an avoidable future obligation to continue.

Thus neither successful revocation nor the absence of manipulative intent is enough to vindicate Ellis's conduct. But neither is the bare creation of new obligations enough to condemn it. The explanation must identify what makes this particular change in Rowan's position objectionable.

## 3. Permission to use is not permission to reorganize control

Start with what Rowan grants. Rowan permits a use of the pump while retaining the decision whether the permission continues. Rowan does not thereby promise a particular period of support. These are different arrangements, and Ellis knows which arrangement has been accepted.

The difference would lose much of its significance if Ellis were generally entitled to eliminate affordable alternatives and then transfer the resulting burden of continuity to Rowan. The recipient could obtain something resembling a commitment by deciding how much innocent third parties would lose if the giver withdrew. Rowan's refusal would remain normatively effective, but the recipient's unilateral organizational choice would determine how costly it was, morally, for Rowan to secure cessation.

This is not a logical contradiction. Revocable consent could coexist with such a rule. It is a reason to reject the rule as an adequate way of respecting the control retained in this transaction. Retaining control over a continuing use is valuable partly because a giver can revise her plans without having already committed to the recipient's plans. Making that control conditional on discharging substantial new rescue burdens changes the practical allocation of responsibility. The recipient should not get to make that change merely because it has been given permission to proceed for now.

The point can be tested by comparing two proposals Ellis might have made initially:

“You may end the loan on a day's notice. I will maintain an available substitute.”

“You may end the loan on a day's notice. But I may dispose of the substitute, leaving people dependent on your generosity while I obtain another.”

A reasonable giver can distinguish these proposals without misunderstanding consent. Agreeing to the first does not authorize the second. The two can involve the same current use of the same property for the same beneficiaries; they differ in the recipient's authority to place the future costs of stopping on the giver. The original case does not assume that Ellis expressly promised to maintain a reserve. The comparison reveals a dimension of permission that a description of the permitted activity alone can miss.

Why should the default favor Rowan rather than Ellis? Because Ellis seeks a departure from the allocation already established: Ellis may use another person's resource, but has not obtained a commitment to its continued provision. Where a modest precaution preserves that allocation, its cost belongs presumptively to the undertaking whose organizer controls it. Charging the cost to Rowan through a new duty to rescue reverses this allocation without securing Rowan's agreement. Rowan's generosity gives Ellis an opportunity to help; it does not give Ellis general authority to decide how much further generosity will become obligatory.

This explanation also identifies a complaint distinct from the residents' possible complaint. Suppose the residents will unquestionably be protected whichever plan Ellis chooses. Rowan is certain to provide the pump until a replacement arrives, and this will be enough to prevent any shortage. There need be no increased risk of injury to the residents. Still, Ellis chooses who bears the cost of keeping them safe. The reserve plan places that cost on the project. The sale plan makes safety depend on Rowan's temporarily surrendering the practical benefit of a retained exit. A concern only with whether the residents receive water misses that difference.

The relevant wrong is therefore not exhausted by endangering third parties. Nor must it involve making Rowan worse off overall. Rowan might welcome the other project funded by the sale. She can nevertheless object that Ellis treated her continuing support as a resource over which Ellis, rather than she, could decide.

## 4. A limited duty of precaution

The argument supports a qualified principle:

When a recipient knows that permission leaves its giver control over whether a continuing use persists, the recipient owes the giver reasonable precautions against the recipient's discretionary arrangements making cessation subject to a substantial new moral burden, unless that exposure was adequately authorized or has an independent justification.

Four limits do real work here.

First, the control must actually have been retained. A gift, a fixed-term commitment, a promise not to revoke, or an agreement with a reasonable notice period can allocate responsibility differently. Whether there is retained control depends on what was communicated, the kind of transaction, and legitimate shared expectations. The principle does not transform every permission into a freely terminable loan.

Second, it concerns a substantial increase relative to the dependence already inherent in, or fairly understood as part of, the authorized arrangement. Loaning someone a pump for a week may predictably require letting them finish a safe shutdown. Agreeing to host a dinner does not entitle one to eject guests into a blizzard. A giver cannot count every ordinary inconvenience or reasonably anticipated transition as a new infringement of control. The target is the recipient's discretionary addition to those burdens, such as abandoning an available substitute.

Third, the precaution must be reasonable. The receiver need not duplicate an entire public service to protect an owner's freedom to change plans. Keeping an existing affordable reserve is different from building a second system at ruinous expense. The strength of the duty depends on the seriousness and likelihood of the new burden, the importance of the retained control, the cost of prevention, and who can most readily provide it. This is a standard of reasonable care for a particular person's normative position, not an instruction to minimize all dependence.

Fourth, an independent justification can defeat the presumption. Suppose selling the reserve is the only way to prevent an imminent catastrophe, and Rowan would face only a slight temporary inconvenience. There may be sufficient reason to sell. But the justification is the exceptional need, not the fact that Rowan once consented to the ordinary use. The distinction matters because an exceptional justification cannot automatically become a routine planning assumption.

Adequate authorization also matters. Rowan might knowingly agree that the reserve should be sold and accept the resulting exposure to further demands. In this property case, there is no general reason to deny that possibility. The argument does not establish that every bodily or personal control interest can be surrendered on the same terms. Nor does mere notification establish authorization. “We have decided to make people dependent on your continued support” is information about a decision, not necessarily permission to make it. If Ellis requires a commitment, Ellis should seek one while Rowan can still reject the proposal without confronting the very predicament under discussion.

A reasonable precaution need not involve constant renewed consent. It can consist in preserving a substitute, retaining funds for a transition, setting a bounded notice period before dependence develops, or limiting how far the activity expands. What matters is whether an arrangement preserves the control in question, not whether its recipient repeatedly obtains the word “yes.”

## 5. Reliance and the price of cooperation

The strongest objection is that permission invites reliance. A loan that licenses no planning would often be useless. Goodin's account of revocation, as summarized in his chapter abstract, treats reasonable detrimental reliance as a reason against withdrawal and in favor of compensation (2024). Why should Rowan enjoy the benefits of cooperation while Ellis bears the cost of ensuring that Rowan can always leave?

The principle does not say that Rowan should always leave without paying. It asks which reliance Rowan authorized, and which additional dependence Ellis may impose. A borrowing arrangement can fairly include notice, reimbursement, or a fixed term. If Ellis reasonably relied on an assurance by Rowan, that fact can change the moral situation. The pump example excludes such an assurance. It is not a case in which Rowan encouraged Ellis to discard the reserve and then opportunistically disavowed responsibility.

Moreover, the category of reasonable reliance cannot be fixed independently of the parties' retained powers. Confidence that a generous person will probably keep helping is not automatically an entitlement to make continuation obligatory for her. A well-founded prediction concerns what Rowan is likely to do. A justified reliance claim concerns who should bear the costs if Rowan acts otherwise. The former does not establish the latter.

This leaves room for unexpected costs arising from legitimate reliance. A recipient who takes the agreed precautions may still suffer when consent is withdrawn. The giver may owe warning or compensation. Conversely, a recipient may owe preservation of an exit even though its own loss would be substantial: having freely chosen a fragile arrangement does not automatically transfer its downside to someone who never undertook that risk. The duties can run in both directions.

Rouméas argues that adequate exit opportunities and a fair allocation of exit costs are distinct concerns; not all costs should simply be removed from the departing party (2023, sections “Exit ought to be fair” and “The fairness of exit: two desiderata”). The present argument specifies one consideration relevant to that allocation: the recipient's discretionary control over a foreseeable increase in the giver's moral burdens. It does not provide a complete theory of fair exit. Some costs are fair even though large; some modest costs are unfairly shifted.

Another objection is that the argument treats cooperation as a threat to independence. Friendship, caregiving, and shared projects make people dependent on each other. Their value cannot be preserved while guaranteeing that nobody acquires unchosen duties.

That objection defeats a universal duty to keep every relationship easily terminable. It does not defeat the limited principle. A long friendship is not merely a standing permission to use an object; its duties may arise without a promise and without a retained power to dissolve them at will. Even within friendship, however, one can wrong a friend by unilaterally arranging for her limited permission to become the indispensable basis of a much larger undertaking. The proper question concerns the particular permission and responsibility, not whether the parties are generally independent.

## 6. What repair requires

Suppose Ellis has sold the reserve and Rowan now wants the pump back. Two responses are tempting. One is to deny that Rowan has a rescue duty because Ellis wrongfully created the need. The other is to conclude that the need vindicates Ellis's decision. Both collapse the distinction between what should have happened and what must happen now.

The innocent residents did not lose their claim to protection when Ellis acted badly. Under the assumptions of the case, Rowan should temporarily allow continued use. But that does not discharge Ellis's responsibility to Rowan. Ellis must seek a substitute, bear reasonable transition costs, and restore Rowan's opportunity to end the use. Simply asking Rowan to affirm consent again leaves the source of the pressure intact. A fresh answer given under the avoidable burden does not by itself repair the decision that produced it.

Nor should Ellis tell Rowan that consenting anew proves that the arrangement is fair. That treats compliance with a moral demand as retrospective approval of its imposition. Rowan can coherently say: “I should help these people now, and you should not have put me in this position.” Preserving that complaint is necessary to distinguish emergency cooperation from endorsement.

Repair need not wait for a refusal. Suppose Rowan still sincerely wants the loan to continue and has no present intention of leaving. Ellis's decision can still be objectionable because it has changed the conditions under which Rowan could revise that intention. The importance of a retained decision is not exhausted by how often its holder exercises it. A recipient who quietly makes cessation much harder can owe restoration even when the holder is currently pleased with the activity.

This does not require costly precautions against every remote future possibility. The earlier reasonableness limits remain. It means that the relevant object of protection is an option over time, not merely a present preference for cessation. The duty is therefore prospective in its grounds and remedial in its aftermath.

Revocable consent leaves room for an important failure of respect. A recipient can accept that permission ends whenever its giver says so, yet organize dependence so that obtaining cessation would violate another duty. The appropriate response is neither to make consent sovereign over all moral reasons nor to pretend that every later burden was authorized by the original grant. It is to require recipients, within reasonable limits, to preserve the control they were given permission to act under.
