# Asking Makes You Answerable: The Question as a Normative Power

**Abstract.** There is now a small literature on the norms of asking, and every norm in it is a
condition on how things stand *before* the answer arrives: on what the asker may already believe
or know, on what the addressee must know, on whether the question intrudes. Nothing in it governs
what the asker owes the answerer *afterwards*. I argue that asking is a normative power with a
downstream face. It authorises the addressee to speak; it burdens them with a need to respond; and
it makes the asker answerable to them for what becomes of the answer. The argument turns on what
answering is: not a commodity handed over but a judgment staked, which can be received only
through its content and on its author's authority. An asker who has foreclosed that reception has
proposed a joint undertaking he is not party to, and the burden he purported to impose does not
arise — while the authorisation he issued does. The account yields a principled permission to
refuse that requires no allegation of malice, a procedural right to demand an asker's bearing
point, and an obligation of accounting that has no counterpart in the existing literature.

---

## 1. Then why are you asking?

Three complaints, none of which is a complaint about being lied to.

**Consultation.** A council publishes a draft scheme and invites residents to comment. The scheme
has in fact been fixed; the comments will be read, counted and filed. Suppose, further, that
everybody involved knows this. Residents comment anyway, or bitterly decline. Nobody has been
deceived. Nobody has been coerced. And yet the exercise is a paradigm of public bad faith.

**Advice.** You ask what I think you should do about your marriage. I give you an hour and tell you
something hard. You had already decided; you say so afterwards, cheerfully, as though this were of
no consequence. I feel used. I cannot easily say I was harmed — the hour was freely given, and I
might even have enjoyed it — and I cannot say you were insincere, since you did want to hear me.

**Interrogation.** A courteous interlocutor asks me, in public, for evidence of a claim I have
made. I supply it. He asks again, slightly differently. This continues. He is unfailingly polite,
and he has misrepresented nothing. But whatever I produce will be met with another question, and
the eventual verdict — his and the audience's — was settled before the first one.

In each case the natural retort is the same, and it is a curiously *constitutive*-sounding retort
rather than a moral one: **then why are you asking?** Not "you shouldn't have asked," which would
concede that an asking occurred; but a demand to be shown that the act was one of its kind at all.

This essay takes that retort seriously. I shall argue that asking a question of another person is
the exercise of a normative power whose burden-imposing side is conditional on the asker's own
liability to account. Asking makes you answerable. Where the asker is not in a position to be
answerable, the addressee owes no answer — and, what matters more, owes no excuse for not
answering.

## 2. Where the norms of asking now stand

The claim that this territory is untouched would be false, and it is worth saying precisely what
is and is not there, because the shape of the gap is the shape of my contribution.

Three norms on asking are in circulation, collected by Habgood-Coote as *Belief* ("one must:
inquire into Q, only if one doesn't believe a complete answer to Q"; Friedman 2017), *Ignorance*
(the same with "know"; Whitcomb 2017) and *Knowledge* ("only if one knows that the presuppositions
of Q hold"; Willard-Kyle 2023) (Habgood-Coote 2026, §5). His own proposals — *Common Belief*,
*Shared Ignorance*, *Distributed Knowledge* — replace the individual epistemic states with
collective ones (§5). Haziza adds a condition indexed to the person asked: "inquirers should not
ask addressees who fall short of a certain epistemic status," namely knowledge — "one may ask
addressee A the question Q only if A knows Q" (Haziza 2023, abstract and an unnumbered footnote in
the saved landing page).<sup>[1]</sup>
Millson and Risjord are reported to defend a commitment account on which asking "either commits
the speaker to having an interrogative attitude to a question, or commits the hearer to answering
that question" (Habgood-Coote 2026, n. 2). Berstler argues, in a paper I have been able to read
only in abstract, that an intrusive question can violate privacy rights because "when you ask me a
question, you sometimes undermine my effective control over whether to reveal the answer to you"
(Berstler 2023, abstract). And there is a recent ethics of defective questioning: Whitcomb on
bullshit questions, Robinson, Alfano and Astola on the same, Claeys on sealioning, Berenstain on
the extraction of epistemic labour from the marginalised.

Sort these and a pattern appears. They are conditions on the asker's prior epistemic state; or on
the addressee's; or on the intrusiveness of the asking; or on the asker's attitude at the moment of
asking. Every one of them is satisfied or violated by the time the question leaves the asker's
mouth. Not one of them says anything about what the asker owes the answerer once the answer is in.
Habgood-Coote comes closest, and from an unexpected direction: discussing directives, he observes
that "second-person directives generate a commitment to non-interference," so that a speaker who
says "pass the salt!" and then knocks the salt from the addressee's hand "are open to legitimate
criticism from that addressee that goes beyond general norms against impoliteness"; they "might
even be generating a commitment to help" (2026, §4). That is a speaker-side commitment of exactly
the family I want. But he states it for directives and does not transpose it to asking, and the
commitments he names are discharged while the answering is going on, not after. He also marks my
question as open, in a footnote: "there's an interesting question how we ought to make sense of
question-asking in non-cooperative contexts in which neither speaker or hearer wants to find out
the answer to the question asked" (2026, n. 31).

So the narrow claim, which is the one I need: there is no downstream obligation in the literature —
nothing owed by asker to answerer concerning what becomes of the answer after it is given. That is
the gap, and the essay's least anticipated condition, *Accounting*, sits squarely in it.

The other near neighbour is zetetic. Friedman's instrumental principle says that "if one wants to
figure out Q?, then one ought to take the necessary means to figuring out Q?" (Friedman 2020, §1;
author's PDF, 2). Asking somebody is usually the cheapest means. What the inquirer owes the person
they lean on is a question that formulation does not reach.

## 3. Five diagnoses that get part of it

**(a) Insincerity.** The asker did not really want to know. But in all three cases of §1 the asker
may sincerely want to hear the answer. The council is curious about what residents think; the
friend wanted my view; the interlocutor may be genuinely interested in my evidence, in the way a
collector is interested in specimens. Wanting to hear is not wanting to be moved. Sincerity
conditions attach to the psychological state an act expresses; the trouble here lies in what the
asker will *do*.

**(b) Deception.** The asker implied openness and lacked it. Often true, but not the wrong, because
the wrong survives full disclosure. Stipulate that the council prints "our decision is final";
that my friend says in advance, "I've decided, but tell me anyway"; that my interlocutor announces
that nothing I say will change his mind. Deception is now impossible, and the exercise is worse
rather than better. A wrong that is aggravated by being announced is not a species of deception.

**(c) Exploitation.** This is the strongest rival and I want to give it its due. Berenstain's
"epistemic exploitation occurs when privileged persons compel marginalized persons to produce an
education or explanation about the nature of the oppression they face," a practice "marked by
unrecognized, uncompensated, emotionally taxing, coerced epistemic labor" (2016, §2). She has the
demand-and-dismissal pattern squarely: "though the privileged demand the epistemic labor of the
marginalized, they often perpetuate epistemic oppression by dismissing the knowledge produced"
(§2); such demands "may appear to be invitations to participate in knowledge production," but "the
ideas and claims marginalized people put forward are readily dismissed and are not taken to be
genuine contributions to knowledge" (§5). She even lists "just asking a question" among the
euphemisms the practice travels under (§2). And she asks my question almost verbatim: "how can we
make sense of the fact that the privileged demand labor from the marginalized and then fail to make
use of the fruits of that labor?" (§5).

Her answer is functional: the pattern persists because of "the role that epistemic exploitation
plays in upholding the active ignorance of the dominantly situated" (§5). That explanation is the
right one for her cases and I do not compete with it. But it does not reach the council, the
friend, or the examiner, where the same pattern recurs with no domination in play; and cost cannot
be the whole story, because the complaint survives its absence. "Do you mind if I bring my dog?" —
"Not at all." If I had already loaded the dog into the car, I have wronged you, and not by costing
you a syllable. The complaint tracks *uptake*, not *expenditure*. So: Berenstain has the
phenomenon, and has it first; what I offer is a different kind of explanation of it, internal to
the act rather than to the system the act serves, and it is the internal explanation that yields
the remedies in §6.

**(d) Harassment and bad faith.** Claeys, adopting Johnson's characterisation of sealioning as a
fusion of "persistent questioning ... with a loudly-insisted-upon commitment to reasonable debate"
(Johnson 2017, 13, quoted in Claeys 2024), argues that it serves "the political goal of forestalling
action on the one hand, and the epistemic goal of undermining the victim's authority on the other,"
and extends the analysis via Sartre to the good-willed questioner who is "culpable ... because of
his culpable ignorance" (Claeys 2024, "Bad faith and performative goodness"). Persuasive for its
cases. But the council is not harassing anybody and my friend is not in bad faith about herself;
she is careless about what she has done to me. We need a wrong available to the courteous and the
self-transparent.

**(e) Bullshit questioning.** Robinson, Alfano and Astola define a bullshit question as an act
which is syntactically a question but where "the speaker does not care about the truth of the
answer," "intends to perform a non-interrogative illocutionary act by means of the utterance,"
and "intends to obscure the nature or existence of that intended non-interrogative illocutionary
act" (2025, §3.2). Their cases are largely mine — JAQing off, sealioning, the rigged courtroom
question, the exam — and their wrongness diagnosis is a near relative of mine: such a questioner
"use[s] the interlocutor as a mere prop," and so disrespects them "as an epistemic agent, i.e.,
someone capable of joining the project of inquiry ... offering testimony that is likely to receive
uptake" (§4).

I have to say what is different, and the definition itself says it. Concealment is a conjunct.
Strip it away and the account no longer applies: the council that prints "our decision is final"
is not obscuring anything, and neither is the friend who says "I've decided, but tell me anyway."
Those are my flagship cases and they fall outside the analysis. Nor is their account directed: the
wrong is a vice of the asker and a form of disrespect, assessed in the round, with no obligation
owed *to* the answerer that the answerer could waive, demand or complain of in her own name. That
matters practically, because what the sealioned target needs is not a verdict about her
interlocutor's character but a standing she can invoke.

What all five diagnoses share is that they treat the asking as normatively inert and look for the
wrong *around* it — in the state that precedes it, the impression it creates, the costs it imposes,
the hostility or insouciance it conceals. My proposal is that the asking is not inert.

## 4. Asking as a normative power

A normative power is a capacity, exercised intentionally and recognised as such, to change the
normative situation by exercising it. Promising is the standard case: it is "a special sort of
power we have over our normative circumstances, the power to invoke obligations by promissory
utterance," distinguished by the fact that "promisors obligate themselves directly, by their own
powers," rather than via convention or engendered expectation (Habib 2022, §1). Consenting is
another: my permission alters what you may do to me.

Asking has three marks of a power.

*First, it burdens.* After a question has been put to me, my silence needs a licence. I may answer,
or say "I'd rather not," or "that's not your business," or "look it up" — but some such move is now
called for, and simple non-response is a small offence that it was not a moment earlier. Contrast
being told something: I may receive an assertion in total silence without owing anybody
anything.<sup>[2]</sup> The burden is defeasible and often tiny, but it is normative rather than
merely social, as is shown by the fact that "you didn't answer my question" is a rebuke, and by the
fact that our most formal institutions — the subpoena, discovery, the parliamentary question — are
recognisably elaborations of it rather than exceptions to it. Habgood-Coote, who rejects the
classification of asking as a directive, grants the burden in his opening sentences all the same:
"asking calls for a response — either a direct answer ('the bus comes at 11.30') or an excuse ('I
don't know')" (2026, §1). I need no more than that. On his own positive view asking is a proposal,
and a proposal put to me calls for acceptance or refusal; ignoring one is a small offence in just
the way I have described.

*Second, it authorises.* A question confers on its addressee a standing to speak on a matter that
they may not otherwise have had. "You asked" is a complete defence to the charge of offering an
unwanted opinion. This is easy to miss because the power is benign, but it is a power: the asker
opens a door, and what comes through it enters licensed.

*Third — the thesis — it makes the asker answerable.* Having summoned the answer, the asker owes
the answerer both that the answer be allowed to bear and an account, if asked, of how it bore.

Making the third mark precise requires two notions.

Every ask directs the answer somewhere. Call the **bearing point** of an ask the attitude or
decision to which answers of the kind it calls for are addressed, in the setting in which it is
put. This is not the asker's private purpose. It is fixed publicly, by what would count as
answering the question together with where answers of that kind go in that setting: "what time is
it?" is addressed to the asker's belief about the time; "may I borrow your car?" to the asker's
decision whether to take it; an examiner's "what is the capital of Mali?" not to his belief about
Mali but to his assessment of the candidate, which is why Searle's distinction between "requesting
information" and "requesting that the hearer display knowledge" (Cross and Roelofsen 2026, §1.1) is
a distinction between bearing points rather than a list of exceptions.

Second, there is a way of taking in an answer that is not receiving it as one. An answer bears *as
an answer* only if it bears through its content, taken on the answerer's authority — if the
answerer's say-so is what does the work. Call this **answer-reception**, and contrast
**symptom-use**: treating the utterance as evidence about the answerer, or about how an audience
will react, rather than as a claim advanced on the answerer's own credit. A council that reads
consultation responses to predict which objections will generate litigation is putting them to use,
and could give a crisp account of the use. But it is not receiving them as answers to the question
it asked. It is reading them as symptoms.

Then:

> **Live Dependence (LD).** At the time of asking, the asker is positioned to let the answer bear,
> by answer-reception, on the ask's bearing point; and there are two answers within the range the
> asker takes the addressee might give between which the bearing-point attitude, so formed, would
> differ.

> **Accounting (AC).** The asker is disposed, on the addressee's request and within reasonable
> limits, to say how the answer bore on the bearing point.

> **Standing (ST).** The asker is entitled to impose on this addressee the burden that answering
> would involve, given who they are, who the addressee is, how available the answer is elsewhere,
> and how often it has already been given.

> **Fair Terms (FT).** The ask is not so structured that every available response is costly to the
> addressee.

LD, ST and FT are conditions on the burden-imposing side of the power. AC is an obligation
generated by an exercise that meets them, so failing it is a breach rather than a nullity.

Two clarifications, since the thesis is easily over-read. LD does not require that the asker be
undecided, unconfident, or likely to change their mind: I may be ninety-five per cent sure and
satisfy it comfortably. It requires only that the answer be *positioned* to make a difference, at
the place where answers of that kind belong. And AC does not require justification: the asker need
not persuade the answerer that the answer was rightly weighed, only be able to show that it was
received as an answer rather than as noise.

Searle's preparatory condition on requests gestures at ST — "if I make a request to someone to do
something which it is obvious that he is already doing or is about to do, then my request is
pointless and to that extent defective," an instance of the requirement that "the act must have a
point" (Searle 1965, 12) — but only gestures. Turning pointlessness into a condition of
*entitlement to impose a burden on this person* is my extension, not his.

## 5. Why the asker is answerable: the argument from staked judgment

Why should soliciting an answer bind the solicitor? Commands do not bind the commander; requests
for objects do not oblige the requester to use them.

The answer lies in what an answer is. Consider the difference between asking you to pass the salt
and asking you where the salt is. In the first, you supply a thing, and the thing is indifferent to
your supplying it. In the second, you supply *your judgment*. You do not merely emit a sentence;
you advance a claim on your own authority and invite me to take it on your say-so. You stake
yourself. If you are wrong, you are on the hook in a way no conduit is: "you told me it was in the
cupboard" is a reproach, and "I don't know" is itself a self-report that exposes you.

> **P1.** In answering, one does not transfer a commodity but advances a judgment on one's own
> authority, thereby assuming a liability for it — for its truth, its competence, or its candour.
>
> **P2.** To ask is to induce the addressee to assume that liability.
>
> **P3.** The liability of answering is assumed for a specific consideration, fixed by the act
> rather than by either party's wishes: that the judgment advanced be received as a judgment and
> allowed to bear where the question directed it — that is, by answer-reception at the bearing
> point.
>
> **P4.** It is wrong to induce another to assume a liability while having foreclosed the very
> consideration for which, by the nature of the act, that liability is assumed — unless they
> release you.
>
> **C.** So an asker who has foreclosed answer-reception at the bearing point wrongs the answerer,
> absent the answerer's release.

P3 is where an earlier version of this argument went wrong, and it is worth saying how, because the
repair is the same repair LD needed. If the consideration is fixed by the *asker's* purposes, then
the litigation-minded council satisfies the premise: it had a purpose, the answers served it. If it
is fixed by the *answerer's* interest in being heeded, the argument proves too much, since not
every answerer who is disappointed has been wronged. Fixed instead by the act — by where answers of
the kind solicited are addressed, and by the only mode in which a judgment can be received as a
judgment — it is the same for both parties, and it is public.

P4 is the moral engine, and it is not peculiar to speech. It is why soliciting tenders for a
contract already awarded is a fraud on the bidders even if their costs are trivial, and why
inviting applications to a filled post wrongs the applicants even if they enjoyed writing them. The
general form is an inducement principle: where A's invitation is the reason B incurs an exposure, A
owes B the conditions under which that exposure has the point A's invitation gave it. I do not
derive this from anything deeper, and I do not think it is derived from anything deeper; I offer it
as a principle of a familiar family, supported by cases across the contractual, the institutional
and the conversational.

What makes asking unlike commanding is P1 together with P3. A command treats the addressee as an
agent whose *doing* is wanted, and a doing can be received while its author is ignored. A question
treats them as an agent whose *take* is wanted, and a take cannot be received while its author is
bypassed, in the way a wrench can be received while its fetcher is ignored. Call this the
**alienability asymmetry**: the deliverable of an ask is inseparable from the deliverer's standing
as one who judges. It is what makes symptom-use a failure of reception rather than a different kind
of reception. Discard the judgment and you have not merely declined to use a good; you have
solicited a person's credit and then declined to bank it. The wrong is not theft of labour. It is
**exposure without reception**.

Notice how this explains the gradient of complaint across §1. The cost of a syllable is nil, but
the exposure is not: in answering "not at all" you have put your preference on the record and made
yourself the author of a permission. When it turns out the dog was already in the car, what you
resent is that your authorship was counterfeit.

One case tests the apparatus usefully. A mentor runs a practice viva. Nothing hangs on it: no post,
no grade, no reference. The student assumes the full liability of answering — he is judged, he is on
record, he can be wrong — and the benefit for which candidates ordinarily answer interview
questions, that their performance should count towards a decision, is foreclosed in advance and
both know it. This is plainly not a wrong, and it is not free acceptance that makes it innocent:
the student would not be wronged even if a supervisor had press-ganged him into it. The account
explains why without special pleading. In that setting, answers of the kind called for are
addressed to the mentor's assessment of the performance, which genuinely depends on them and is
given back. Nothing is foreclosed, and the answers are received as answers: the mentor engages
their content. It is the setting, publicly available to both, that fixes this — not a redescription
of the mentor's private purposes after the fact.

## 6. The participation argument, and what fails when it fails

The argument of §5 shows that non-responsive asking wrongs the answerer. A second argument shows
something about the act itself.

Habgood-Coote's positive view is that "a speaker S asks a hearer H a question Q iff either: i) S
proposes that S and H adopt a shared intention that H answer Q," or the same with S answering, or
with both (2026, §4); and that asking so understood "aims at common knowledge of the complete
answer to a question" (§4). I take over the structure without taking over the semantics, because it
supplies the mechanism my thesis needs and he does not use. A shared intention, as he says,
"generates obligations for everyone who participates in it, but its content can concern just one
person"; and it is "the fact that a speaker participates in a shared intention that a hearer does
something" that "explains her obligation to not interfere" (§4). Participation is the key term.

> **The Participation Argument.** To ask B a question is to propose that B and the asker jointly
> see to it that the question is answered — at a minimum, that B's answer be put where answers of
> that kind go. A proposal for a joint undertaking is defective *as a proposal* if its proposer is
> not party to the undertaking proposed. One who says "let's go to the cinema" having made it
> impossible for himself to go has not proposed a joint plan, whatever else he has done. An asker
> who has foreclosed answer-reception at the bearing point is not party to the undertaking his ask
> proposes. So no share of that undertaking falls to B, and B's burden does not arise.

Three things recommend this over the argument it replaces. It is structural rather than
psychological: what fails is participation, not sincerity, so nothing here reduces to insincerity
with a wider object. It survives transparency, and indeed is exhibited by it — announcing "our
decision is final" does not cure a non-participatory proposal; it displays one, exactly as "let's
go to the cinema, though I shan't be coming" does. And it identifies what the retort of §1 is
asking for. "Then why are you asking?" is a demand to be shown the asker's share.

An earlier version of this essay ran a different argument, on which the non-responsive asker
occupied both sides of a contradiction in a presupposition, and supported it with the analogy of "I
promise to come, though I have no intention of coming." That analogy was the wrong one. The
insincere promise is the standard illustration of what Austin calls an *abuse* — an act that comes
off and then fails a standard, as Green puts it: "here I have promised all right, but the act is
not felicitous because it is not sincere ... it fails to live up to a standard appropriate for
speech acts of its kind" (Green 2020, §2.2). The apt comparison is Green's other case: "I cannot
bet you $100 on who will win the election unless you accept that bet. If you do not accept that
bet, then I have tried to bet but have not succeeded in betting" (§2.2). A bet fails for want of
the addressee's participation; the burden of an ask fails for want of the asker's. Both are
two-party structural defects, and neither is about anyone's state of mind.

But a failed bet is nothing at all, and an ask is not nothing at all — which brings me to the
verdict, where I now think the strong claim was two claims run together.

> **Asymmetric failure.** A non-participatory ask still authorises. It does not burden.

Authorisation runs on the public face of the act. The resident who commented was invited to comment
and is entitled to have spoken; it would be perverse if the council's own defect deprived her of
the defence "you asked." Quite generally, a defective exercise of a normative power may not be
pleaded by its author against someone who relied on its face. That principle is independently
plausible — it is why a promisor cannot escape by pointing to a formal flaw in his own promise that
the promisee took at face value — and it is what keeps the account from disarming the answerer along
with the asker. The burden is different, because the burden is a *share in a joint undertaking*,
and one cannot come to have a share in an undertaking nobody has entered.

This also answers a real difficulty about access. The bearing point is often not something the
addressee can see; a permission whose trigger condition is invisible is no use to her. The reply is
that she does not need to establish the trigger. She needs a right to demand it.

> **Challenge.** The addressee of an ask may require the asker to state its bearing point and his
> position to let the answer bear there. While the demand stands unanswered, the burden of response
> is suspended. If it is refused, or cannot be met, the burden lapses. If it is met, the burden
> revives.

This is the practical payoff, and it is more modest and more usable than the verdict it replaces.
Claeys identifies the characteristic double bind of the sealioned: the target "is left with the
choice to either respond (and tire herself needlessly) or seem either unreasonable or unwilling"
(Claeys 2024). Robinson, Alfano and Astola describe the same trap: refuse, and "the sealion can play
the victim, accusing their interlocutor of bad faith for refusing to answer 'legitimate' questions"
(2025, §3.2). The bind exists because refusal reads as a normative default: she was asked, and she
did not answer. Challenge dissolves the default without requiring her to prove anything about her
interlocutor's mind or motives. She is not declining to discharge a duty. She is declining to
incur one that has not been shown to have arisen.

I should be exact about how much this buys. It removes the *normative* default, not the social
penalty. Berenstain's double bind is largely a social fact: Amina "knows that if she refuses to
explain herself to Ben or simply says, 'I don't want to talk about it,' she runs the risk of being
painted as overly emotional, irrational, hypersensitive, unfriendly, and aggressive" (2016, §3), and
no account of felicity conditions will stop that happening. What the account can do is deny the
demand its normative cover, which is the cover Berenstain describes it as travelling under — the
appearance of an invitation to participate. That is worth having, and it is not everything.

One institution has reached for something recognisably similar, and it is worth a paragraph
because the convergence is narrower than it first looks. English administrative law recognises four
conditions on lawful consultation, the "Sedley criteria," endorsed by the Supreme Court in *R
(Moseley) v Haringey LBC*, the fourth of which is "that the product of consultation must be
conscientiously taken into account in finalising any statutory proposals" ([2014] UKSC 56, [25],
quoting *R v Brent LBC, ex p Gunning* (1985) 84 LGR 168, 189). So an uptake requirement is
recognised. Separately, Lord Reed locates the purpose of the statutory duty in "public participation
in the local authority's decision-making process" ([38]), sets out "minimum requirements" for
"meaningful public participation" ([39]), and concludes that where they were not met, "in reality,
therefore, there was no consultation on the fundamental basis of the scheme" ([42]). That is the
structure I have described — validity conditions grounded in participation, whose failure voids
rather than merely spoils. But the requirements that failed in *Moseley* were the informational
ones of [39]–[41], not the fourth criterion: the document "misleadingly implied that there were no
possible alternatives" ([42]). The case therefore supports the general shape of the account and
does *not* supply an independent verdict on uptake failure. I record this because an earlier
version claimed the latter, and the judgment will not bear it.

| The litigation-risk council; "Do you accept these terms?" | Live Dependence, via bearing-point drift | burden lapses; authorisation stands |
| "We've taken your views on board", and nothing further, ever | Accounting | breach of an obligation owed to the answerer |
| Sealioning; demanding that the marginalised explain their own oppression | Standing and Fair Terms, often with Live Dependence | burden lapses on each ground independently |
| "Is the Pope a Catholic?" | no answer solicited | not an ask; nobody is exposed and nothing is owed |
| "I'm just asking whether X", where an answer *is* solicited | Live Dependence, via bearing-point drift | burden lapses; and an unacknowledged assertion stands on the table |

Two rows need comment.

**Bearing-point drift** is the case where the ask's bearing point is inert and some other use is
operative — and where, crucially, the operative use is symptom-use. This is not a fifth pathology
alongside LD failure but the most common species of it, which is why it belongs in the same column.
The clearest published example is not mine: Robinson, Alfano and Astola's terms-and-conditions
question, where the company asking "do you accept the terms and services listed above?" does "not
actually expect us to have read it all," and where "by asking, they get legal coverage from
litigation" (2025, §3.3). They classify this as *minimally obscure* bullshit and note that "here,
we have no real deception." Just so: nothing is concealed, and the defect is that the advertised
bearing point (your considered acceptance) is inert while the operative one (their liability
exposure) is a place where your standing as a judge has no purchase at all. My account reaches it
without needing obscurity, and reaches the transparent council by the same route.

**"Just asking questions"** divides. Where no answer is solicited — the rhetorical "is the Pope a
Catholic?" — there is no ask, nobody is exposed, and my account says nothing beyond that it is an
assertion in interrogative costume. Where an answer *is* solicited, as when a journalist puts a
question to a press secretary in order to harvest a clip, the addressee is genuinely exposed and
the defect is drift. The distinction matters because the two cases have different victims: the
first misleads an audience, the second uses a person. Assertion and asking each have a price. The
price of assertion is answerability for the truth of what one says; the price of asking is
answerability for what one does with the answer. The JAQ-er in the second case pays neither: not
the first, because he did not assert; and not the second, because the bearing point he operates on
is the audience's impression, to which his interlocutor's judgment is admitted only as raw
material.

The overlapping row is the one that does the most explanatory work. Berenstain's cases fail three
conditions at once: the demand is not one the asker is entitled to make of this person (ST), the
target is worse off whatever she does (FT), and the answer is wanted as "fodder for skeptical
responses," "harvest[ed] for content that can be developed into straw arguments and rejected out of
hand" (2016, §5), which is symptom-use and so an LD failure too. Overdetermination explains
something otherwise puzzling about such exchanges: why refusing them one ground at a time never
settles anything.

## 8. Objections

**(i) This is Gricean implicature.** The suggestion is that asking merely *implicates* openness, as
asserting implicates belief, and that the implicature is cancellable.

The test I would now use is not whether cancellation sounds absurd but whether it removes the act's
characteristic normative upshot while leaving the words intact. Cancel a genuine implicature and
the upshot survives: "some of the students passed — in fact all of them did" is still an assertion,
still answerable for its truth, still rebuttable. Cancel LD — "what do you think? Though nothing you
say will make any difference to anything I believe or do" — and the upshot goes: the speaker can no
longer rebuke his addressee with "you didn't answer my question." He has said the words and lost
the standing they normally confer. That is the signature of a condition on the power rather than an
implicature of the utterance.

**(ii) Over-demandingness.** If every question puts me on the hook, people will ask less, and asking
is epistemically precious.

Three replies. The obligation is graded and usually trivial to discharge: for most asks AC costs a
sentence, and for many it is waived by default. The counterfactual is wrong: the alternative to
answerable asking is not more genuine asking but more sham asking, and sham asking is
self-consuming. Where consultation is known to be ornamental, people stop consulting and stop
responding; this is the mechanism Green notes for promising, that "a pattern of abuses of speech
act institutions might deprive a person of an ability to perform speech acts" (2020, §9). And a
powers-based account has a relief valve built in: the obligation is owed *to* the addressee and so
is theirs to waive. "Ask away — you needn't tell me what you do with it" is a real and common
release.

**(iii) The examiner, the lawyer, the therapist.** Plenty of legitimate questioners know the answer
and will not be moved by it.

Bearing points handle these without special pleading. The examiner's bearing point is the grade; his
question satisfies LD robustly, and he receives the answer as an answer, engaging its content. An
examiner whose grade was fixed in advance is running a sham, which is the right result.
Cross-examination has the tribunal's finding as its bearing point rather than the questioner's
belief, and the institution is transparent about this, which is why the witness is not wronged and
also why we speak of a kangaroo court when the finding is fixed. The therapist's bearing point is
her understanding of the patient.

It is worth claiming a comparative advantage here. Robinson, Alfano and Astola save the exam
question by arguing that examiners can be surprised — the Bohr barometer story — since "quizzing that
starts as a test of knowledge can transform ... into a case where the questioner becomes informed
of unexpected answers" (2025, §3.1). That defence is fragile: an examiner asking a routine question
against a known mark scheme will not be surprised, and on their account he must be convicted. The
bearing-point treatment does not need surprise. The grade genuinely depends on the answer, and the
answer is taken on the candidate's authority as his answer. Willingness to be surprised is good
evidence of LD, not what LD consists in.

**(iv) Isn't Live Dependence just good faith?** Claeys quotes Sullivan and colleagues: one argues in
good faith when "one is willing to change their views considering some conceivable evidence or
argument that one's opponent might offer" (Claeys 2024, quoting Sullivan et al. 2020). Robinson,
Alfano and Astola say the "most definitive indication that someone is not engaging in bullshit
questioning is that they are willing to be surprised" (2025, §1).

The resemblance is real and I do not want to overstate the distance. Three things are added. Their
formulations are marks of a virtue of debaters, or diagnostics of one; mine is a condition on a
normative power, which is what delivers the lapse of the burden and hence the permission to refuse.
Theirs index openness to the asker's *views*; mine indexes it to a bearing point that is often not a
view at all, which is why mine handles examiners, consent and the litigation-risk council. And
neither has any counterpart of AC, which is where most of the ordinary wrongdoing in this area
actually lies: most bad asking is not rigged, it is unaccounted for. If I had to say which of my
conditions is the contribution, it would be that one, not LD.

**(v) Broadcast and machine asks.** A public call for comment is addressed to no one in particular;
and I ask search engines things all day.

Broadcast asks are owed severally and may be discharged jointly. Each respondent is owed the
account, which is why she may demand it in her own name; but a single published response to
comments, or an editor's report, can discharge what is owed to all of them at once. Institutions
that take consultation seriously invent precisely such joint discharges, and that is what one would
predict. The machine case is more instructive: querying a database generates no obligation of
uptake, and the account says why, without legislating about machine minds. The obligation tracks the
exposure of an agent who stakes a judgment. Where nobody is exposed, nothing is owed. A theory that
located the wrong in wasted labour or in deception would have to strain here.

**(vi) Self-directed and rhetorical asking.** Habgood-Coote's counterexamples to the standard view
are also, prima facie, counterexamples to mine: Douglass asking "what, to the American slave, is
your 4th of July?" and then answering it himself; Hilbert setting problems for a discipline.

These mark the scope of my claims rather than refuting them. My thesis concerns asks that recruit an
answer from the addressee — clause (i) of the Shared Proposal view. Self-directed asking, clause
(ii), recruits nothing from the addressee and exposes nobody, so it generates no answerability of
this kind. Notice, though, which way it cuts: Habgood-Coote says of Douglass that "he is generating
a conversational commitment for himself: if at the end of the speech he hadn't answered, he would
have let the audience down" (2026, §3.2). That is an asker-side commitment created by asking, which
is the family of thing I am arguing for. Shared asking, clause (iii), recruits both parties, and
the account predicts that the obligations run both ways — as they do, in the ordinary etiquette of
collaborative inquiry, where someone who proposes a joint question and then ignores the collective
answer has behaved exactly as badly as my council.

## 9. What follows

*For institutions.* The standard defence of consultation is instrumental: it produces better
decisions. On the present account that defence is insufficient even when true, because it leaves the
consultee's complaint unexplained — an ignored consultee whose view was wrong has still been wronged
— and because it predicts the wrong remedy. If the point were information, the remedy for bad
consultation would be better information-gathering. If the point is answerability, the remedy is
publication of how the responses bore, which is a different institutional design and a testable
difference between the two justifications.

*For zetetic epistemology.* If the zetetic instrumental principle tells the inquirer to take the
necessary means to answering her question, and asking others is the cheapest means, then the
principle unqualified licenses free-riding on other people's epistemic labour. The norms of asking
are a candidate constraint and have the right shape: they do not forbid leaning on others, they
price it. This is also where my account and Habgood-Coote's converge on a structural point from
different directions. He argues that the norms of asking come apart from the norms of inquiry
because asking is collective where inquiring is individual. I am arguing that they come apart
because asking recruits a second person who can be wronged. Both conclusions say that zetetic
epistemology cannot get the norms of asking by reading them off the norms of inquiry.

*For the second-personal framework.* Darwall's circle runs from authority to accountability: "the
authority to demand implies, not just a reason for the addressee to comply, but also his being
responsible for doing so," and these notions "comprise an interdefinable circle" (Darwall 2006, ch.
1, 16–17, course PDF). The circle as stated makes the *addressee* accountable. What asking shows is
that for at least one power the implication also runs the other way: the authority to summon an
answer is conditional on the summoner's liability to account for it. If that is right for asking,
it is worth asking — and I mean the question seriously — which other powers are held on similar
terms. Demanding an apology, calling someone to account, issuing a reproach: each solicits from
another an exercise of agency that exposes them. Perhaps powers to recruit another's agency are, as
a class, held in trust for the exercise they recruit.
