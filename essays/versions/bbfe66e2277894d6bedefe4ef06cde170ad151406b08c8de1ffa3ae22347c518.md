# What a Second Vote Cannot Repair

A community decides to try a policy for a year. Nobody must stay. At the end, those who remain will vote on whether to continue. Initial authorization, freedom of exit, and a fair second vote seem to offer a reassuring combination: the community can learn without binding itself indefinitely.

There is a difficulty when trying the policy changes who belongs to the community. People whose experience most urgently calls for reconsideration may leave before reconsideration becomes available. The second vote can then perform one legitimate function while failing another. It can authorize what the remaining members will do together. It need not fulfill the institution's assurance that those who underwent the trial would receive an answer about what happened to them.

This is not an argument that former members should govern an association forever. It is an argument that their claim to a promised review need not expire with their membership. Its distinctive target is the combination of safeguards. An exit right can work exactly as intended, and a renewal vote can be perfectly fair, while exercising the first removes the standing needed to invoke the second. The safeguards do not automatically add up to the protection that was offered.

## Two meanings of “we can reconsider”

Temporary authorization can mean something quite modest: this rule will lapse unless the appropriate body authorizes another period. Call this the renewal assurance. It concerns future governance. It makes no promise to compensate anyone, reconsider every past decision, or hold a hearing for everyone who participated.

A different assurance can help justify consenting to an uncertain trial: we will assess what the trial does to its participants, hear objections arising from their experience, and have the power to correct failures where correction is warranted. Call this the accountability assurance. It concerns the institution's answerability for the trial. A participant might reasonably accept a risk under this assurance that she would reject under the renewal assurance alone.

The distinction matters even if one meeting is supposed to deliver both. A vote on next year's rules can be the occasion for reviewing this year's experience, but the two tasks do not become identical. An institution could discontinue an objectionable policy without answering anyone's complaint. Conversely, it could investigate complaints properly and conclude that a suitably amended policy should continue.

I will defend a conditional claim. When an institution invokes the accountability assurance to justify a democratic trial, it cannot fulfill that assurance solely through a membership-bound renewal process if people lose access to that process by leaving. It must preserve some effective route to review after exit, or provide another arrangement that genuinely delivers what was assured. This condition does not follow from putting an expiration date on a rule.

“Effective” here is limited but substantive. A complaint must be receivable, its relevant evidence assessable, and a reasoned answer obtainable from a body competent to address the issue. Where that body's assessment finds correction warranted, the arrangement must provide an appropriate capacity for correction or redress. None of this guarantees the complainant a favorable result. Nor does it give every person a veto over the review's conclusions.

## A trial that passes both votes

Consider a democratic association that maintains shared workshops. Its hundred members unanimously approve a one-year trial of a new system for allocating maintenance duties. Forty expect to benefit. Sixty remain uncertain, particularly about the effect on people with irregular working hours. They nevertheless approve after being assured that the trial will be assessed in light of members' actual experience and that serious problems can be addressed at the annual review.

Exit is practical. Members can transfer their ordinary credits to another workshop network. There is no punishment for leaving, and nobody is being held hostage to an essential service. We can also grant that the pilot is reasonable on the evidence available at its inception. The case does not depend on an originally fraudulent vote or a policy that everyone should have known was wrong.

The trial works well for forty members. For the other sixty, unexpected scheduling burdens make continued participation unattractive. They complain, but relief is deferred to the promised annual review. Waiting would mean continuing to incur the burdens. They therefore leave.

At the review, the forty remaining members sincerely and unanimously vote to retain the new allocation system. Their meeting is well advertised, orderly, informed and unpressured. The policy suits the activity they now undertake together. Suppose, moreover, that former members have no remaining interest in the ordinary allocation of next year's workshop duties. There is a good case for allowing the forty to govern that activity without a continuing electorate of former members.

But the institution's review procedure permits only current members to put complaints on its agenda, demand an answer, or challenge the handling of evidence. The forty consider the reports of departures as part of their assessment of future policy. They conclude, accurately, that the association now serves a smaller group well. The chair treats renewal as completion of the promised review. The sixty are told that they have left and that the matter is closed.

The problem is not that the forty reached the wrong decision about next year's duties. It is that the institution has substituted their decision for a different obligation: answering the complaints under the assurance on which the trial was authorized. It has treated those most moved to use the exit safeguard as people to whom the accountability safeguard no longer applies.

This description does not establish that the sixty deserve repayment, reinstatement or an apology. Their complaints may fail. Perhaps the burdens fell within risks they knowingly accepted, or the administrators acted reasonably throughout. Those are possible answers following review, not reasons why departure itself completes review. The institution must distinguish “your complaint does not succeed” from “your complaint ceased to count when you left.”

## More than missing information

Part of the trouble is familiar selection. Imagine another history with the same initial authorization, the same forty current members, and the same unanimous renewal vote. In that history, the other sixty enjoyed the trial but departed for unrelated reasons. If we look only at the membership numbers and the renewal result, we cannot tell the two histories apart. Survivor approval cannot by itself establish how the original participants experienced the trial.

There is nothing novel about that observation. Hirschman's account of exit and voice already explains how departure can remove precisely those people most likely to press an organization to improve. His discussion of demanding consumers and dissatisfied members provides the causal background here (1970, chapter 4, especially pp. 51–55).

But obtaining perfect information does not finish the argument. Give the forty a complete and accurate account of every departure and every complaint. Suppose they carefully deliberate and correctly judge that continuation is desirable for their current activities. The institutional question remains: have the sixty received the review they were assured, or have the forty merely made an informed decision about themselves?

Information about a person's experience can serve different purposes. It can help current members choose a successful future policy. It can also support an answer owed to that person about an earlier exercise of collective power. An institution may do the first without doing the second. Thoroughly reading the complaints is not sufficient if the only question the body is empowered to settle is whether current members want to continue.

This need not require a different set of human reviewers. The remaining members could constitute an appropriate review body if former participants retain access to it, if it must address the relevant claims, and if its competence extends to the appropriate consequences. The distinction concerns what the body is doing and to whom it is answerable. A competent body can conclude that no remedy is due without obtaining every complainant's agreement. What it cannot do is derive that conclusion simply from its separate authority to renew.

This is the proposed failure of composition. Initial consent authorizes an uncertain period of common activity. Exit protects people who find continued participation too burdensome. Renewal puts future activity under the control of its legitimate participants. Each can be valid. Yet, when the institution also offers answerability for the trial, their conjunction is insufficient. It leaves a gap if using exit removes the standing through which the promised answerability must be exercised.

## Why this concerns democracy

An obvious objection is that the association has merely broken a promise. Why present that as a problem in democratic theory?

The objection identifies a genuine limitation. Persistence of institutional obligations is not a special discovery about democracy. A privately owned business can also promise review and then improperly deny it to departing customers. Nothing in my argument establishes a universal right to post-exit participation from democracy alone.

The democratic significance lies in the justificatory work assigned to the assurance. A collective decision under uncertainty exposes some participants to costs that others do not yet understand. A trial, coupled with review, can make it reasonable for those participants to authorize an arrangement they would not yet endorse permanently. They are not required to settle the full issue before they have experienced it. Instead, the institution reserves a place for their later experience in the exercise of collective judgment.

If only those who remain can claim that place, the assurance protects a narrower group than the argument for accepting the trial suggested. A participant is offered future answerability as a reason to authorize present risk, but must continue accepting the burdens to preserve access to that answerability. Freedom of exit remains valuable. Nevertheless, it has become costly in a further institutional sense: leaving forfeits the route by which the participant was supposed to obtain an answer.

The ordinary duty to keep an assurance therefore has a particular consequence for one defense of democratic experimentation. Consent, exit and renewal cannot be cited as independently accumulating protections without checking whether one protection disables access to another. This remains true when administrators are sincere and the original decision is valid. Initial validity does not guarantee faithful performance throughout the trial.

Nor does the argument make the first vote retroactively invalid whenever performance fails. People can validly authorize an arrangement whose administrators later fail them. The appropriate conclusion may be a surviving duty, not nullification of every decision the association has made. That distinction is important: finding an accountability defect does not automatically deprive current members of authority over their shared future.

## Two questions, without two permanent electorates

The tempting response is to let everyone present at the beginning vote at the end. This can be useful, but it does not by itself solve the problem.

First, a former participant might have no appropriate claim to decide ordinary future policy. Reinstating all former members as permanent voters would burden current self-government with an indefinitely expanding constituency. Second, an expanded yes-or-no renewal ballot may still fail to answer complaints about the trial. More people voting on one question does not ensure that another question gets considered.

The alternative is to distinguish renewal jurisdiction from trial-accountability jurisdiction. The first determines who may authorize future common activity. The second determines who may call the institution to account for the trial and through what procedure. A former participant can lack the first kind of standing and retain the second.

Such differentiation is not a new general theory of democratic boundaries. Erman's function-sensitive approach already argues that different decision functions can be governed by different inclusion principles, justified through different values (2022). The distinction here is narrower. It concerns an enduring claim generated by the justification and conduct of a particular trial, alongside a legitimate change in the membership entitled to govern future activity. It does not require choosing one comprehensive principle for every democratic electorate.

A sufficient arrangement might be simple. Trial participants retain the right to submit a complaint for a stated review period after departure. The review body must assess complaints against the trial's authorized terms and supply reasons for its conclusions. It has access to relevant records and the competence to recommend or require appropriate corrective measures. Its conclusions do not depend on whether complainants still use the workshops. The current assembly remains free, within its ordinary authority, to determine future workshop policy.

These are examples of how to satisfy the condition, not a mandatory administrative blueprint. An existing ombudsman, a review committee, or a properly empowered assembly could suffice. Representation may replace personal participation if it gives the relevant claims an effective hearing and answer. A second physical institution is unnecessary; a second question with adequate standing and powers is necessary.

The institution, rather than a permanent electoral constituency, carries the obligation through time. Current members inherit governance of an association that undertook the trial. They do not inherit unlimited liability merely because they remain, but neither can the association make its relevant duties disappear by changing the roster of people entitled to vote.

## The limits of the objection

The argument requires several qualifications.

**Departure need not be caused by the policy.** An independently departing participant may retain exactly the same claim under the review assurance. The source of the claim is not proof of policy-induced exit. It is the institution's undertaking toward a person who participated in the trial. Causal exit matters because it explains a systematic vulnerability: the experience that most urgently motivates a complaint may also motivate conduct that removes complaint standing. It identifies why the two safeguards can work against one another, not who alone deserves review. A sound procedure should not require former participants to prove the policy caused their departure before allowing them to complain about it.

**Not every sunset offers the accountability assurance.** An association may openly propose a temporary change with only a future renewal vote. Prospective members might knowingly choose among associations offering different packages, and former members might knowingly waive certain review rights. If no relevant assurance or independently grounded obligation exists, my argument cannot manufacture it. The arrangement must then be defended on those more limited terms. It cannot receive credit for accountability it has not undertaken to provide.

An explicit and valid waiver also differs from the exercise of an exit right. To leave an activity is to stop participating in it. Whether leaving additionally relinquishes a retained claim about the activity requires a further reason. Treating both acts as necessarily identical would let the exit safeguard silently amend the accountability assurance. In some contexts clear, informed terms may establish that connection. In others they may be impermissible. The present argument need not settle the limits of waiver generally.

**Review does not reverse history.** People may have lost time, opportunities or relationships that cannot be restored. Preserving a hearing does not make those losses disappear. The condition concerns continued contestability and warranted response. It therefore limits, rather than supports, the rhetoric that a temporary rule makes an experiment wholly reversible.

**Post-exit standing is not a claim to victory.** Former members might strategically complain, misunderstand the original terms, or simply dislike a valid decision. Their claims can be rejected on their merits. Time limits, evidential requirements and procedures against abuse can be appropriate. What requires justification is a membership condition that excludes the very people to whom review was offered. Removing that condition does not settle the applicable standards of responsibility or redress.

**Current members may bear costs.** Reviewing the trial can consume resources; correcting a failure may cost more. That does not show that former members must regain an unrestricted vote. Current governance routinely operates within obligations that its present electorate did not individually undertake. The scope of the trial obligation must be specified and defended, but its possible cost is not a reason to identify it with the current majority's preferences. Conversely, former participants cannot use the accountability assurance to demand control over unrelated future decisions.

**Membership change need not be objectionable.** An experiment may reveal that an association serves one group better than another. A smaller association can be a legitimate result, particularly where alternatives are accessible and departure is free. My argument permits that outcome. It challenges the inference from legitimate specialization to completed accountability. The association can answer its former participants properly and then continue serving those who remain.

## What this adds to criticism of sunset clauses

The general warning that an expiry date is insufficient is established. Molloy argues that sunset clauses require effective review and draws attention to who participates, what is examined, and whether scrutiny has substance (2020, especially pp. 14–19). The present argument does not compete with that warning. It identifies one reason why a review that is exemplary as a renewal procedure may still be inadequate: its membership rule can exclude people whose participation in the trial generated a continuing claim.

Nor does the argument refute the value of flexibility. Kuyper presents exit and renegotiation provisions as ways to facilitate initial cooperation, learning and correction of institutional development (2013). His distinctions matter: temporary escape from an agreement need not remove membership or review standing. Where standing survives, my objection does not arise. The tension concerns designs in which actual exit removes access to the procedure invoked to make provisional cooperation acceptable.

The additional claim is thus precise. Fair initial authorization and fair prospective renewal do not exhaust the democratic assessment of a trial. There can be an unmet obligation between them, even when both votes are valid, all relevant facts are known, membership has changed legitimately, and no former member has a claim to govern the association's ordinary future. The obligation can be honored without freezing the electorate. That combination is what the workshop case is meant to establish.

A second vote can decide that a community should keep going. It cannot, merely by deciding that, establish that the community has finished answering for how it got there.
