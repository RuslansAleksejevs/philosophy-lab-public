# What a New Proof Cannot Repair

A conclusion can remain rationally believable after the argument used to establish it has failed. The familiar response is to distinguish having good reasons for a belief from believing it for good reasons. But a further distinction matters. An agent can have a good deductive reason for a conclusion, know that the conclusion follows from her premises, and nevertheless be required to withdraw a particular deduction of it. Neither the truth of the conclusion nor the existence of an acceptable deduction vindicates the deduction she actually used.

This essay develops the normative significance of that last distinction. Its central claim is a principle of non-substitution: independent support for a conclusion cannot, merely by supporting that conclusion, answer an objection to a specified inferential route. It can justify retaining the belief. It can supply a replacement route. It cannot make the old route an acceptable ground without addressing the objection to it. This principle identifies a limitation in approaches that connect logical consequence to rational belief through constraints on attitudes toward propositions, even when those attitudes include beliefs about consequence. The limitation is not that those approaches are false. It is that they leave a question unanswered: which of the agent's accepted routes may continue doing justificatory work?

The proposal is deliberately narrower than a new general theory of logic's authority. Norms governing inferential methods already exist in the literature, and proof correction is hardly an unfamiliar demand. What needs clarification is the structure of a repair that leaves the relevant beliefs and the end-to-end consequence relation unchanged. That structure also sets a limit on what logic alone can require.

## 1. A correct conclusion, an unacceptable route

Suppose an engineer accepts three premises about a device. Either circuit P or circuit Q is active. If P is active, the indicator is lit. If Q is active, the indicator is lit. The indicator's being lit follows from these premises by reasoning through the two cases.

Her recorded deduction instead takes the first premise, selects P, and applies the conditional about P. Asked why she selected P, she says that the disjunction licenses selecting its first disjunct. She is not using a supposition that will later be discharged. She presents P as established from the stated premises. A colleague supplies the obvious counterexample: Q might be active while P is inactive.

Assume that P is in fact active. The engineer also has an independent, reliable measurement establishing this. She has excellent grounds for every proposition she believes, including the conclusion. Moreover, when the colleague explains the proper argument by cases, she recognizes that the stated premises entail that conclusion. None of these concessions vindicates her recorded step from the disjunction to P. The measurement could be added as an additional premise. The argument by cases could replace her argument. Either response changes what establishes what. Simply repeating that the indicator is lit does neither.

This is not a case in which the correct repair must delete a belief. Nor does the repair have to alter the engineer's judgment about whether the original premises entail the final conclusion. The relevant change is to her reliance on one route between them. She should stop treating the selection of P from the disjunction as a successful deductive step.

The distinction can be stated precisely without a theory of belief individuation. Let the premises be:

Γ = {p ∨ q, p → s, q → s}.

In classical propositional logic, Γ entails s. The defective route proceeds through p. Yet Γ does not entail p: take p false, q true and s true. All of Γ is true while p is false. The engineer's independent evidence for p does not remove that countervaluation. It supplies a reason to change the premise set of the relevant step. The repair is therefore an alteration of the argument's dependence structure, even though the actual values of p, q and s and the engineer's justified beliefs need not change.

The example is elementary so that its philosophical burden is visible. It does not depend on disputes about higher-order evidence, enormous proofs or inaccessible logical truths. The agent understands the precise defect. The conclusion has independent support. End-to-end entailment is common ground. The outstanding requirement concerns the route alone.

## 2. What the existing bridges already capture

MacFarlane's influential taxonomy varies the force, scope and polarity of bridge principles connecting consequence with norms for believing premise and conclusion propositions. It also permits versions triggered by knowledge of consequence. These distinctions help explain why a valid argument does not straightforwardly oblige someone to add its conclusion: sometimes a premise should be abandoned, and not every consequence merits attention. MacFarlane explicitly distinguishes formal inference from belief revision; he does not simply overlook norms of proof. The question is whether constraints on belief and belief change exhaust the significant connection between logic and ordinary reasoning. [MacFarlane 2004, §§2–3](https://johnmacfarlane.net/normativity_of_logic.pdf)

They do not exhaust it in the engineer's case. All the required attitudes to the original premises and conclusion can be in place while the inferential defect persists. Adding knowledge of Γ's entailing s does not fix this: the engineer can obtain that knowledge from the colleague while continuing to defend her own route. Her failure is not a missing belief about the end-to-end entailment.

There is an important precedent for moving beyond combinations of belief. Evershed argues that invalidity can constrain methods of deductive belief formation. Having another reason for the conclusion does not exempt a person from the restriction on the invalid deduction. His account expressly distinguishes methods from belief combinations and permits alternative, non-deductive methods of reaching the same conclusion. This already establishes much of the ground needed here. [Evershed 2021, §§4–6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-021-03049-z)

My case isolates a further parameter. The final conclusion is entailed by the stated premises. An invalidity principle with those endpoints therefore has no invalidity fact to trigger it. One must descend to the inferential route, identifying the intermediate claim and the premises purportedly supporting that claim. Doing so reveals the defect. That observation is not a refutation of Evershed: his method-based framework can accommodate finer descriptions. It shows why such descriptions matter and why merely adding an invalidity branch to an endpoint taxonomy does not finish the task.

Boghossian connects inference with taking premises to support a conclusion and distinguishes deductive from inductive intentions. I need no universal claim about what every inference must represent. The engineer explicitly presents a deduction from specified premises, so the relevant dependence claim is present whatever happens in less reflective thought. [Boghossian 2014, §§3–4](https://paulboghossian.com/pb/files/WhatIsInference.pdf)

There are consequently three questions to keep apart: whether the premises entail the conclusion; whether the agent is entitled to the conclusion; and whether this route is an acceptable way for her to establish it from these premises. Positive answers to the first two do not guarantee a positive answer to the third.

## 3. Why independent support does not substitute

The non-substitution principle is not the claim that one must keep using an old proof until one repairs it. Abandoning it is an entirely satisfactory response. It is also not the claim that defects in one argument contaminate everything its author believes. The point is about what counts as meeting a particular objection.

Consider an argument presented as establishing c from Γ by route r. A relevant objection identifies a failure in the route's claimed support: an intermediate step lacks the required premises, an assumption remains undischarged, or the warrant for trusting an otherwise opaque segment has been undermined. An answer must either remove that failure or withdraw the claim that the route currently establishes c from Γ. Evidence that establishes c by another means does not by itself do either.

The argument for this principle has three parts. First, using an argument as a ground commits one to more than its conclusion. It makes some transitions, assumptions or procedures responsible for supporting that conclusion. This commitment need not be another premise in the argument. It is manifest in which objections would force the agent to reconsider the argument's use.

Second, objections to those transitions have satisfaction conditions that are not identical to establishing the conclusion. In the engineer's case, the objection is met by identifying a premise that warrants P, by treating P as a temporary supposition inside a properly completed argument by cases, or by abandoning the step. It is not met by more evidence that the light is on. The countervaluation remains available for exactly the inference that was challenged.

Third, treating any new support for c as a defence of r erases this distinction. It permits an argument's success to be determined entirely by resources it does not contain or employ. If that were acceptable, every defective proof of a known theorem would be protected from criticism by the theorem's independently established status. Proof checking would reduce to checking whether someone else had secured the same answer. That cannot be the relevant standard for identifying what a particular proof establishes.

This last consequence is not merely inconvenient for mathematicians. The ability to ask which assumptions an agent's conclusion depends on is central to ordinary deliberation. Suppose the engineer must later assess the device under circumstances in which no independent measurement of P is available. The repaired argument by cases still establishes that the indicator is lit. The old selection of P does not. Knowing which route is licensed determines which resources can be withdrawn while preserving the inference's force.

The normative force here comes from the agent's active use of a route as a reason. Once she understands a decisive objection to that use, continuing it without answering the objection misrepresents the support she has. The remedy is local: cease relying on the route in that capacity, unless and until the defect is addressed. She need not abandon the conclusion, research every possible objection or reconstruct every inference she has ever made.

One might call this a demand to keep one's reasons accountable to their actual work. The useful content lies in its non-substitution clause. A replacement can preserve a conclusion while changing its basis. It cannot be silently credited as a success of the discarded basis.

## 4. Continuing reliance and rational repair

Why speak of continuing reliance if the original inference happened in the past? A past event cannot be changed. But its role in present reasoning can change. People cite earlier arguments, treat their assumptions as sufficient, reuse intermediate results and count supposedly distinct proofs as corroboration. An old route continues to matter when the agent is prepared to rely on it in one of these ways.

The engineer can respond appropriately by saying: “I still believe the indicator is lit, but my first argument did not establish it. The argument by cases does.” No forced oscillation in belief is needed. She has changed which inferential resources she treats as available.

Contrast an apparent repair in which she says: “I know my selection of P was invalid, but my proof is fine because the result is correct.” Here the defect remains operative. If she later exports the proof, omitting the independent measurement, she is presenting a dependence relation she has already recognized as unsupported. The same problem exists privately if she uses that route as a template for other disjunctions.

There is also an intermediate case. She accepts the objection and shelves the proof without yet acquiring an alternative argument, while keeping the conclusion on independent observational grounds. This is a complete response to the local requirement. Logic does not demand that she recover a deductive basis for every independently justified belief. Whether doing so is worth her time depends on what she needs the deduction for.

These alternatives explain why a repair norm should distinguish withdrawal of reliance from production of a new proof. A demand always to produce a replacement would be excessively strong. A demand merely to retain a defensible conclusion would be too weak. The appropriate response is to stop assigning the challenged route justificatory work it can no longer perform on the agent's acknowledged information.

Discussion of proof repair already gives diachronic correction an important place. Hamami's publisher abstract, for example, proposes that mathematical justification involves correcting repairable proofs and rejecting unrepairable ones. I do not assess his full account here. The narrower point is that correcting a belief and correcting a proof have different objects, and replacement support can accomplish the former without accomplishing the latter. [Hamami 2023, abstract](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/715137)

## 5. Valid routes, defeated reliance

The elementary example concerns an actual local invalidity. There is a related case in which the logical structure contains no such failure. A researcher has two proofs of a theorem. One is a transparent short argument. The other depends on a long computer-generated derivation whose acceptance was based on a particular checker. The researcher then learns that the checker accepted malformed input of exactly the kind used in the long derivation. She cannot presently inspect the derivation herself. Stipulate that the information seriously undermines her only reason to trust that derivation, while leaving the short proof untouched. In fact, the long derivation happens to be correct.

The researcher remains entitled to the theorem and to the claim that its premises entail it. What she loses is her current warrant to treat the long derivation as an independently established proof. In particular, she cannot still count it as a second verification merely because the first proof succeeds. Doing so would turn the same surviving support into both the theorem's warrant and the supposed vindication of the compromised check.

This is an application of the non-substitution principle, not a theory of exactly when higher-order evidence defeats justification. The example stipulates a serious and relevant loss of the route's warrant. Philosophers who deny such defeat can reject that extension without rejecting the engineer's acknowledged counterexample.

The importance of separating bases is especially clear in discussions that assume a deduction is the agent's sole ground. Montessori defends a single-premise closure principle with a condition excluding defeat of the conclusion. In discussing a long inference, he notes that undermining the chain undermines the conclusion when that chain is the only ground. My two-proof case removes that restriction. It is not a counterexample to his principle; it separates the survival of justification for the conclusion from the fate of an additional route. [Montessori 2026, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-025-01024-5)

An invalidity principle applied to every local step does not by itself handle this case. No step need be invalid, and the researcher need not believe that any particular step is invalid. What is compromised is the warrant for certifying a route. A richer norm can address that, but its antecedent must include epistemic circumstances beyond an endpoint fact of consequence or non-consequence.

## 6. Is this really a norm of logic?

The strongest objection is that the essay has established an epistemic platitude, not anything about logic. Of course defective reasons need correction. Similar points apply to a discredited witness, a miscalibrated instrument or a memory exposed as unreliable. Why call the demand logical?

The objection is partly right. The general duty not to rely on a recognized defective ground is not uniquely logical. Nothing in the relation Γ entails c, taken alone, tells an agent to review a proof, assign a priority to that review or distrust a checker. A fully adequate directive will incorporate epistemic access, the agent's use of the argument and the kind of support being claimed. Presenting the non-substitution principle as a derivation of practical obligations from bare logical facts would repeat the mistake it helps diagnose.

Logic nevertheless makes a distinctive contribution to identifying the defect and the possible repairs. In the engineer's example, the countervaluation shows precisely why the intermediate claim has not been established. The argument by cases shows how the conclusion survives without that claim. Independent observation changes the permitted premises rather than the consequence relation. These distinctions are not supplied by the generic instruction to use good evidence.

There is therefore a division of explanatory labour. Logical structure determines whether a specified transition preserves the support being claimed under the relevant interpretation. Epistemic norms determine whether the agent may rely on her means of identifying that structure and how she must respond to an acknowledged failure. The route is where those contributions meet.

A second objection says that everything can be encoded as a belief. Let the engineer believe that her particular proof establishes s. Once she recognizes the bad step, rationality requires revising that belief. Why posit an additional normative object?

No claim of in-principle non-encodability is necessary. A language rich enough to mention proofs, premise dependence and continuing reliance can describe the whole case propositionally. But this concession does not vindicate a framework restricted to attitudes toward Γ and s, or even Γ's entailing s. The added proposition is about the route. Encoding it preserves, rather than eliminates, the distinction for which I have argued.

Nor does merely adding a metabelief ensure that the actual reliance changes. Someone can sincerely acknowledge that an argument is defective while continuing to use its intermediate conclusion as established from the old premises. The required correction concerns that use. A theory may explain it through beliefs, dispositions or actions. It must still represent it.

Finally, does the proposal demand too much reflective monitoring? It does not require an explicit proof for every everyday inference. The strictest case concerns a route an agent is actively endorsing after understanding a specific objection. When a route is forgotten and no longer plays any justificatory role, there may be nothing further to withdraw. When an objection is merely speculative, whether it warrants investigation is a separate question. These restrictions keep a local repair requirement from becoming an obligation to audit one's entire mind.

The resulting picture is modest but consequential. An adequate account of logical rationality must sometimes track the structure of an agent's reasons, not just the propositions she accepts. An adequate account of repair must then distinguish preserving a conclusion, replacing a basis and vindicating that basis. New proofs can accomplish the first two. They cannot accomplish the third simply by arriving at the same truth.
