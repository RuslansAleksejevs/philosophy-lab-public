# What Asking Does: Requests, Answerability, and the Deliberative Agenda

*Final manuscript (final-001), revised from `draft-001.md` in the light of `critique.md`. The original draft is preserved unchanged in its own folder.*

## 1. Cheap to refuse, expensive to ignore

A colleague stops you in the corridor: "Could you look at my draft this weekend?"

Two things are true of this small event, and they do not sit comfortably together.

The first is that the request is cheap to refuse. "Sorry, I'm swamped" will do; so, often, will "Sorry, I'd rather not." You need not show that your weekend outweighs your colleague's need. You need not weigh anything at all.

The second is that the request is expensive to ignore. If you look through your colleague as though nothing had been said and walk on, you have wronged them — and you have wronged them even if, had you considered the request, you would have been entitled to refuse. The failure is not that you gave a consideration too little weight. It is that you did not take the matter up.

Put the two together and something structural emerges. Whatever a request contributes to your practical situation is so slight that almost anything defeats it; yet it is so insistent that it cannot be passed over in silence. Considerations that almost anything defeats are normally the very considerations we are permitted to ignore. Requests are not.

This essay argues that the oddity is real and that it is diagnostic. My thesis has three parts.

**(T1) The negative claim.** The act of requesting adds no weight to the first-order balance of reasons bearing on the requested act. Whatever favours your reading the draft — your colleague's need, your friendship, the good of the profession — would favour it exactly as strongly had the request never been made.

**(T2) The positive claim.** Requesting is nonetheless the exercise of a genuine normative power, operating on a different dimension. It puts an option on the addressee's *deliberative agenda* and makes the addressee *answerable* to the requester for what becomes of it. The addressee must either comply or fail to comply for a reason she can, and normally must, give.

**(T3) The taxonomic claim.** The requirement so created is not a first-order reason; nor is it quite what Joseph Raz called a second-order reason. Raz defined second-order reasons as reasons to act, or to refrain from acting, *for* a reason. A requirement to put a question on one's agenda is neither. It is a reason bearing on deliberative *uptake*, and it deserves a category of its own.

The two substantive claims are independent: one could accept T2 while holding that requests also add weight. What I shall argue is that the second dimension does all the work that made the first seem necessary, and that when we look for the residual weight we cannot find it. If that is right, the long-running dispute about whether requests "really" give reasons has been conducted on the wrong axis. Some of morality's work is done not by adding weights but by settling what must be weighed at all.

## 2. The debate and its shared assumption

The contemporary literature is organised around one question: when I ask you to φ, does my asking *create* a reason for you to φ, or does it merely *reveal* a reason you already had?

David Enoch's influential answer is that requests exhibit "robust" reason-giving, distinct from merely epistemic reason-giving. On the fullest report available to me, for X robustly to give Y a reason "X must intend to give Y a reason, communicate this intention to Y, intend Y to recognize this intention, and intend Y's reason to depend on Y's recognition of X's intention"; and the mechanism is X's "rendering true the antecedent of a conditional along the lines of 'if X asks Y to Z, Y has a reason to Z.'"[^1] James Lewis adds a condition: the request creates a reason only if the addressee "places sufficient discretionary value" in the requester, these being "attitudes that one is not obliged to hold." Peter Schaber grounds the power in value — it is good for all of us that people should have it — and restricts it to respectful requests, which "only create reasons if they are respectful with respect to their form as well as their content."[^2]

Against these stands Daniel Weltman's defence of the epistemic account, on which "a request does not create any reasons and thus does not have any special normative power." His conclusion is deliberately deflationary: "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking" (Weltman 2023, §6).

One further position matters more than its billing suggests. Micha Gläser groups Enoch, Lewis, and Lance and Kukla together as offering what he calls a "mere addition account" of requests, and argues, on the reports of two authors who have read him, that "requests do not create additional reasons, they rather put the requestees under an obligation."[^3] Gläser is thus the closest thing in the literature to a philosopher who combines a negative claim about added reasons with a positive claim about a distinct deontic change — structurally, T1 plus something in the neighbourhood of T2. I have not been able to read him, and I say below exactly where my account must differ from his if the reports are accurate.

What unites these positions is an assumption about where the action is. All of them ask what the request contributes to the *first-order balance* — the considerations determining whether the addressee ought to φ. Enoch, Lewis and Schaber say it contributes a reason; Weltman says it contributes nothing but information; Gläser, on the reports, says it contributes an obligation. All are answers to one question.

The alternative surfaces once, and is waved away. Weltman considers N. G. Laskowski and Kenneth Silver's suggestion that a request may cause the addressee to attend to reasons already possessed, and replies: "The epistemic account bites the bullet and says that in cases where requests cause us to attend to something, there is nothing normatively special about the request. The only normative strength is in the reasons that are attended to (which already exist) and the reasons to attend to those reasons (which also exist prior to the request)." A footnote concedes and then dismisses the possibility: a request "may technically have normative power insofar as you have more reason to attend to a reason brought to your attention, because (for instance) it would be rude to ignore someone's having brought it to your attention. But this normative power has nothing to do with the request being a request rather than any other action" (Weltman 2023, §5.2 and n. 40).

That footnote is where I plant a flag. The attention-directing effect of a request is not a psychological by-product with a normative shadow. It is the whole of the request's distinctive normative work, and it has everything to do with the request being a request rather than any other action.

There is an older witness for the thought that asking resists the categories philosophers reach for. Thomas Reid divided the powers of the mind into the solitary and the social, the latter being "such operations as necessarily suppose an intercourse with some other intelligent being." His list is telling: "when he asks information, or receives it; when he bears testimony, or receives the testimony of another; when he asks a favour, or accepts one; when he gives a command to his servant, or receives one from a superior; when he plights his faith in a promise or contract." These "suppose understanding and will; but they suppose something more which is neither understanding nor will; that is, society with other intelligent beings." And: "To ask a question is as simple an operation as to judge or to reason; yet it is neither judgment nor reasoning, nor simple apprehension, nor is it any composition of these." Reid's complaint was that "in the divisions that have been made of the mind's operations, the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain."[^4]

Reid's point was about the philosophy of mind; mine is its normative analogue. In the standard divisions of practical reasons — weights, defeaters, cancellers, enablers — the social operations have again been thrown behind the curtain, because the only place the theory has for them is the balance, and the balance is not where they live.

## 3. What a theory of requests must explain

Five observations about the practice. I take them to be pre-theoretical.

**(D1) Cheap defeat.** A request may be refused for reasons that do not remotely match its apparent importance, and without any showing that the competing reason is weightier. This is what Lewis calls discretion, and what Lance and Kukla mark by writing that "it is essential to the notion of a request that this reason is not an obligation"; an imperative, by contrast, "has failed in its pragmatic function if the target does not acknowledge that she has an obligation to act as ordered."[^5]

**(D2) Costly silence.** Requests must be taken up. Treating a request as though it had not been made is a distinctive failure, and it is a failure *even when refusal would have been permissible*. Lance and Kukla note a relative: "acting as though the request was just evidence of the speaker's psychological desires" is a way of *defying* it. One can defy a request without disobeying it — already a hint that requests make demands that are not demands to act.

**(D3) Repetition does not accumulate.** If A asks S to φ, then asks again, and again, S's reason to φ does not triple. If anything the case degrades: repeated asking becomes nagging, and nagging is worse for the requester's cause than asking once. Ordinary weighted reasons do not behave like this. Ten people's needs aggregate, on most views; ten askings do not.

**(D4) The residue of refusal is an account, not a repair.** Breaking a promise calls for apology and often compensation. Refusing a request calls, at most, for an account of oneself — and sometimes for a little more: help in finding another helper, or an apology for curtness. What it does not call for is restitution, because nothing has been taken. What refusal leaves behind is a liability to answer, discharged by speech.

**(D5) The silent sufferer.** Someone is drowning and cannot call out. Your reason to save them is not one jot weaker than it would be if they could shout "Help!" Generalising: take a case in which refusing a request seems seriously wrong, remove the request while holding fixed the underlying facts and your knowledge of them, and the wrong of not acting appears undiminished. What weight there is belongs to the need, the friendship, the prior favour — not to the asking.

D5 supports T1, and it is the claim of mine most exposed to counterexample. §7 tests it on three cases designed to break it. D1 and D2 together are the explanandum that drives everything else: cheap to refuse, expensive to ignore.

## 4. Why weights are not enough

Call any view a **Weight Model** if it holds that a request contributes a first-order pro tanto reason of positive weight *w* to do the requested act. Enoch, Lewis and Schaber hold versions of this.[^6]

I should be clear about what follows, because my earlier draft was not. None of these philosophers claims that contributing such a reason is *all* a request does. A hybrid view — the request adds weight *and* puts the question on the agenda — is available off the shelf, and Schaber's requests are already multi-tasking: "Requests create non-obligatory reasons in a robust sense. However, they can create obligatory reasons in a non-robust sense, by triggering conditional obligations." So the argument of this section is not a refutation of the Weight Model. It is an argument that weights *alone* cannot deliver D2, and therefore that a second dimension is needed whatever else is true. Whether the first-order weight is also there is a separate question, taken up in §7.

**Horn 1: substantial weight.** Suppose *w* is non-negligible — comparable to a modest inconvenience. Then D1 fails. If my colleague's request has the weight of a modest inconvenience, I may not rightly refuse it merely because I would prefer to see a film, unless the film is worth more than a modest inconvenience, which it typically is not. Making *w* large enough to explain why requests grip us makes it too large to explain why they release us so easily.

**Horn 2: negligible weight.** So suppose *w* is an epsilon, smaller than any ordinary competing consideration. This preserves D1 elegantly: any genuine competing reason defeats an epsilon. But consider two principles about deliberation.

> **Idleness.** A consideration C is *idle* in a deliberative context D if the balance of reasons in D is the same whether or not C is included.
>
> **Permitted Inattention.** A deliberator is not open to criticism for failing to attend to a consideration she reasonably takes to be idle.

Permitted Inattention is not a concession to human frailty; it is a condition of deliberation being possible. Every choice sits in a field of vanishingly small considerations — the penny on the pavement, the marginal aesthetic disvalue of the chair's angle — and a deliberator who attended to all of them would never act. Attention is scarce, and rationality does not require spending it where it cannot change the outcome.

An epsilon is idle in every context in which some weightier consideration is present and the rest do not exactly balance. So in the overwhelming majority of cases — every case in which you already have some ordinary reason not to comply — you would be permitted to give the request no attention whatever. That contradicts D2. On a hard deadline I know in advance that the request cannot alter what I ought to do; the epsilon model therefore licenses me to leave the message unopened and unanswered. But this is exactly what one may not do. Something is owed to the request that is not owed to the penny.

*Reply: an epsilon can break ties.* True, and that is why the model gets D1 right. But tie-breaking contexts are rare and not identifiable in advance; the model preserves its verdicts in a narrow class of cases at the cost of leaving the main phenomenon unexplained. *Reply: weight varies with relationship and stakes.* Certainly — but the dilemma is generated pointwise. Fix any case, assign any weight, and it falls on one horn or the other. In the cases where requests are most clearly cheap to refuse, low-stakes requests among equals, they remain just as clearly impossible to ignore.

**Horn 3: the deontic retreat.** There is a better reply, and it is the one the literature actually makes. Lewis and Schaber explain discretion not by low weight but by a deontic category: "A reason is non-obligatory if it would not be blameworthy for one not to act on it, and obligatory if it would be blameworthy not to act on it."[^7] A reason can be weighty and non-obligatory; that is the point of the category. So the inference "refusal is blameless, therefore *w* is tiny, therefore the reason is idle, therefore ignorable" never gets started. My earlier draft missed this, and the correction matters.

But notice what the retreat concedes. We are now told that requests create reasons such that it is *never blameworthy not to act on them*. D2 says that it *is* blameworthy not to attend to them. Put the two together and we have a consideration that carries no liability to blame on the question of action and a firm liability to blame on the question of attention.

That combination is not available within a one-dimensional theory. On any weight-based account, the point of attending to a reason is to get the action right: attention is instrumentally required because inattention risks acting wrongly. If failing to act on the request is never blameworthy, then inattention to it cannot be blameworthy in virtue of risking a wrong act — there is no wrong act to risk. The blame attaching to inattention must therefore have some other ground. Locating that ground is the whole of my project, and the deontic retreat has conceded that there is one.

Nor is "non-obligatory reason" itself a resting place. It is not a weight; it is a deontic status that a reason has, defined by where blame does and does not fall. Anyone who invokes it has already left the scale. The question is what explains the status, and the answer I shall offer is that the request does not require the act at all — it requires an accounting.

**An independent argument: what we defend against.** Set the dilemma aside and consider what we in fact protect. We install do-not-disturb settings, unsubscribe links, gatekeepers, office hours, screening assistants, and elaborate conventions of indirection whose function is to let a request be withdrawn before it lands. We say "don't ask me that," and "I'm not going to ask, but —," precisely in order to prevent an agenda item from being created. Schaber's own example of the latter is exact: a colleague may say "I want you to read my draft (your comments are always so helpful) but I am not going to ask you."

None of this makes sense if what a request does is add a small weight to someone's scales. We do not build institutions to defend ourselves against small increments to our reasons; there would be nothing to defend against, since a small increment is by hypothesis easily outweighed and costlessly set aside. All of it makes sense if a request conscripts attention and makes silence culpable. Attention is the scarcest thing we have, and a power to commandeer it, backed by a liability to answer, is not a small power. I regard this as the strongest independent evidence for the two-dimensional picture — stronger than the dilemma, because it appeals not to intuitions about cases but to the shape of our practices.

## 5. The Agenda Thesis

> **The Agenda Thesis.** In requesting that S φ, A exercises a power to bring it about that:
> **(i) Uptake** — S is required to take up the question whether to φ, under the description "what A has asked of me";
> **(ii) Answerability** — if S does not φ, S must fail to φ *for* a reason that S can offer A as the reason on which she acted;
> **(iii) Account** — S must actually render that account to A where A would otherwise be left without one.

Three comments.

**The burden is one of production, not of persuasion.** Lawyers distinguish the burden of producing some evidence on an issue from the burden of persuading the tribunal on it. The structure of a request is the same. Before the request, S owed A nothing on the question whether to φ; the question was not S's to answer. After it, S must put something forward — but only a reason, not a *weightier* reason. That is precisely the shape of D1 and D2 taken together: the burden is easy to discharge and impossible to ignore, because discharging and ignoring are different relations to it.

This also disposes of a worry about permissiveness. If any reason discharges the burden, does bigotry discharge it? "I won't help because I dislike people like you" is, after all, a reason, and the bigot can state it. The answer is that discharging a burden is not acting well. A burden of production is met by producing something of the right *type*; it does not certify the quality of what is produced. The bigot who refuses to your face has avoided the specific wrong of ignoring you and has done something monstrous for entirely independent reasons — reasons that have nothing to do with requests and everything to do with contempt. Compare a litigant who meets a burden of production with vile testimony: he has met the burden and disgraced himself. Discharging the request-burden makes S non-culpable *for ignoring A*; it says nothing about whether S is culpable *for refusing*, which is settled by the first-order balance the request did not touch. That division of labour is the two-dimensionality in miniature.

**Clause (iii) is derived, not bolted on.** My earlier draft stated Answerability counterfactually — S must have a reason she *could* avow — and insisted that this was not a demand for actual speech. That will not do, and the objection is decisive. Take the corridor case. S hears the request, silently considers it, silently declines because she is on deadline, and walks on without a word. Counterfactual avowability is satisfied in full; yet this is exactly the wrong of looking through your colleague as though nothing had been said. A purely counterfactual condition does not deliver D2.

But the response duty need not be an extra postulate. Answerability is a *relation to another person*: it is a standing liability to be called to account by A. A liability of that kind is discharged in whatever way the circumstances allow. Where A cannot know whether S has taken the question up except by being told — which is the ordinary case, since A cannot see into S's head — the liability can be discharged only by S's telling her. The response duty is therefore what answerability comes to under the epistemic conditions that normally obtain, and the account predicts, correctly, that it lapses when those conditions fail: no answer is owed where compliance is itself manifest, where A has said "no need to reply," where A is unreachable, or where the setting makes reply absurd. A theory that simply postulated "you must reply" could not explain those exceptions.

**Sincerity, not mere avowability.** The reason S offers must be the reason on which S acted. Stated that way, the condition is a *sincerity* condition, and naming it correctly matters. It rules out pretexts: if S declines because she dislikes A but says "I'm busy," the burden is not discharged, since the reason given is not the reason acted on. It does not rule out much else. In particular it does not rule out randomisation: "I had two equally good uses of Saturday and picked by lot" is a perfectly adequate account, because the lottery was itself adopted for a reason, namely indifference between the options, and that reason is the one offered. What the condition does rule out is drift — never engaging with the question at all, and then finding after the fact that some excellent reason to refuse happened to be available. Weight models cannot register this difference, because weights attach to the objective situation rather than to what the agent acts on. Burdens are discharged by agents, not by facts.

**Standing is a permission, not a success condition.** Not everyone may put anything on anyone's agenda. A stranger may ask me for directions but not for my afternoon; an employer may ask for overtime but not for my political opinions. Schaber treats respectfulness as a condition on the *creation* of reasons: requests "only create reasons if they are respectful with respect to their form as well as their content." I take the other option. The ill-standing request lands; that is why it is an imposition. The characteristic wrong is not that a reason was given and then outweighed, but that the requester *has conscripted attention and demanded an accounting without title*. If nothing were created, there would be nothing to complain of, and the natural response — "you have no business asking" — would be idle rather than apt. That response is a challenge to standing, not a weighing, and it presupposes that something has been done. This is also where the account gives a *rival* explanation of Laskowski and Silver's datum that one can wrong a person precisely by requesting something of them: on my account requesting is always the exercise of a power over another's deliberation, and powers can be abused. They explain such wrongs by appeal to trust, and Weltman reports that their account supports his; mine is a competitor to both, not a home for either.[^8]

## 6. The structure of the second dimension

An added dimension needs its own logic. Three questions, and my best answers.

**Defeat.** Is the uptake requirement defeasible? Yes. I may ignore a request while performing surgery, or while my child runs into traffic. But notice what defeats it and what does not. It is not defeated by the *pointlessness* of uptake: that I already know I shall refuse is no licence to ignore. Nor is it defeated by small costs, which is why "I couldn't be bothered" is not an answer. It is defeated by incapacity, and by situations that themselves conscript attention more urgently. These are threshold and capacity conditions, not proportionality conditions, and that is a genuine structural difference from weight.

More telling still: when the uptake requirement is defeated, answerability is not extinguished but *deferred*. Having come out of surgery I must still say, "Sorry — I was in theatre; I can't take this on." Outweighed reasons leave no such residue. A defeated agenda item leaves a duty to answer late, which is exactly what we should expect if what was created was a liability rather than a weight.

**Conflict.** Ten people ask ten different small favours in one morning. Attention is scarce, and competition for a scarce resource is ordered by something that will look a great deal like weight. I concede the point, and think the concession is principled. Weight governs the *order* of the queue, not its *membership*. Which request I take up first is settled by urgency, cost of delay and stakes — ordinary first-order matters. That every request is on the queue, and that none drops off unanswered without residue, is not settled by weight at all. On a pure weight model the ninth request would simply be swamped and would disappear; on the present account it waits, and I owe an answer to the ninth person as much as to the first, even if a briefer one.

**Interaction.** How do agenda requirements combine with first-order reasons to determine what S ought to do? They do not compete, and that is the point of a second dimension. Raz's exclusionary reasons *remove* inputs from the first-order balance; the requirements created by a request *do not touch the inputs at all*. They require that a certain question be run through the deliberative process, and that the agent act on, and stand behind, its output. The relation is composition, not addition. This is why the account can be simultaneously very demanding (you must engage, and you must own what you do) and entirely permissive about the result (whatever the balance says, goes).

**Non-aggregation, revisited.** D3 now falls out. Burdens do not stack: once a question is on the agenda it cannot be put there again, and once S is answerable she cannot become more answerable by being asked twice. Idempotence is relative to the question, which is the right prediction: asking again after six months, or after circumstances change, *does* renew the claim, because it is no longer the same question. What repetition within a single unchanged situation adds is nothing — which is why it reads as pressure rather than as reason-giving, and why it irritates. I withdraw my earlier jibe that a weight theorist's diminishing-returns function is "ad hoc"; the honest comparison is that the burden model *derives* idempotence from the structure of questions, where the weight model must *fit* a curve to the data. Derivation is better than fitting, but it is not the same as refutation.

## 7. Does asking add weight? Three hard cases

T1 is the eliminative half of my thesis, and it rests on D5 rather than on §4. The drowning swimmer is, as an argument, weak: it is a case where the underlying reason is overwhelming, so any increment would be swamped, and where emergencies conscript attention without anyone's help. So here are three cases built to break T1.

**Near-indifference.** Two strangers need a lift home. One asks; the other visibly needs it and says nothing. I can take one. Does the asker have the stronger claim?

Many will say yes, and something in the vicinity is true, but it is not weight. Grant that I have noticed both. Then, on my account, the asymmetry is this: if I take the silent one, I owe the asker an answer, and if I take the asker, I owe the silent one nothing. That is an asymmetry in *transaction cost*, not in the merits. It makes it easier to satisfy the asker, and easy is easily mistaken for right. Test the difference. If the asker's request added weight, then driving the person in visibly greater need would be choosing the less-supported option, and would call for justification on the merits — I would owe the asker not just an answer but a *defence*. I do not think I do. "Sorry, I'm taking her — she's further and it's raining" closes the matter completely, and the asker who replied "but I *asked*" would be making a mistake about what asking does.

**Withdrawal.** A asks, then says "actually, please don't." If asking adds no weight, withdrawing should subtract none; yet withdrawal can make φ-ing positively objectionable.

This conflates two different acts. "Please don't" is not the cancellation of a request; it is a second request, with the opposite content. Of course it changes things: it puts *not*-φ-ing on my agenda and makes me answerable for φ-ing anyway. Consider instead a genuine retraction — "never mind, forget I asked." Now ask whether my reasons revert to what my information supports. I say they do, and the residue is exactly what the account predicts: I am *released from answerability*. If I read the draft anyway, that is at worst mildly officious, not a wrong. The asymmetry between "forget I asked" and "please don't" is itself evidence for the view: retraction removes a liability, counter-request creates one, and neither operation is the addition or subtraction of a weight.

**Content-creating requests.** "Please don't tell anyone." Telling afterwards is a betrayal. Telling when I had merely surmised she would prefer silence is thoughtlessness. That looks like a difference in the gravity of a wrong, not merely in whether I owe an account.

My earlier draft replied that such cases are consent rather than request. That reply was borrowed rather than argued, and I think a better one is available — one which turns the case into support for the account.

When A says "please don't tell anyone," and I say nothing and let her go on speaking, something has happened beyond A's asking. A has relied on me, and has *reasonably* relied, because I did not demur. My silence has functioned as acceptance. And now the source of the extra gravity is visible: it is the undertaking, not the asking. Test it. Suppose I say, "I'm not promising anything — I'm a terrible gossip," and A tells me regardless. If I later tell, I have been indiscreet; I have not betrayed her. The gravity tracks uptake, not address.

This matters for the shape of the theory in two ways. First, T1 says that the *act of asking* adds no weight. It does not say that nothing in the vicinity of asking ever adds weight: requests that are accepted create commitments, and commitments are weighty. Refusal is cheap precisely because acceptance is what creates the obligation. Second — and this is the part I did not see before — the Agenda Thesis explains why tacit acceptance is possible at all. Silence can count as acceptance only where silence is not neutral. Because a request makes the addressee answerable, saying nothing is already a move: it is the visible non-exercise of an available objection. On a weight model, silence in the face of a request is no more significant than silence in the face of a penny on the pavement, and the ubiquity of tacit undertakings is left a mystery. So the second dimension is not merely compatible with the gravity of confidences; it is what makes such confidences mechanically possible. Requests are, on this account, the ordinary gateway to obligation: they open a question whose closure by acceptance creates a commitment.

I do not claim that these three replies settle T1. It is a universal negative, defended case by case, and one clear counterexample would defeat it. I claim only that the three hardest cases I have been able to construct come out right, and that in each of them the apparent weight of the asking turns out on inspection to belong to something else: to information, to transaction cost, to a second address, or to an undertaking.

## 8. Address without a claim

How do we tell the Agenda Thesis apart from the epistemic account, which says that whatever requests do, they do by revealing information?

The obvious test — compare being asked with overhearing the same need — does not work, and I have abandoned it. Overhearing is confounded twice over. It varies common knowledge rather than mere information, and higher-order information is still information, so the epistemic theorist gets the asymmetry for free. And it introduces independent norms against acting on, or revealing, what one was not meant to hear.

A cleaner instrument is already in the literature. Raz observes that requesting is not the communication of information, since "[i]t is possible ... to tell a person that, while I would like him to do something for me, I am not asking and am not going to ask him to do it"; Schaber's version has John telling Jill, "I want you to read my draft (your comments are always so helpful) but I am not going to ask you."[^9]

> **The Disclaimed-Request Test.** Case (a): A says to S, "Could you read my draft this weekend?" Case (b): A says to S, "I'd love you to read my draft this weekend — your comments are always so helpful — but I'm not asking you to, and I'm not going to."

Here there is full information, full common knowledge, a genuine addressee, no eavesdropping, and no difference in what S knows about A's need, A's wishes, or A's view of the relationship. Raz makes the point that requesting is intelligible precisely under such conditions: it "explains why one might request even when one knows that the other person knows of one's need and of one's desire for help."

What differs? Not, I think, whether S must acknowledge that A spoke. In both cases S must say *something*; the norms of conversational uptake apply to any address whatever, which is exactly why they cannot be what distinguishes the two. The difference is this. In (a), S must *close the question*: help, or decline. "I'll think about it" is a permissible deferral, but it is a deferral, and leaving the matter permanently open is itself a failure. In (b), S need not open the question at all. "That's kind of you to say" is a complete response. S may decide silently never to read the draft, may never mention it again, and has wronged A in no way — though she may of course be criticisable for stinginess, which is a different charge, made on different grounds.

So the asymmetry is not informational, and it is not a matter of conversational politeness. It is a difference in whether S is *answerable to A on a question*. That is what address-with-a-claim does and mere address does not.

The epistemic account has a ready reply, and it should be met head on, because its author has already made it. Weltman writes that "when I request something, and someone acts on my request, I am to some extent responsible for this, whereas when someone acts on a wish, I am not," and calls this "a big difference." A footnote then brackets it: "It is also a normatively important difference, but not in the sense of the normativity we are interested in when we ask why requests are normative. Similarly, if I accidentally reveal what cards I am holding in a game of bridge, I am less culpable than if I intentionally do so, but in each case the cards I reveal give my opponents identical reasons to play certain cards in order to win."[^10]

This is the most important objection to the essay, and three things must be said about it.

First, on the question Weltman poses, he is right and I agree with him: the request does not alter how strongly S is favoured toward φ-ing. That is T1. Our disagreement is about whether that question exhausts the subject.

Second, the bracket is a definition of the topic, not a finding about requests. "The normativity we are interested in" is stipulated as the addressee's reasons to φ — which is precisely the axis I claim is the wrong one. A question counts as one of practical normativity if it concerns what an agent is required to do and is criticisable for failing to do. The duty to answer is a duty to perform an action; failing to discharge it is a practical failure; and it wrongs a specific person, who has a standing to complain that nobody else has. If that falls outside practical normativity, it is hard to see what could fall inside it except weights, which is the conclusion under dispute.

Third, the bridge analogy misdescribes the structure. In the bridge case, the difference in culpability attaches to *me*, the revealer; my opponents' reasons are untouched, and nothing whatever is required of them. That is why the case supports the bracket. But the request case is not analogous. The liability created by a request falls on the *addressee*: it is S, not A, who must now do something she need not otherwise have done, and who is criticisable if she does not. A theory may bracket the speaker's responsibility for the hearer's conduct and still owe an account of a requirement that falls on the hearer. Weltman's analogy shows that some differences between requests and wishes are normatively inert for the addressee. It does not touch the difference I have identified, because that difference is not on the speaker's side of the ledger.

## 9. Where this belongs among second-order reasons

My earlier draft claimed that Raz's definition of second-order reasons names a genus with two species, that he developed only the negative one, and that the positive slot was unoccupied and unnamed. The second claim is right; the third is wrong, and correcting it improves the view.

Raz's definition, as quoted by Christian Piller, is this: "A second-order reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason."[^11] The positive species is neither nameless nor empty. It is standardly called a *positive second-order reason*: Daniel Whiting, attacking the whole apparatus, frames the target as the idea "that there are second-order normative reasons (not) to φ for or on the basis of certain first-order normative reasons," and Kenta Sekine has recently defended the category and offered a fourfold typology of positive second-order reasons, sorted by whether the further value is contingent or constitutive and whether it coincides with the basic one, into which he locates the moral-worth literature.[^12] So the slot is furnished.

What survives, and is better, is this. Look at the Agenda Thesis's clauses against Raz's definition.

Clause (ii) — S must fail to φ only for a reason she can offer as her own — *is* a second-order reason in Raz's sense. It constrains the reasons for which S may act, and it is positive: it requires that S's not-φ-ing be done *for* a reason of a certain kind. It belongs in the literature just mentioned.

Clause (i) — S must take up the question — is *not*. Considering is not acting for a reason. A requirement to put a question on the agenda is a requirement on *deliberation*, on what gets weighed, not on the reasons for which one acts. Raz's disjunctive definition does not reach it, in either direction.

I therefore drop the label "inclusionary reason" that I earlier proposed, and with it the tidy symmetry it suggested. The better claim is less tidy and more interesting: requests reveal a category of *agenda reasons* — reasons bearing on deliberative uptake — which the standard taxonomy of first-order and second-order reasons does not contain.

That the category is missing rather than merely undeveloped can be seen inside Raz's own motivating case. Piller's exposition of the tired investor is that the agent's refusal "is based on a reason not to consider the merits of the offer" — a reason bearing on uptake, which is then classified as a reason not to *act for* the excluded considerations. The two are run together at the point of origin. And requests are the place where the received Razian picture explicitly finds nothing to say. Michael Moore, surveying the phenomena Raz took exclusionary reasons to illuminate, observes that threats and requests are illuminated by the *absence* of such reasons: "unlike a promise, say, or an order issued by one in authority, a mere request does not preclude one from weighing the pros and cons of doing the action requested."[^13] That is right, and it is exactly half the story. A request excludes nothing. It also does something second-order, on an axis the framework does not have.

Two further acknowledgements are owed. The coherence of second-order reasons is under live attack; Whiting argues that the very idea is incoherent, and the challenge has generated a substantial literature. My account is not hostage to that dispute, and this is a virtue rather than an evasion. Clause (i), which does most of the work, is not a reason to act for a reason at all; it is a requirement of deliberative uptake, and it survives untouched if Whiting is right. Only clause (ii) would need reformulating, and it could be reformulated as a condition on what counts as discharging the burden rather than as a second-order reason. And the word "answerability" is a term of art in the theory of moral responsibility, where it names one member of a trichotomy alongside attributability and accountability. I use it in a narrower and more mundane sense — a standing liability to be called to account by a particular person on a particular question — and intend no commitment to that debate. The nearest relative of my positive thesis is Stephen Darwall's claim that authority requires answerability and that second-personal address is sui generis; I have not been able to read either him or Raz's reply, and I record the debt as unpaid rather than discharged.[^14]

Finally, Gläser. If the reports are right that he denies that requests create additional reasons and holds that they put the requestee under an obligation, then he and I agree on the negative claim and differ on the positive one. The difference is testable against D1. An obligation to φ predicts that non-compliance is blameworthy; the whole force of Lance and Kukla's observation, and of the Lewis–Schaber category of non-obligatory reasons, is that it is not. What is obligatory, on my account, is not φ-ing but taking the question up and standing behind the answer. Whether Gläser would say something similar I do not know; I have been unable to obtain his paper, and I flag this as the point at which an informed reader should press hardest.

## 10. Extensions, and a moral

If agenda reasons exist, requests will not be their only source.

**Questions.** Reid put asking a question and asking a favour on the same list, and rightly. A question creates an obligation to answer that is cheap to discharge ("I don't know"; "I'd rather not say") and, where the asker has standing, costly to ignore. Refusing to answer is permissible; failing to register that one was asked is not. The standing apparatus is needed here, and it does the expected work: rhetorical questions, hostile questions and a stranger's intrusive questions may be ignored precisely because the asker lacks title to an answer.

**Procedural rights.** Legal standing, the right to be heard, the right to petition: these are powers to put a claim on an institution's agenda, not powers to win. It is a familiar puzzle why a hearing one was always going to lose is nonetheless something one was owed. The two-dimensional picture offers a natural answer — the right to be heard is an agenda power, and being answerable is a distinct status from being obliged to decide in one's favour. I offer this as a puzzle the picture is well placed to address, not as a verdict on a literature I have not read.

**Protest.** Much political speech is not an attempt to shift anyone's balance of reasons; the reasons are usually there already and known. It is an attempt to force a question onto an agenda and to make someone answerable for how they respond. The characteristic complaint of the unheard is not "you weighed us too lightly" but "you did not even consider us" — unintelligible if weight is the only normative dimension, and straightforward if it is not.

Reid noticed that "[i]n all languages the second person of verbs, the pronoun of the second person, and the vocative case in nouns, are appropriated to the expression of social operations of mind." Languages reserve a grammatical form for addressing somebody. Schaber, from the other end of the tradition, begins by saying that "[r]equests are acts of second-personal address." A theory of practical reason that recognises only weights has no work for that form to do, because on such a theory being addressed is merely an efficient way of being informed.

So here is the conclusion. Asking adds nothing to the scales. It puts a question on your agenda and makes you answerable for what becomes of it — and this is not a deflationary result but a redirection, since the power to conscript another person's attention and to make their silence culpable is among the most consequential powers we have over one another. The cost of the redirection is a symmetry I would have liked to claim and cannot: the requirement a request creates is not the mirror image of a Razian exclusionary reason, but something for which the standard taxonomy has no slot. Reid thought the social operations of mind had been thrown behind the curtain. So, I think, have the social operations of practical reason.
