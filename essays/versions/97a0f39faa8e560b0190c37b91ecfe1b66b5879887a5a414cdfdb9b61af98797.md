# Being Able to Stop

A skilled carpenter can sand a wooden panel while thinking about a conversation. Her hands adjust to the surface without instructions about each movement. If asked to stop, she can stop at once. Is that enough to show that she controls whether the sanding continues?

It depends on what could bring the need to stop to her attention. Suppose a mark on the panel indicates how much wood may be removed. She has become absorbed in her thoughts and will not notice reaching it. She remains perfectly able to stop when someone speaks. Yet that ability leaves a gap: nothing in her present way of working connects the approaching limit to a decision to stop.

This gap matters even if the panel turns out well. Perhaps a colleague, whom she does not know is watching, will always warn her in time. The panel is safe. But we should hesitate before crediting her with the same control as a carpenter who has arranged for a colleague to watch the mark and signal her.

I will argue that control over the continuation of a routine requires more than the ability to interrupt it after a suitable warning. There must also be a dependable way for relevant changes to affect what the person does. When that way runs through another person or a device, its reliability alone is not enough to make it the performer's control. The help must be part of how the performer pursues a plan that she can change. This claim concerns the control of an ongoing task. It neither denies that unattended movements are actions nor makes every missed warning a moral fault.

## What the ability to stop leaves open

A habit is a way of acting that tends to recur in familiar circumstances. A skill is an ability to perform an activity well. The same activity can involve both: the carpenter habitually starts sanding from one end, and she has the skill to keep the surface even. Habits need not be skilled, and skills need not be exercised habitually. My subject is their familiar overlap, where practiced work proceeds with little deliberate attention to its details.

There are at least two questions about the carpenter's control. Can she adjust the pressure and direction of the sanding? Can she keep sanding only while that activity still serves her plan for the panel? She may answer the first question through her hands while failing at the second. Calling her simply controlled or uncontrolled hides this difference.

Fridland gives an important reason not to equate automatic movement with absence of control. Her account separates control of strategy, attention, and movement. Attention can respond to a performer's aims without being consciously directed at each feature of the situation. The point of practice includes learning what to notice, as well as learning how to move (Fridland 2014, §§4.1–4.3). Nothing in my argument requires a person to speak silent instructions to herself throughout a skilled performance.

The problem arises when an explanation appeals only to what would happen *if* she noticed a reason to stop. That conditional can be true even when noticing has become practically unavailable. A disconnected fire alarm could still make a person leave if it sounded. Its capacity to produce that response does not show that it currently protects the person from fire. In the same way, an available stopping response does not by itself show control over when a routine ends.

The analogy does not turn people into alarm systems. It identifies a missing part of an explanation. A claim about the response to a warning leaves unanswered how the warning can arrive. If the explanation concerns the person's control over continuation, both parts matter.

Nor must she detect every possible reason for stopping. The carpenter cannot be expected to sense an invisible flaw in the wood. Control is always limited to a range of circumstances. Here the relevant range includes the ordinary stopping point marked for this very job. It is not selected merely because a bad result eventually happened. We identify it from the task and its normal variations before seeing the outcome.

The first conclusion is therefore modest but firm. Where ending an activity at the right point is part of the task, control of that task cannot be established by testing only the person's response to an ideal warning. We must also examine the available path from the stopping condition to that response.

## Two ways to receive the same warning

Now consider two versions of the carpenter's workshop. In both, she works with the same skill. Her colleague sees the same marks and gives the same accurate warnings. Neither carpenter has to pay continual attention to the limit.

In the first workshop, the carpenter asks her colleague to watch the mark. They agree that a tap means to pause. She leaves herself able to feel the tap and pauses when it comes. If she decides that the panel needs a different stopping point, she tells her colleague. If the colleague leaves, she resumes watching the mark herself.

In the second workshop, the colleague secretly watches because she is worried about damage to the panel. The carpenter has no expectation of help. If she wanted a different stopping point, she would have no reason to communicate it. The colleague would continue protecting the old limit. The warning, when it arrives, may give the carpenter an entirely good reason to stop. But before it arrives, the carpenter is not using this arrangement to govern the sanding.

The difference is not the probability of a ruined panel under the existing plan. We can stipulate that both colleagues are equally dependable. It is a difference in whose guidance explains the protection. In the first case the warning helps carry out the carpenter's plan. In the second it is an intervention by someone else, which the carpenter may then take up.

A useful test is to change the plan while leaving the movements and warning equipment in place. Suppose the carpenter now wants to remove a little more material. In the first workshop, her change can alter what her colleague watches for. In the second, the hidden protection continues to serve a purpose she no longer has. This test shows why dependable help and the person's control can come apart. Reliability at one fixed result does not establish responsiveness to the person whose control is in question.

The second carpenter is not a puppet. She intentionally sands, controls her movements, and may intentionally stop after hearing the warning. What she lacks is control of the whole interval *through that hidden arrangement*. Someone else's well-timed assistance need not erase her agency. It also need not enlarge her agency retrospectively to include work she never directed or relied on.

This qualification is crucial. The issue is not whether the final stop belongs to her. Once she hears, understands, and responds to the warning, it can. The issue is whether her ability to respond gave her continuing control before the warning arrived. The secret colleague cannot automatically supply the missing answer on her behalf.

## When help becomes a way of controlling

The examples suggest three requirements for outside help to preserve the performer's control over continuation.

First, the help must be tied to the task. It must bring an appropriate range of relevant changes to bear on the work, with enough time for a response. A timer that always rings after ten minutes does not monitor a sanding limit that varies unpredictably with the wood. It may be useful, but it does not provide the missing control in this case.

Second, the performer must be using the help. This need not mean that she designed it, remembers choosing it, or can describe how it works. A carpenter can learn to work with a colleague's signals as part of ordinary training. A person can also knowingly rely on a standard workshop procedure installed by others. What matters is that her conduct fits reliance on that help: she remains reachable, treats its signals as bearing on her work, and adjusts her conduct when she learns that the help is absent. A hidden intervention lacks this connection before it is taken up.

Third, the arrangement must leave room for the performer's plan to change. The helper need not accept every request or have the same technical abilities as the carpenter. The carpenter must, however, have some effective way to revise the instruction, suspend the arrangement, or stop relying on it when its limits cease to fit the task. A colleague can say, “I cannot judge the new limit.” If the carpenter can then take over or pause, the arrangement remains usable. Control does not require unlimited command over another person.

These requirements address different failures. An accurate but unknown helper meets the first and fails the second. A familiar warning that comes too late meets the second and fails the first. A trusted mechanism that keeps enforcing an obsolete plan, while preventing the performer from leaving or changing course, fails the third. Merely saying that a person has adopted a device cannot make each of its later interventions an exercise of her current control.

Together, the requirements explain a limited sense in which monitoring can be shared without the performer surrendering control. They do not establish that every part of the helper's activity becomes the performer's action. The colleague still watches and judges for herself. The carpenter controls her task by working through that contribution, much as she can control a tool without personally producing every process inside it. Shared dependence does not require exclusive authorship of every contribution.

This account also avoids treating independence as an ideal built into control. A person who needs a warning device can control a task just as fully, over the relevant range, as someone who notices the stopping point unaided. The difference in where the information is detected does not settle whose practical activity it serves.

## Three objections

One objection says that the distinction is merely about knowledge. Both workshops contain a reliable chain from the mark to the carpenter's stopping. Why demand that she know about the chain?

Knowledge by itself is not the demand. Suppose someone tells the second carpenter that the colleague watches, but the carpenter does not understand the signals and carries on as before. Information has been added without making the help part of her performance. Conversely, a well-trained pair can use their signals without recalling any agreement. The relevant relation is practical: the performer pursues her task through the help, and changes in her plan can change how she uses it. Awareness often enables that relation, but a description in her head cannot replace it.

The objection is right that there may be no difference in immediate success. That is why success cannot settle the issue. Imagine that the hidden colleague would prevent sanding whenever she herself disliked the result, regardless of the carpenter's revised purpose. A reliable causal chain still exists, but it now plainly belongs to someone else's project. The ordinary hidden-helper case need not be coercive. The more intrusive case simply makes visible a distinction already present: serving the current outcome and being available to the performer's changing direction are different properties.

A second objection worries about a regress. If control requires a way to notice that sanding should stop, must another system notice when that noticing system fails, and another system watch that one?

No further watcher is required merely because a reliable arrangement can fail. The account does not require certainty, or require the performer to certify her own control continuously. It requires a working connection across the relevant range of situations. An ordinary trained sensitivity to a mark can provide it. So can a well-established exchange of signals. At some point the explanation reaches capacities that work without being deliberately supervised. Automatic attention is particularly important here.

A broken signal may nevertheless reveal that an additional check is needed in a particular setting. This is a practical question about the range of failures the task requires one to handle, not a logical demand for an endless chain. If every available arrangement is too unreliable for that range, the right conclusion is that the person lacks the specified control. We should not invent a final perfect watcher merely to guarantee that everyone has it.

Schwind's account of negligence also takes seriously the role of advance arrangements. He argues that duties can concern the maintenance of conditions through which existing knowledge becomes available, and that those duties may be met by external safeguards (Schwind 2026, §§4.1–4.3). My question is different from whether a precaution is owed. An arrangement can protect a person without being something she controls through; and she can control through an arrangement even where failing to use it would have been morally harmless. The two issues should not be inferred from each other.

A third objection says that the account excludes ordinary habits. People often stroll, wash dishes, or hum without a plan for when to stop. Must they arrange monitoring before their conduct can count as action?

They need not. The claim is about control over continuation relative to a task that has conditions for continuing. A person can intentionally hum without intentionally keeping the humming within any particular time limit. Failing to monitor the clock therefore shows no failure to control such a limit. If she undertakes to hum until a recording reaches its end, the question changes. There is now a condition that her way of performing must be able to register if she is to control that aspect of the activity.

This does not allow us to protect every control claim by endlessly narrowing the task. The carpenter's original undertaking included preserving the marked thickness. When she loses track of the mark, we cannot quietly redescribe the task as moving sandpaper and declare that nothing was lost. We can credit her remaining control of those movements while acknowledging the loss of control over the job she undertook.

## The skill that remains

This last point helps explain a common disagreement about habitual error. One observer says that the carpenter is still highly skilled; another says that she has lost control. Both can be right. She may retain her ability to sand smoothly while no longer governing the process toward the intended stopping point.

Restoring a warning path can then restore control of this performance without improving her sanding technique. It can even do so without teaching her a new perceptual skill. This is why an account of the individual's stored abilities is not yet a complete account of her control on an occasion. The setting can make an ability usable, isolate it from relevant information, or connect it to help that the performer can employ.

There is also a reason not to assume that smoother work means more control. Suppose two routines are equally accurate before their stopping point. One permits a clear pause and lets the performer change the plan. The other is faster, but its rhythm makes ordinary warnings practically ineffective. The second routine may display greater fluency and less control over continuation. The loss is not explained by a lack of technical skill. It is explained by what can still reach the performer and alter the course of the task.

The practical lesson follows from the argument rather than replacing it: when judging habitual performance, ask what the person can notice and use, not only what she can do after being given perfect information. Ask also whether outside help remains available to her changing purposes. A routine can leave the hands free to work precisely while leaving the person unable to redirect the task at the point that matters. Being able to stop is one part of controlling it. Having a usable way for the need to stop to make a difference is another.
