# Off the Map: Unawareness, Obligation, and the Duties That Survive It

**Abstract.** Work on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility is built on an
assumption it has never isolated: that whatever an agent fails to be aware of is a
proposition she was in a position to entertain. Formal epistemology recognises a second
kind of failure — *unawareness*, the absence of an item from the agent's algebra
altogether — which is not a doxastic deficiency at all. I argue (i) that a principle I call
**Ought Implies Representability** governs the deliberative 'ought' and is genuinely
distinct from *ought implies can*; (ii) that this generates a severe sceptical problem at
exactly the frontier where the gravest novel harms occur; and (iii) that the problem is
solved not by weakening the principle but by observing that options are individuated more
finely than requirements. Unawareness of an option almost never dissolves obligation; it
*coarsens* it, relocating the binding requirement to a description the agent can represent.
Because the only requirements that can bind a genuinely unaware agent are *de dicto* —
precautionary, procedural, deferential — frontier ethics must be institutional rather than
substantive. A residue of cases remains in which nothing binds; I argue that this residue is
real, small, and correctly classified as tragedy rather than fault.

---

## 1. Two failures

Nadia is a hospital pharmacist. Consider two ways her work can go wrong.

**Sign.** She reconstitutes a drug in the usual way. The vial carries a label: *do not warm
above 25°C*. She does not read it, warms the solution, and a patient is harmed. Nadia did
not know that warming was dangerous. But the thought *this might need to be kept cool* was
available to her; the evidence was in her hand; had she raised the question she would have
answered it correctly.

**Tubing.** In a different decade she prepares infusions using the standard giving set.
Years later it emerges that a plasticizer in the tubing leaches into warmed solutions and
harms neonates. At the time nobody — not Nadia, not the manufacturer, not the regulator,
not the toxicologist — entertains the thought that tubing might contribute anything to what
passes through it. *Use a glass-lined line* is not an option Nadia considered and rejected,
nor one she failed to consider through carelessness. It figures nowhere in the space over
which she deliberates. Her reasoning is impeccable given her repertoire. The repertoire is
the problem.

English describes both by saying Nadia was "unaware" of something, and the philosophical
literature on the epistemic condition for moral responsibility follows English. That
literature is large, sophisticated, and — I shall argue — almost entirely about cases of the
first kind. Its central puzzle is the regress generated by tracing: if blameworthy ignorant
wrongdoing requires culpable ignorance, and culpable ignorance requires a culpable
"benighting act", and culpability for that act requires awareness in turn, then, as Rosen
puts it, "the only possible locus of original responsibility is an akratic act" (Rosen 2004:
307, quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). The responses to this regress — weakened
internalism, epistemic-vice views, quality-of-will views, capacitarianism — disagree about
almost everything except the shape of the failure they are theorising.

My claim is that *Tubing* is a different shape, that the difference is structural rather than
one of degree, and that seeing it changes what we should say about obligation, not merely
about blame.

## 2. The Propositional Assumption

Start with how the literature defines its terms. As Yates's survey records, "recent theorists
of the epistemic condition take true belief to be necessary and sufficient for awareness, and
they identify ignorance as the lack of true belief, the opposite of awareness (Peels 2010;
Rosen 2008; Rudy-Hiller 2017)" (Yates n.d., §1). Awareness is thus *defined* as a doxastic
state, and unawareness as its complement within the space of doxastic states. Call this:

> **The Propositional Assumption (PA).** For any relevant content *p*, the agent either
> believes *p*, disbelieves *p*, or withholds; unawareness of *p* is one of the latter two.

PA is not an idle stipulation. It is load-bearing in every position on the map.

*Volitionists* require an occurrent, *de dicto* belief that the act is wrong; the agent who
lacks it is ignorant, and ignorance is culpable only if it traces to an act performed "in full
awareness" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §2). Both the missing belief and the benighting act are
specified by contents the agent could have entertained.

*Weakened internalists* substitute dispositional for occurrent belief. A dispositional belief
is one "disposed to be occurrent under certain conditions" (Yates n.d., §1) — which
presupposes that the content is in the repertoire, waiting.

*Epistemic-vice* theorists locate culpability in overconfidence, dogmatism, incuriosity and
the rest, on the ground that these explain a failure to respond to challenges the agent
encountered. A challenge one cannot formulate is not a challenge one dismisses.

*Quality-of-will* theorists hold that an act can express contempt for another's standing
without any *de dicto* moral belief. But the expression runs through the agent's take on
what she is doing; Talbert and Harman accordingly retain a *de re* awareness requirement,
and on Angela Smith's answerability view, as Yates summarises it, unwitting omissions are
culpable because they "express objectionable evaluative attitudes" (Yates n.d., §2.c.i). An
attitude is expressed toward something the agent's mind has hold of.

*Capacitarians* come closest to breaking free, since they explicitly deny that awareness is
required. But their disjunctive condition is that agents "should and could be aware of
[relevant considerations] given the available evidence, the opportunity to adequately
process it, and their cognitive capacities" (Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.4, reporting Sher 2009:
87). Evidence, here, is evidence *for* something. A body of physical signals counts as
available evidence for an agent only relative to a set of hypotheses she can raise; the
plasticizer studies of the 1990s are not evidence available to Nadia in the 1970s in any
sense that could ground a should-have-known claim, even though the molecules were
there.

Notice, finally, that the requirement most obviously relevant to *Tubing* — what
Rudy-Hiller calls "awareness of alternatives" — is formulated by Levy as the demand that
agents have "epistemic access to a range of alternatives", such that "they can only
appropriately be blamed for performing an action if they believed that alternatives were
available to them, and understood the significance of these alternatives" (Levy 2011: 111,
quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022, §1.1). This is a belief condition on an alternative already in
view. It says nothing about alternatives that are not in view at all.

PA is therefore not a thesis anyone defends. It is a frame, and *Tubing* falls outside it.

## 3. Unawareness, formally

The distinction I need is standard elsewhere. In the logic of general awareness of Fagin and
Halpern, awareness is a syntactic operator independent of the knowledge operator. As
Halpern and Rêgo summarise it: "implicit knowledge is the standard (S5) notion of
knowledge; explicit knowledge amounts to implicit knowledge and awareness" (Halpern and
Rêgo 2006: 2). Semantically, *A<sub>i</sub>φ* holds at a state just in case φ belongs to the
set of formulas the agent is aware of there, and explicit knowledge *X<sub>i</sub>φ* is
defined as the conjunction *K<sub>i</sub>φ ∧ A<sub>i</sub>φ* (2006: §2).

The philosophical payoff of this architecture is that awareness and belief are orthogonal.
An agent can stand in the knowledge relation to a content — it can be true at every state she
considers possible — while failing to be aware of it. Unawareness is not a false belief, a
missing belief, or a suspension. It is not a position in doxastic space; it is absence from the
space. That is precisely why PA cannot accommodate it: PA's trichotomy has no cell for it.

Two further features of unawareness do the work in what follows.

> **Opacity.** *De re* self-knowledge of one's own unawareness is unavailable. Where
> awareness is generated by primitive propositions — the standard restriction, satisfied by
> the models of Modica and Rustichini, Dekel, Lipman and Rustichini, and Heifetz, Meier
> and Schipper — "it is impossible for an agent to know that he is unaware of a specific
> fact" (Halpern and Rêgo 2006: 2).[^1]

> **Transparency.** *De dicto* self-knowledge of one's own unawareness is available. On
> Halpern and Rêgo's quantified extension, *K<sub>i</sub>(∃x(A<sub>j</sub>x ∧
> ¬A<sub>i</sub>x))* "is consistent and … can be true at state *s* even though there might be
> no formula ψ … such that *K<sub>i</sub>((A<sub>j</sub>ψ ∧ ¬A<sub>i</sub>ψ))*" (2006: §3).

Their illustration is exact for my purposes: a physician referring a patient to an oncologist
"knows that an oncologist is aware of things that could help the patient's treatment of which
he is not aware", yet "is unlikely to know which specific thing he is unaware of … (if he
knew which one it was, he would not be unaware of it!)" (2006: 2).

Opacity and Transparency are compatible, and their joint truth is the hinge of this essay.
An agent can know, with full justification, that her repertoire is incomplete, while being
constitutively unable to say where. She is not in the position of someone who knows there
is a sign she has not read. She is in the position of someone who knows only that there are
kinds of sign.

## 4. Ought implies representability

I now state the normative principle.

> **Ought Implies Representability (OIR).** If *S* ought to φ, then φ is representable in
> *S*'s option space at the time of deliberation.

Three clarifications. First, "ought" here is the *deliberative* ought — the one that issues
requirements to an agent, supports the demand that she comply, and grounds accountability
when she does not. It is not the objective or fact-relative ought that ranks acts by their
actual upshots, which is not addressed to anyone and is not subject to OIR.[^2] Second,
"representable" does not mean "consciously entertained". It means available to be
entertained: within the agent's conceptual and combinatorial reach, so that the question
*shall I φ?* could arise for her. Third, OIR is a constraint on requirement, not on
evaluation. *Tubing* is a bad outcome; the glass-lined line would have been better; none of
that is in dispute.

The case for OIR is that a requirement which fails it cannot play the role requirements
play. A requirement is something that can, in principle, be put to the agent; that she can
comply with *as such*; and whose violation licenses the demand *why did you not?* An
unrepresentable requirement fails all three. It cannot be put to her, since putting it to her
would consist in making her aware of it, which by hypothesis has not happened. She
cannot comply with it as such, since compliance would be accidental. And the demand
*why did you not use a glass-lined line?* has, in 1975, no answer that is not a report of the
state of the art — which is to say, no answer that is about her.

**OIR is not** *ought implies can*. This is the crux, so let me be blunt about it. In *Tubing*,
Nadia *can* use a glass-lined line. She has the hands, the training and the opportunity;
suitable glassware is in the cupboard. Whatever ability-plus-opportunity analysis of "can"
one favours, the antecedent of OIC is satisfied and OIC therefore imposes no bar. What
Nadia lacks is not the ability to perform the act but the ability to *pose* it. OIR is thus an
additional constraint, satisfied and violated independently.

One could resist by reading "can" in OIC as *can do intentionally, under the relevant
description, as the upshot of deliberation*. I grant that this reading, if adopted, would cover
the case. But then the point stands in a different form: that reading has not been isolated in
the literature on the epistemic condition, it is not the reading that figures in debates about
determinism and alternate possibilities, and its consequences — the ones traced in §§5–7 —
have not been drawn. Whether OIR is a new principle or an undistinguished reading of an
old one, the work it does is not being done.

**OIR is not Levy's rationality condition either.** Levy holds that blame requires that
conforming to the standard be "something they can do rationally (and not merely by chance
or accident)", where the relevant rationality is "thoroughly internalist", concerning "what I
can decide to do as the result of engaging in reasoning" (Levy 2009: 735, quoted in
Rudy-Hiller 2022, §3.2). That condition concerns *normative* access: whether the agent can
see the reason. Mr Potter, Levy's target case, can represent perfectly well the option of
revising his business practices; what he cannot do is see why he should. Nadia's situation is
the mirror image. She can see the reason — *do not poison infants* is fully intelligible to her,
and she is moved by it — but the option that answers to it is not in her space. Normative
access without representational access, and representational access without normative
access, are different failures, and they call for different treatments.

## 5. The Frontier Problem

OIR looks harmless until it is combined with Opacity and with a claim about frequency.

In the philosophy of science, Kyle Stanford has argued for what he calls a New Induction:
that "we have, throughout the history of scientific inquiry and in virtually every scientific
field, repeatedly occupied an epistemic position in which we could conceive of only one or a
few theories that were well confirmed by the available evidence, while subsequent inquiry
would routinely (if not invariably) reveal further, radically distinct alternatives as well
confirmed by the previously available evidence as those we were inclined to accept"
(Stanford 2006: 19, quoted in Stanford 2023, §3.3). Whatever one makes of the argument
about theories, its premise about *practice* is hard to deny, and the practical analogue is
stronger: the history of technique is a history of options that turned out to have been there
all along, unconceived.

Put the three together:

1. (OIR) Unrepresentable acts are not required.
2. (Opacity) An agent is never in a position to identify a specific gap in her repertoire.
3. (New Induction, practical form) Repertoire gaps are normal rather than exotic, and they
   are densest where technique is newest.

The conclusion — call it **the Frontier Problem** — is that obligation systematically lapses
at the frontier, that it lapses invisibly, and that it lapses in inverse proportion to the
novelty and hence the scale of possible harm. This is worse than the volitionist regress. The
regress threatens to make blame rare; the Frontier Problem threatens to make *requirement*
absent, and absent in exactly the region where we most want to say something.

It is also worse than constitutive moral luck. Nelkin defines constitutive luck as "luck in
who one is, or in the traits and dispositions that one has" (Nelkin 2025, §1), and the
familiar reply is that traits are not opaque to assessment: a coward's cowardice shows up in
what he wills. Repertoire luck resists that reply. There is no will directed at an option one
does not have. Nothing about Nadia's concern for her patients is expressed, one way or the
other, by the absence of glass-lined lines from her deliberation.

## 6. The grain of obligation

The Frontier Problem is generated by a tacit equation of two things that ought to be kept
apart: the individuation of options and the individuation of requirements. Once separated,
the problem shrinks dramatically.

Options are fine-grained. They are specific act-types, indexed to technique: *use a
glass-lined line*, *stage the rollout in three cohorts*, *chelate the effluent*. Requirements
need not be. The content of a requirement can be an outcome constraint, a policy over an
unspecified space, or a second-order condition on how one proceeds. Nothing in the concept
of a requirement demands that its content be an available act-type; what OIR demands is
only that the *content of the requirement* be representable, not that every means of
complying with it be.

> **Coarsening.** If *S* is unaware of option *o*, *S* may nevertheless be bound by a
> requirement *R* such that (a) *R* is representable for *S*, and (b) *o* would have been a
> means of complying with *R*. Where such an *R* exists, unawareness of *o* does not
> excuse: what *S* violates is *R*.

Coarsening is the key move, and it needs care, because a coarse requirement that the agent
cannot knowingly satisfy is no better off than a fine one. There are three families that
survive the test, and it is worth seeing why each does.

**(i) Outcome constraints.** *Do not expose patients to avoidable toxic risk.* Representable
by Nadia, and intelligible to her. But does she *violate* it? Relative to her evidence, she does
not: nothing available to her marked the giving set as a source of risk. Relative to the facts,
she does. So outcome constraints locate the objective wrong — they explain why something
genuinely went wrong, and why recall, compensation and regret are in order — without
locating a deliberative failure. This is the right result, and it disposes of a tempting
overreach: coarse requirements do not, by themselves, restore accountability. They restore
*wrongness*.

**(ii) De dicto precautionary requirements.** These do restore accountability, and they are
licensed precisely by Transparency. *Given that you know there is something you do not
know, proceed in ways that tolerate being wrong.* Stage the introduction. Prefer reversible
interventions to irreversible ones. Instrument the outcome so that surprises surface.
Monitor for effects you have not hypothesised. Do not scale until the small case has run
long enough for the unknown to show itself.

The theoretical point is that these requirements have contents of the form *K<sub>i</sub>∃x
¬A<sub>i</sub>x* — I know there is something I am missing — and Transparency establishes
that such contents are representable without any specification of the missing item. There is
therefore no formal barrier to a duty of this shape, and no version of the Frontier Problem
touches it. The duty does not ask Nadia to conceive of plasticizer leaching. It asks her to act
as someone who knows she may be conceiving of less than there is.

**(iii) Awareness-transferring requirements.** *Route this decision through someone whose
repertoire is larger than yours.* Halpern and Rêgo's own example is of this form: the
physician who refers to the oncologist acts well precisely because he knows the oncologist is
aware of what he is not, without knowing what that is (2006: 2). Consultation, external
review, adversarial testing and mandatory second opinions are all ways of acting on
knowledge of one's own unawareness without converting it into knowledge of what one is
unaware of. They are representable, specifiable in advance, and auditable after the fact.

The upshot is a redescription of what we are doing when we say, after a disaster, *you
should have thought of that*. Taken literally, that reproach violates OIR and is unfair.
Taken as it is almost always meant — *you should not have proceeded in a way that made
everything depend on your having thought of everything* — it is a charge under (ii) or (iii),
and it is fair. Unawareness does not usually unbind. It shifts the description under which
one is bound, from the substantive to the procedural.

## 7. Objections

**"Unawareness is just very deep ignorance; your distinction is one of degree."**

There is a test that separates them: *evidence-responsiveness*. Ignorance is corrigible by
evidence. Hand the ignorant agent the relevant data and, absent further failings, the false or
missing belief is repaired. Unawareness is not corrigible by evidence, because evidence is
evidence for a hypothesis, and there is no hypothesis for it to bear on. What repairs
unawareness is not evidence but a change in the space: a new concept, a new technique, a
new distinction. That is a different kind of intervention with a different success profile, and
the difference is not one of degree. I do not deny that there are intermediate cases —
partial, inarticulate or merely nominal grasp of a possibility. A distinction can be robust
without being sharp everywhere.

**"Quality of will already handles it. Nadia's repertoire reflects her cares; blame the
repertoire."**

I accept a limited version of this. What one notices is shaped by what one attends to, and
attention is shaped by concern; some gaps in a repertoire really are the shadow of a
defective will, and I do not want a view on which the sloppy are excused by their own
sloppiness. But the limited version does not cover *Tubing*, and there is a test that shows
why:

> **The Shared-Gap Test.** If a repertoire gap is shared across agents of every evaluative
> orientation in the relevant community — including those whose concern for the affected
> parties is exemplary — then the gap is not an expression of any particular agent's will.

In *Sign*, the gap is not shared: attentive pharmacists read labels. In *Tubing*, it is shared
completely; the most conscientious toxicologist of the period has it too. Where the Shared-
Gap Test is passed, quality-of-will explanations have no differential to work with. At most
they deliver attributability — this is the sort of thing that happens to people like her — and
not accountability. That is a real distinction, and the accountability side is the side on which
demands and reproaches live.

**"OIR proves too much. It excuses the slaveholder who 'could not conceive' of abolition."**

This is the objection I take most seriously, and answering it is where Coarsening earns its
keep. The slaveholder is not unaware of the relevant *options*. *Do not beat this person*,
*do not sell this child*, *let these people go* are fully representable to her; they are not
technical novelties. What she may lack is the moral concept under which those options are
required. But that is ignorance, not unawareness, and it is handled by Coarsening in the
strongest way: there is a representable requirement — do not inflict this suffering on this
person — which she violates, and which she is in a position to comply with. The volitionist
worry that she cannot *see* the requirement as a requirement, and so is excused unless her
moral ignorance is itself culpable, is a serious worry about moral ignorance, on which OIR
takes no stand either way. My thesis does not extend the domain of excuse by one inch in
the interpersonal case. Its domain is technical and structural: gaps in the repertoire of
*means*, not gaps in the perception of *claims*. That asymmetry is not an ad hoc restriction;
it falls out of the fact that interpersonal requirements are typically statable in coarse terms
that any competent agent can represent, whereas technical requirements often cannot
be.[^3]

**"Precautionary duties are vacuous, or they counsel paralysis."**

They would be if they were substantive — *do not act under uncertainty* is both vacuous
and self-undermining, since inaction has unconceived consequences too. That is exactly why
the duties licensed by Transparency are procedural. They do not tell you what to do; they
constrain the *form* of doing it, and they are costed and defeasible like any other duty.
Staging a rollout has a price, paid in delay; the duty is to pay a proportionate price, not an
unlimited one. Nor are they vacuous: they generate determinate, checkable requirements
(instrument this, stage that, submit to review by these people) which are routinely
specifiable in advance and routinely violated.

**"Does Opacity not also undercut the precautionary duty? Nadia might be unaware that
she is in a domain where unknown effects are possible."**

Yes. This is the residue, and I do not think it can be argued away.

## 8. The residue, and why frontier ethics is institutional

Transparency says that *de dicto* knowledge of one's own unawareness is *possible*, not that
it is universal. An agent can be unaware that she is in the kind of situation in which
unawareness matters. Where that is so, (ii) and (iii) lapse along with everything else, and
nothing binds.

> **The Residue.** Unawareness fully excuses just where no representable requirement — not
> even a *de dicto* precautionary or deferential one — was available to the agent. Here there
> is a wrong without a wrongdoer: the objective ought is violated, no deliberative ought is,
> and no one is accountable.

I take this to be the correct verdict rather than a cost. It is what we in fact say about
genuinely first-of-a-kind harms, once the retrospective illusion of foresight fades: that
something terrible happened, that it was nobody's fault, and that this does not make it less
terrible. Refusing this verdict is what drives the two familiar distortions — hindsight
scapegoating, which manufactures a fault to match the harm, and blanket exculpation of
whole technical domains, which infers from *no one conceived of it* to *nothing was
required*. Both are errors, and they are errors of the same kind: they assume that the grain
of obligation must match the grain of the harm.

Note how narrow the Residue is. It requires not merely that the agent lacked the option,
but that she lacked any reason to think herself in a domain of unknown effects. For a
twenty-first-century engineer deploying a novel system at scale, that condition is almost
never met; the *de dicto* premise is part of the professional common ground. The Residue
shrinks as a field matures, not because the unknowns shrink — Opacity guarantees they do
not — but because knowledge that there are unknowns becomes universally available. This
predicts something we can check: duties to consider specific items should appear exactly
where a domain has been routinised enough for the items to be listed — checklists, failure-
mode catalogues, standard protocols — and should be absent at the frontier, where only
procedural duties can be stated. That is, I think, the pattern we observe.

The final consequence is the one I care about most. If the only requirements that bind
genuinely unaware agents are *de dicto* — precautionary, procedural, deferential — then
the primary bearer of frontier duties cannot be the deliberating individual. Individuals
comply with *de dicto* duties badly: Opacity means the duty never has a salient object, and a
duty with no salient object loses every contest with a live one. What complies with *de dicto*
duties well is structure: staged approval, mandatory instrumentation, adversarial review,
rotation of reviewers with different repertoires, institutional memory of past surprises.
These are not second-best substitutes for individual conscience. Given Opacity, they are the
only form in which the relevant requirement can reliably be met, and designing them is
therefore itself a first-order moral task rather than a matter of administration.

So the conclusion is double. The epistemic condition literature has a blind spot of precisely
the kind it studies: it has theorised failures of belief while assuming away failures of
repertoire. And once repertoire failures are admitted, morality turns out to be able to speak
at the frontier only in the *de dicto* voice — which is why, at the frontier, it must speak
through institutions or not at all.

[^1]: I read Halpern and Rêgo directly (arXiv:cs/0603020v1); I have not read Fagin and
Halpern (1988), Modica and Rustichini, Dekel, Lipman and Rustichini, or Heifetz, Meier
and Schipper, and I rely on Halpern and Rêgo's characterisation of those models. Opacity as
I state it is a claim about models in which awareness is generated by primitive propositions.
It is not a metaphysical thesis about minds; it is the formal correlate of the ordinary
observation that recognising a specific gap in one's repertoire is already filling it.

[^2]: Tripartitions between fact-relative, evidence-relative and belief-relative senses of
'ought' are familiar. I use only a two-way contrast between an objective ought and a
deliberative ought, and I do not rely on any particular author's formulation; I have not
consulted the primary texts of that debate for this essay.

[^3]: A further case sits between the two: failures of *collective* interpretive resources,
where what is missing is the concept under which a harm can be named and protested. The
structure is close to unawareness, and the connection to work on hermeneutical injustice is
obvious, but I was not able to consult primary sources on it here and so do not develop it.
My tentative view is that in such cases the *agent* is usually not unaware of the option
(*stop saying this to her*), so OIR does not excuse; the unawareness is on the side of
naming and demanding rather than on the side of obligation.

**A note on scope.** The argument is deliberately silent on several large questions: whether
the deliberative ought is prior to the objective one, whether accountability blame requires
desert, and how precautionary duties trade off against the goods forgone by caution. Each
would change the tone of §8 but not, I think, the argument of §§4–6.
