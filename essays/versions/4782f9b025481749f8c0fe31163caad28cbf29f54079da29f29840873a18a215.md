# The Signal in Saying No: Confidentiality, Opt-Outs, and Design-First Responsibility

## Abstract

A person can be free to refuse disclosure and still be exposed by refusing. In a group that has chosen to keep a legitimate confidential matter from outsiders, an optional disclosure procedure may make one member’s refusal unusually informative: everyone else’s answers, or a recognizable pattern of answers, can identify the exception. This essay calls that risk response-pattern disclosure. It argues for a limited responsibility on the people who govern such a procedure. When they have accepted responsibility for confidentiality, invited reliance on it, and can reasonably foresee a material inference from the response pattern, they should first redesign the procedure to reduce the signal. This is a modest duty of stewardship, not a general right to prevent inference or a duty on peers to lie. Privacy-preserving refusal by others may be a voluntary last resort, but it is not presumptively owed. The argument is contextual, proportionate, and defeated by stronger rights or duties.

## 1. The problem with an apparently free choice

Suppose a small group has agreed not to expose a member’s sensitive personal circumstance to outsiders. The circumstance is legitimate to keep private: it is not evidence of an imminent threat, abuse, or public wrongdoing that others have a duty to reveal. One member, Ada, has a reason to withhold it. The group’s coordinator introduces an optional round of individual responses, perhaps to learn which participants are affected by a topic or to decide who will speak at a meeting. Participants may answer or decline.

The rule appears respectful. No one is forced to disclose. Yet imagine that the coordinator tells the group that exactly one member has a protected exception. If Ada declines and everyone else answers, the choice reveals who she is. Even without that announcement, an audience may know enough about the group to interpret the pattern. Each answer is voluntary, but the relation among answers creates information that no single answer contains.

The familiar question is whether Ada has a right to say no. She does. A second question is whether others have a duty to imitate her refusal. That question is harder. If they do, their choice may reduce the signal, but the price is that they must perform a response they otherwise would not make. It may also invite pressure to mislead an outsider or suppress others’ speech. Treating solidarity as an automatic duty can make privacy depend on asking the rest of a group to carry the cost of a procedure they did not design.

The prior question is about the procedure itself. If a coordinator has promised confidentiality or taken responsibility for protecting the group’s information, did that responsibility include noticing that the opt-out would identify the person who used it? A meaningful choice must be assessed against the information its exercise makes available. Offering an unprotected refusal does not by itself make the procedure privacy-preserving.

I will call the resulting risk response-pattern disclosure: an observer learns something about a person or a group from the pattern of individual answers, refusals, or deviations from a shared response. The term describes an information channel, not yet a moral wrong. My claim is narrow. When a person or institution has accepted a governance role over a disclosure procedure, has invited reliance on its confidentiality, and can reasonably foresee that the procedure makes a protected refusal materially identifying, that role gives it a defeasible responsibility to redesign the procedure before relying on other participants to conceal the signal. This responsibility does not entail that every inference must be blocked, that group members owe false statements, or that a person’s privacy always defeats public interests.

## 2. Privacy, inference, and the response pattern

Three distinctions keep the claim from becoming too broad.

First, confidentiality is not the same as secrecy or anonymity. A group may share information internally while seeking to limit its flow to outsiders. Nissenbaum’s account of contextual integrity is useful here because it treats privacy as partly dependent on who gathers information, about whom, in what role, and under which rules of distribution. A fact disclosed in one context is not automatically available for every use merely because some participants know it. But contextual integrity is not a command to preserve every custom. Nissenbaum recognizes that existing conventions may need critical evaluation and that conclusions can depend on facts about a proposed change and its effects (Nissenbaum 2004, 138–145, 154–156). The question is not simply “Was this custom broken?” It is “What information moves between which roles, and what values are put at risk by changing the flow?”

Second, inference is not identical to disclosure by the person inferred about. Rumbold and Wilson argue that privacy interests can sometimes extend to information a person did not intend to reveal, even where another person infers it from information that was made public. Their argument makes visible a crucial distinction between access to a signal and the additional fact inferred from it. Response-pattern disclosure fits that general problem: Ada need not utter the sensitive fact for observers to learn it from her refusal and the others’ answers. This does not mean every correct inference violates a right. It means that the moral assessment cannot stop at the words each person literally chose.

Third, group confidentiality and inferential privacy are different. Loi and Christen distinguish privacy in a shared secret among group members from privacy threatened by inferences about members of a group. They argue that a group can have a meaningful interest in protecting shared information, while an absolute right against inference is implausible (Loi and Christen 2020, 207–209, 222–224). My claim accepts that limit. It does not give groups ownership of every fact outsiders infer about them. It concerns a procedure that a responsible actor controls, an identifiable information flow, and a particular person whose legitimate choice to withhold is being converted into evidence by that flow.

The proposed obligation is therefore not an unrestricted right to be unknowable. It is a responsibility attached to a role in designing or governing a procedure. A clinic, research team, support group, workplace committee, or other body may create a channel through which members make choices. If its responsible organizers assure people that a protected response will not expose them, then the organizers have a reason to examine the procedure’s outputs, not just its wording. The duty arises from the conjunction of an accepted responsibility, reasonable reliance, foreseeable risk, and practical power to reduce that risk. Membership alone is insufficient.

## 3. Why a refusal can carry information

A small Bayesian model shows how a response pattern can identify a person without anyone stating the underlying fact. Imagine ten people. Exactly one has a protected exception and always declines to answer. Everyone else independently declines with probability one third, perhaps because they value privacy for their own reasons. Assume an observer knows these rules, starts with an equal prior probability for each person, and sees exactly who declines.

The observer can rule out anyone who answered. Among those who declined, the stipulated model assigns equal likelihood to each person being the protected person. If (X) is the number of non-protected people who also declined, the posterior probability assigned to the protected person after observing the pattern is (1/(1+X)). Before observing any response pattern, the same symmetric observer assigns each person probability 0.10. The model therefore illustrates how an opt-out channel can concentrate belief even when no one directly names the protected person. If no individual response pattern is released and the observer has no other information, the model leaves the prior at 0.10.

The model does not show that real audiences reason this way, that 0.295 is a morally acceptable or unacceptable privacy risk, or that this probability is a general privacy metric. It assumes one protected person, a known rule, a uniform prior, independence, and fixed response tendencies. Real groups may have prior knowledge, correlated choices, unequal reasons to refuse, or repeated observations. Those facts can make the inference weaker, stronger, or different in kind.

Repeated rounds can make the channel more revealing under the same assumptions. If participants answer repeatedly and the observer focuses on those who decline every time, a non-protected person has probability (1/9) of declining in both rounds and (1/27) in all three. The model’s expected posterior probability assigned to the protected identity rises to about 0.623 after two rounds and 0.849 after three. These values are conditional consequences of the toy model, not observations of actual people. Their limited point is that a formally voluntary procedure can accumulate an identifying signal.

The example also shows why a response pattern is not the same as one person’s private answer. It is a joint output of the procedure. Even if every participant exercises an individual choice, the organizer has chosen which choices become visible together, when they become visible, and to whom. A reasonable assessment of confidentiality must include those design choices.

## 4. The role-based responsibility

Why should the organizer bear a responsibility for this risk? Rawls’s distinction between justifying a practice and justifying an action within it helps clarify the question. A practice gives participants roles and rules that make particular acts intelligible; the fact that a practice has a purpose does not mean each participant is free to disregard its rules whenever a local calculation seems attractive. Rawls’s discussion of promising, however, does not establish that any group has a confidentiality practice or determine its content. It is an analogy about how accepted roles can carry obligations, not a source for the conclusion itself (Rawls 1955, 3–4, 15–16, 29–30).

A confidentiality role can be accepted in different ways. An organizer may make an explicit promise, design a procedure advertised as confidential, or take charge of rules whose purpose is to protect sensitive information. Those actions can invite participants to rely on the organizer’s competence and good faith. In accepting the role, the organizer takes on more than a duty not to repeat a fact that was directly disclosed. The organizer has a prospective responsibility to check whether the chosen procedure predictably exposes that fact in another form.

That is a responsibility of stewardship, not omniscience. It applies only to risks the role-holder can reasonably identify, and only to measures within the role-holder’s control. A facilitator cannot promise perfect privacy against every observer or every inference. But a promise not to reveal names is inadequate if the facilitator knowingly publishes a response pattern that identifies the person whose name is withheld. If there is no practical way to prevent a risk, the organizer should avoid overstating what the procedure guarantees and, where possible, let the affected person choose whether to take part.

The research-ethics guidance in Canada offers a close but limited institutional comparison. TCPS 2 says that a researcher who obtains information under a promise of confidentiality assumes an ethical duty to safeguard it, and that institutions should support this work. It also addresses cases where one participant’s decision to waive anonymity could compromise the anonymity of other members of a participant group (TCPS 2, ch. 5, art. 5.1; ch. 10, application of art. 3.2[f]). This is not a general rule for all social groups. Its importance is narrower: a respected confidentiality framework treats promises as responsibilities and recognizes that one person’s choice can affect the privacy of others. It does not instruct every peer to lie or imitate a refusal. The institutional analogue supports role-based design responsibility while leaving the scope of any peer duty open.

A general low-cost aid principle can point in the same direction without settling the case. Singer argues that people should prevent serious harm when they can do so without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance (Singer 1972, 231). If an organizer can make a disclosure procedure safer at little cost, that consideration strengthens the case for doing so. But the analogy does not show that every participant must surrender a truthful answer or deceive an outsider. A design change may affect access, accuracy, accountability, or another person’s interests; its moral cost must be assessed rather than assumed negligible.

## 5. A design-first test

A responsible organizer should ask six questions before treating the opt-out as protective.

**Is there a legitimate confidentiality interest?** The procedure must concern information that a person has a good reason to keep within a limited circle, and the person must be free to choose whether to disclose it. Group members cannot convert their wish for comfort or reputation into a right to silence a person whose speech serves a serious public interest. A group also cannot use “privacy” to shield abuse, imminent danger, or misconduct that others have a stronger obligation to address. Where the protected person does not want concealment, the organizer must not impose it for the group’s convenience.

**Did the organizer accept a relevant responsibility?** An explicit confidentiality promise is strong evidence. So is taking charge of a procedure while representing it as protective or inviting people to rely on it for privacy. A person who merely attends a meeting, or who has no influence over its format, does not acquire the organizer’s duties by association. This condition distinguishes role-based stewardship from a general claim that everyone must help every other person remain unidentified.

**Was the inference reasonably foreseeable?** The question is not whether a cryptographer or omniscient observer could extract information from any conceivable combination of facts. It is whether an ordinary, relevant observer could use the released answers or refusals to identify the exception, given what the organizer knows about the group and audience. The organizer should consider small group size, repeated rounds, familiar patterns, public announcements about how many exceptions exist, and information likely to be combined with the responses. The answer will often be uncertain; uncertainty should shape the strength of the intervention rather than erase the question.

**Would the pattern reveal something material?** Not every difference is sensitive and not every inference harms anyone. A risk matters when it can expose a protected status, breach a reasonable expectation, invite stigma or coercion, or change how the person is treated. This requirement prevents the argument from turning every instance of statistical knowledge into a rights violation.

**Is there a proportionate design change that actually reduces the risk?** The first remedy is to change what the procedure emits. Depending on context, the organizer might collect responses privately through an independent person, avoid publishing individual response vectors, report only broad totals, delay release until small subgroups cannot be isolated, provide a common way to skip without displaying who used it, or avoid collecting the sensitive distinction at all. These are options, not a universal checklist. A nominal change that preserves the same inference does not discharge the responsibility. Nor should a solution create a more serious risk, such as giving a trusted intermediary unnecessary access to identities or withholding information needed for safety.

**Does the response respect other rights and duties?** Participants retain their right to answer, decline, speak truthfully, or challenge the group’s rules. Legal reporting obligations, obligations to protect someone from serious harm, and legitimate public accountability can override a promise. The organizer should not conceal these limits behind a broad assurance. If a genuine conflict exists, it should be identified early and managed with the least harmful procedure available.

When the six considerations point toward a foreseeable, material risk that the organizer can reduce at proportionate cost, there is a defeasible responsibility to redesign before asking peers to do extra work. “Defeasible” matters. The responsibility can be outweighed by an urgent duty to disclose, by serious costs of the privacy measure, or by the affected person’s own contrary choice. The test does not set a universal probability threshold. It asks for a reasoned assessment of the procedure, roles, observer, information, likely effects, alternatives, and competing duties.

## 6. Why cover refusals are not the default answer

A tempting solution is to require other members to refuse too. If enough people do so, the protected member’s refusal may no longer stand out. Yet a demand for cover can reproduce the very problem it aims to solve. It shifts the organizer’s design burden onto individuals who may not have accepted responsibility, and it can curtail their right to speak. If they are told to make false claims about their own status, it also conscripts them into deception.

There is a difference between lying about a fact and declining to confirm or deny it. A group member who freely says, “I do not want to answer that question either,” may be speaking truthfully. That option can support a person’s privacy if the group understands that every refusal is not evidence of the same underlying condition. Still, the option should not be transformed into a compulsory performance. Members may have reasons to answer; the procedure should not presume that they owe concealment merely because they belong to the group.

The organizer may invite voluntary cooperation when it is safe, honest, and genuinely optional. But that is a last resort, after considering whether the channel can be changed directly. If the organizer needs everyone to refuse to make the original procedure safe, that is evidence that the procedure itself may be doing the wrong work. Affected people should be told what can and cannot be protected, and they should not be asked to rely on a cover strategy whose success depends on secrecy about the strategy.

## 7. Objections and limits

### Objection 1: There is no right against inference

A person who refuses in front of others has communicated something. Observers may draw reasonable conclusions from public conduct. Rumbold and Wilson’s account still requires care in deciding when inference violates privacy; Loi and Christen reject an absolute right against inferential privacy. The proposed responsibility may therefore seem like an indirect attempt to control other people’s beliefs.

The objection is correct against a broad right to prevent inference. My argument does not claim one. It distinguishes an observer’s freedom to reason from an organizer’s responsibility for a procedure the organizer controls. One may be permitted to infer a fact while the organizer remains responsible for not designing an avoidable disclosure channel after assuring people it is confidential. Even then, no duty arises unless the information is legitimately protected, the risk is material and foreseeable, and redesign is proportionate. Privacy can generate limits on how information is supplied without claiming ownership over every thought an observer might form.

### Objection 2: Covering an opt-out deceives outsiders

If other members respond in a way designed to confuse observers, perhaps the group misleads people who have a legitimate interest in the information. Privacy protection cannot turn any secret into a license to deceive.

That is why cover refusal is not the central remedy. Redesigning the channel can reduce inference without making a false statement. Sometimes the proper response is to disclose the limits of confidentiality, not to conceal them. If public accountability or safety gives outsiders a claim to the information, that claim may defeat the privacy interest. The organizer’s duty is not to construct a lie; it is to avoid a misleading assurance and to govern information flow responsibly. Where non-disclosure itself would be wrongful, the argument does not apply.

### Objection 3: The group may be protecting wrongdoing

Confidentiality can protect vulnerable people, but it can also shield misconduct or preserve unjust power. A group could frame an exception as private merely to prevent scrutiny.

The legitimate-interest condition rules out that shortcut. Group agreement alone does not settle whether the information should be concealed. A serious threat, abuse, or public-interest claim may override the expectation. Contextual analysis must evaluate the purpose of the confidentiality practice, not simply inherit its boundaries. Nissenbaum’s context-sensitive framework is useful only when paired with scrutiny of the values served by the practice. If the underlying secrecy is unjustifiable, the design duty falls away.

### Objection 4: The organizer cannot know what observers will infer

Responses may be combined with hidden information or interpreted in ways that no procedure designer could predict. A duty based on unforeseeable inference would be impossible to satisfy.

The responsibility is limited by reasonable foreseeability and control. It does not require prediction of every observer or guarantee of perfect anonymity. It asks whether ordinary knowledge of the group and the information flow makes a material inference a reasonable concern. If the risk cannot be assessed, the organizer should avoid absolute assurances and may choose a more protective format when that choice is low cost. The standard is reasonable stewardship, not omniscience.

### Objection 5: Redesign is too costly or undermines the purpose

A privacy-preserving procedure may reduce accuracy, make a necessary decision impossible, increase administrative burdens, or prevent participants from being credited for their contributions. Sometimes individual answers are important.

The response is proportionality, not automatic suppression. A designer should identify the procedure’s legitimate function and compare workable alternatives. A modest delay or aggregation may solve the inference problem; in another case it may destroy information needed for fair treatment. If no alternative adequately protects the person without a greater cost, the organizer may need to tell the person the limit and leave the decision to them. A promise should not be interpreted as unlimited if it was never possible to keep. But it should not be treated as empty merely because perfect protection is impossible.

### Objection 6: Responsibility belongs only to the person who refuses

Perhaps each participant is responsible only for their own speech. If others answer truthfully, they do nothing wrong. It is unfair to make them responsible for a third party’s inference.

This objection identifies an important limit. Participants do not acquire the organizer’s duty merely by being present, and no one is generally required to sacrifice truthful speech for another’s privacy. The organizer’s responsibility follows from control over the procedure and an accepted assurance or governance role. If participants voluntarily agree to a shared confidentiality practice, they may take on further obligations, but the organizer cannot assume that agreement without asking or translate membership into consent.

## 8. Conclusion

An opt-out can preserve a person’s formal freedom while making the exercise of that freedom identifying. The resulting information may come from the pattern of responses rather than from any answer taken alone. That possibility does not establish a general right against inference, a universal secrecy norm, or a duty on peers to lie.

It does support a limited principle of responsible design. A person or institution that has accepted responsibility for a confidentiality procedure and invited reliance on it should consider whether its own response rules turn refusal into evidence. If a material inference is reasonably foreseeable and a proportionate alternative can reduce it, the first responsibility is to change the channel, not to require other people to simulate the same refusal. Voluntary and truthful refusal by others can remain available when needed, but it is not owed by default.

The principle is modest because responsibility depends on context, accepted role, reasonable reliance, foreseeable effect, control, and the legitimacy of the information being protected. It leaves room for public duties and for the affected person’s choice to speak. It asks only that a promise of confidentiality be evaluated by what the procedure actually reveals, including what can be learned from saying no.
