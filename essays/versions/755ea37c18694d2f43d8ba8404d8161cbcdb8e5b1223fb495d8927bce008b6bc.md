# Privacy Should Not Leave Us Alone

A housing office asks applicants to discuss their needs in private. That seems right. A person should not have to tell the whole town about a disability, a family breakdown, or an unsafe home in order to seek help. But suppose several applicants believe that the office humiliates people with a particular need. Each knows only what happened in one appointment. The office treats each complaint as an isolated misunderstanding. To show a pattern, applicants must find one another. To find one another, they may have to disclose precisely what the private appointment was meant to protect.

The example is imaginary, but the problem is clear. Keeping personal information private can prevent people from discovering that they share a public problem. It does not follow that privacy is the obstacle we should remove. We should ask why people must expose themselves in order to make the institution answer for its conduct.

I argue that an institution can owe people a protected way to combine their evidence. This duty arises when it runs a practice that seriously affects them, when separate complaints predictably conceal possible repeated harm, and when a reasonably reliable, less exposing route is feasible. People retain a choice about contributing. The institution must provide the route because it owes them an effective opportunity to question how it treats them.

The claim concerns the arrangement of a practice, not a new device. Confidential complaints and protected reporting already exist. The philosophical question is when their absence is a failure of justice rather than a missed opportunity for better management.

## What needs to become public?

Return to the housing office. There are at least three things a person might want others to learn. First, that something troubling happened to her. Second, that similar things happened to several applicants. Third, that a particular official is responsible for wrongdoing. These claims need different support, and they need not have the same audience.

An independent reviewer might need to know who the applicants are in order to check that their accounts concern different events. The public may need to know how many accounts were checked, what conduct they describe, and how the office responded. It does not automatically need the applicants’ names. The official accused of misconduct may need enough detail to challenge the allegations. That still does not give every neighbour or future employer a claim to those details.

Privacy is therefore not simply a choice between making information available and keeping it unavailable. It also concerns who receives it, for what purpose, and under what limits. Nissenbaum’s account of privacy makes these questions central. She also allows established information practices to be challenged by their effects on justice; existing customs do not settle what ought to happen (2004, III.B–C). The housing office cannot defend an ineffective complaint system merely by saying that it has always handled cases separately.

The question is whether access can be divided so that a pattern is open to scrutiny while the people behind it remain protected from unnecessary exposure. Sometimes it can. Sometimes the facts are so distinctive that publishing even a summary identifies someone. The duty defended here depends on that difference. Removing names is not enough to fulfil it.

## Why the duty belongs to the institution

It is easy to say that a protected reporting system would be useful. A stronger claim needs an argument about what the institution owes.

Begin with a modest requirement of public authority. An office that makes consequential decisions about people must give them a reasonable way to challenge its conduct. It cannot answer a complaint merely by pointing out that it followed its own procedure. Whether that procedure conceals errors may be the very matter in dispute. An opportunity to challenge must therefore reach beyond the evidence that the office finds convenient to collect.

This requirement is grounded in the position of the people governed. An applicant is not simply a source of information for an efficient agency. She is someone to whom the agency must justify its use of power. If the agency could choose any evidential arrangement it liked, it could make its conduct practically unanswerable without declaring itself above criticism. It could demand evidence that its own arrangements make almost impossible to obtain.

Now consider harms that become intelligible only across cases. A single rude remark may look ambiguous. Repeated demands made only of one class of applicants may reveal a policy in practice. A person can have ample grounds to doubt her treatment without having enough evidence to establish this wider claim. If all appointments are private and all complaints are sealed into separate files, the evidence may exist while no affected person can use it to challenge the pattern.

The institution is not necessarily responsible for every difficulty in finding evidence. It is, however, responsible for the way it organizes the practice and its review. Where that organization predictably leaves each person with only a fragment, it owes a reasonable means of bringing the fragments together. Otherwise it offers an individual opportunity to complain about a wrong that can only be shown collectively. The opportunity misses the complaint.

Finally, the institution should not attach an avoidable personal burden to the exercise of that opportunity. Publicly identifying oneself as a disabled applicant, a person escaping a violent household, or someone in financial distress can be a serious burden even where no official retaliates. If a protected route can obtain sufficiently reliable evidence, requiring public exposure adds nothing needed for scrutiny. The office makes applicants pay for a design choice it could reasonably change.

These steps yield a conditional duty. The institution must offer a protected route when a consequential practice creates a foreseeable need to compare experiences, the ordinary means of challenge fail to meet that need without serious exposure, and a feasible alternative would make the challenge meaningfully possible. It need not fund every conceivable inquiry. It must address a gap in the accountability of its own practice.

The duty can arise before wrongdoing is proved. Requiring proof of a pattern before providing the means to establish it would repeat the problem. But the possibility of any imaginable harm is not enough. Relevant grounds might include features of the practice that make selective mistreatment hard to see, recurrent but inconclusive complaints, or a serious reason to doubt that people can report safely. The appropriate response depends on the stakes, risks, and costs. A large housing authority faces stronger demands than a temporary group making a minor shared decision.

This is an argument about public institutions. Private organizations that exercise comparable power may face similar duties, but that extension needs its own account of their authority. Nothing here implies a general entitlement to pool every fact about everyone.

## Providing a route without demanding a story

There is a familiar way to defend privacy’s public value: people who can think, speak, and associate without constant scrutiny are better able to participate in collective life. Cohen argues that institutions and surveillance shape the capacity for citizenship itself, not just the choices of already formed citizens (2013, III). That view helps explain why a complaint system must be assessed as part of the conditions of participation.

My narrower point concerns access to a particular form of participation. A person may be able to complain only if others can contribute related experiences. Protecting each account in complete isolation may leave everyone formally free to speak while making an effective common challenge unreasonably costly. The remedy is an opportunity to combine accounts under suitable restrictions.

Ayres and Unkovic describe a closely related mechanism: a trusted intermediary can hold a report and release it when an agreed condition is met, such as receiving another allegation about the same person (2012, abstract). That proposal already addresses the difficulty of being first to disclose. My claim is not that this mechanism is new. It is that, under the conditions just defended, the institution owes an effective protected route, whether an escrow, an independent reviewer, or another arrangement best serves the purpose.

Providing that route does not make disclosure compulsory. The duty falls on the institution partly because the alternative unfairly burdens those seeking to challenge it. Turning the alternative into an obligation to report would place a different burden on the same people. Someone may wish to contribute; she may also reasonably prefer to recover, leave, or keep an experience to herself. Neither silence nor refusal to use the system should count as evidence that nothing happened.

There will be losses when people decline. A pattern may remain hidden. That is a limit on what institutions can know, not an automatic licence to extract personal testimony. Some exceptional duties to warn of grave danger may override this presumption, but they need a separate argument. The value of collective knowledge by itself does not establish them.

## Three decisions, three demands for evidence

The strongest objection is that protected reporting could make accusations easy and answers impossible. Several people can repeat a rumour. One person can submit several accounts. A reviewer can misunderstand events. Shielding sources can shield mistakes.

These are not reasons to abandon protection. They are reasons to specify what a protected report may justify.

A collection of reports can first justify an investigation. It need not establish that the reported conduct occurred. It may identify a question worth examining through records, interviews, or observation. At this stage, the institution can respond without announcing that any official is guilty. Even here, investigations impose costs and can damage reputations, so a mere count should not trigger unlimited inquiry. But the threshold need not be the threshold for punishment.

Second, reports may justify changing a procedure. Suppose applicants independently describe being required to discuss intimate circumstances within hearing of a waiting room. The office might install a private interview space after checking the layout, without determining which official caused each disclosure. The justification combines reports with evidence about the arrangement itself. Protection can coexist with a well-supported remedy because the remedy does not depend on a final finding about one disputed encounter.

Third, reports may be used to impose a serious penalty on a particular person. Here the duty of fairness to that person becomes more demanding. He must have a genuine opportunity to answer the grounds for the decision. A statement that several secret reports exist may not provide it. Sometimes details, and perhaps identities, are necessary to test an allegation. If the required evidence cannot fairly be used without disclosure, the institution may have to forgo the penalty, obtain other evidence, or seek the contributor’s informed agreement to a different process. The earlier promise of protection cannot honestly mean that every desired remedy will remain available.

These distinctions also constrain institutional evasion. An office cannot say that because a confidential report would not support dismissal, it supports no response at all. Failure to justify the third decision does not settle the first two. Conversely, advocates cannot treat a sound reason to investigate as a finding of guilt.

The protected route should explain these limits before accepting reports. People need to know who may see their information, what checks can be made, what circumstances could require further permission, and what cannot be promised. Absolute secrecy advertised by a system that cannot deliver it is a new way of exploiting trust.

## Can the institution be trusted?

Another objection goes deeper. The organization accused of wrongdoing may control the reviewer, the categories, and the published result. It might turn protected reporting into a way of collecting damaging information while keeping complainants apart.

This possibility matters because the duty is to enable a challenge, not merely to receive information. A system fails if it provides privacy but leaves the institution free to suppress every uncomfortable pattern. Depending on the circumstances, adequate provision may require a reviewer outside the office’s chain of command, a way to contest the reviewer’s findings, and a public account of what was done in response. People using the system should also have a way to point out harms that the reporting form’s categories do not recognize.

The public must have grounds for trusting the process without receiving every personal record. It can examine the reviewer's independence, checking methods, reasons for excluding accounts, and the basis of the resulting conclusions. Some of that examination may itself require restricted access by a second reviewer. This is not perfect transparency. It is divided scrutiny, with each access justified by what the person receiving it needs to check.

There is no guarantee that such an arrangement will work everywhere. A tiny community may make contributors identifiable whatever is published. An abusive institution may control every plausible reviewer. Data collection may create risks greater than the benefit of the proposed route. In these cases, the conditional duty does not demand an unsafe database. It demands serious attention to alternatives: external review, changes that remove the need for intimate disclosure, or a less detailed form of investigation. Where no adequate protected route is feasible, the institution must acknowledge the gap. It should not describe silence produced by danger as reassuring evidence of fairness.

## What privacy asks of collective life

Privacy and accountability are sometimes presented as opposing demands: either protect people’s information or reveal what the public needs to know. The housing-office example shows why this framing can miss the institution’s own responsibility. The conflict may be intensified by a procedure that keeps evidence separate and then asks each person to prove a shared pattern alone.

An institution that owes people an effective opportunity to challenge its conduct sometimes also owes them a protected way to combine their accounts. That duty is limited by feasibility, reliability, and the rights of those accused. It protects voluntary contribution rather than requiring it. It separates evidence sufficient to raise a question from evidence sufficient to punish someone.

The central demand is simple. People should not have to choose between keeping an intimate experience private and finding out whether it is part of a public wrong when a reasonable institutional arrangement could preserve both possibilities.
