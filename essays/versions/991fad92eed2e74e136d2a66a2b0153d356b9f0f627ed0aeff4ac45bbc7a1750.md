# What an Allocation Decides: Choice Without a Whole-Life Ranking

## Abstract

When a scarce resource goes to one person rather than another, people often treat the decision as if it ranked the recipients’ lives. That inference is not automatic. A comparison of claims to a specified intervention has different objects and may rest on reasons that do not compare the beneficiaries’ complete lives. I defend a limited non-entailment claim: a justified allocation decision need not establish that one recipient’s life, considered as a whole, is at least as good as another’s. The claim applies only when the allocation rule is independently defensible, its relevant context includes the facts its reasons treat as important, and its strict-priority relation is acyclic. A unique maximal option can then be selected; if several remain, a separately defended tie procedure is needed. An equal-claims lottery can illustrate the point, but only under a defended principle of fair procedure; equality alone does not settle how to distribute unequal claims. Sen’s distinction between ordering and choice supports the formal possibility without proving a moral rule. Chang’s account of parity blocks an inference to incomparability. The result is a diagnostic for arguments about allocation, not a general triage theory or a claim of originality.

## 1. The decision and the conclusion it does not contain

Imagine a clinic with one indivisible treatment slot left for the day. Two patients, Ada and Ben, meet the stated eligibility requirements. In this deliberately narrow example, the evidence supports the same expected treatment benefit and urgency for each. Their rights and special obligations do not differ. The allocation policy also treats their relevant histories as equal. The clinic has no additional slot, no safe way to divide the treatment, and no defensible reason to give either patient priority. Its published rule therefore gives each an equal chance through a public draw. Ada’s name is drawn, and she receives the treatment.

The result is a choice. But what does it tell us? It tells us that Ada was selected by a specified procedure among two equal claims. It does not, by itself, tell us that Ada’s life is better than Ben’s, that Ben’s life is worse, or that their lives are equally good. Nor does it tell us that either patient is more worthy, has greater dignity, or matters more. The reason to select Ada is that a fair draw resolved a tie, not that her complete life won a competition of value.

The distinction sounds simple, but it matters whenever an institution must distribute a scarce benefit. A decision-maker may need to say who receives an organ, a place in a shelter, a treatment, or a limited public benefit. The official choice can be mistaken for a judgment about which person has the better life or whose welfare counts more. That conclusion requires an additional premise. A choice between actions is not yet a complete ranking of the people affected by those actions.

I will defend only this limited claim: **a justified selection among feasible allocations does not, on its own, entail a complete weak-betterness ordering of the recipients’ lives as wholes.** By this I mean a reflexive and transitive relation that compares every relevant pair of lives, saying of each pair that one is at least as good as the other, or that both are equally good. This is a claim about comparative well-being across lives, not about equal moral worth. It does not deny that there is such an ordering, that another moral theory may require one, or that particular allocations sometimes depend on whole-life consequences. It says only that the fact of a justified choice does not logically establish that relation without further premises.

The word “justified” also needs care. A bare choice is not thereby justified. In the example, the conclusion depends on several independent claims: the patients have equal claims under a defensible policy; the policy has no relevant reason for preferring one; the resource cannot be shared; and an equal-chance procedure is fair for resolving this kind of tie. Each is contestable. If one fails, the clinic may have no justified choice. The non-entailment argument does not turn an arbitrary decision into a just one. It asks what follows if a separate allocation rule is justified.

## 2. Three different relations

To see why the inference fails, distinguish three questions.

First, which feasible action should the decision-maker take? The options may be to assign a treatment slot to Ada, assign it to Ben, or use a defined procedure to determine which assignment is made. This is a question about actions or allocation outcomes.

Second, which claim has priority with respect to this resource, at this time, under these circumstances? A person may have a stronger claim because of greater urgency, a relevant right, a more effective intervention, a prior promise, or a history of being unfairly excluded. This is a question about claims and reasons for action. Its answer depends on which reasons the policy accepts and how they interact.

Third, how do Ada and Ben’s complete lives compare in well-being? This question considers each life as a whole, potentially including its past and future, not merely the impact of one treatment or the strength of one claim. It is not identical to asking whose life has greater moral worth. A society could affirm equal worth while judging one life better in welfare, or decline to make any complete welfare comparison.

These questions can be connected, but the connection must be argued for. A medical policy might treat expected survival benefit as decisive. A particular argument could then compare the consequences of assigning the slot to Ada or to Ben. A prioritarian policy might weigh a benefit more heavily when its recipient is worse off over a lifetime. A rights-based policy might give priority to someone whose claim has been wrongfully delayed. These rules have different objects, and each needs a defense. It is not enough to call a reason “local” or “procedural.” The decision-maker must show what the reason is about and why it matters.

The equal-claims case makes this point visible. The clinic’s rule does not ask whether Ada’s life is better than Ben’s. It asks whether there is a relevant basis for unequal priority in this allocation. Finding none, it uses a procedure that gives each claimant equal odds. The outcome identifies the winner of that procedure. It does not change the comparison’s subject from “claims to this slot” to “complete lives.”

The relation itself must still be coherent. On a finite menu, an acyclic strict-priority relation has at least one maximal option. If there is only one, the rule can select it without comparing complete lives. If there are several, the relation alone does not identify a winner and needs a defensible tie procedure. If local priorities form a cycle, there is no maximal option under that relation. This structural fact diagnoses what the rule can select; it does not justify the rule’s priorities.

Equal chance is not the same as equal lives. Equal chance is a probability assigned to access under a rule. Equal lives would be a claim that the beneficiaries’ lives are equally good, or perhaps that their interests have some specified equal value. The first claim can be true without the second. Likewise, equal moral worth does not itself imply equal claims to every resource. There may be differences in need, effectiveness, history, rights, or the number of people who can benefit. Those differences must be examined rather than erased by an appeal to equality.

## 3. Why choice does not entail a complete life-ranking

The argument can be stated more exactly. Let the feasible allocation options be the options of assigning the treatment to Ada or to Ben. Let the clinic’s allocation rule use only the specified claim facts and the fair-draw procedure. Assume, for purposes of this example, that the rule is independently justified and that those facts are equal. The procedure then gives Ada and Ben the same chance of selection. Once the draw occurs, one option is selected.

Now consider what would be required to infer a complete ordering of their whole lives. We would need a premise connecting the allocation result to whole-life betterness. For example: “Whenever a person is selected for a scarce benefit, that person’s life is at least as good as the life of each person not selected.” The clinic has no such premise. Its rule treats the draw as a fair way to resolve equal claims, not as evidence about which life is better. Without a bridge premise of this kind, the conclusion does not follow.

This is not just a gap in words. The same allocation rule is compatible with different whole-life comparisons. Suppose the facts about the present claim remain as stipulated, while the complete welfare comparison is left open. One possible account might rank Ada’s life above Ben’s. Another might rank Ben’s life above Ada’s. A third might say that one life is neither better, worse, nor equally good, or that the comparison is uncertain. The fair-draw procedure can remain the same in each case, unless the clinic adopts a further rule that makes those life facts relevant. Thus the allocation premises alone do not entail one particular whole-life ranking.

The example is conditional, not a demonstration that actual clinicians can safely ignore every difference between patients. If the treatment produces different expected benefits, if the patients have different claims, or if a morally relevant history has been omitted, the allocation premises change. If a policy explicitly aggregates whole-life benefits, the argument does not apply to that policy. The point is narrower: there are defensible allocation rules whose stated reasons can settle a choice without ranking the recipients’ complete lives. Whether such a rule is suitable in a particular case is another question.

Formal work on choice helps to keep these ideas apart. Arrow’s famous social-choice framework includes a completeness condition for an ordering over alternatives: for every pair, one alternative is at least as good as the other (Arrow 1951, 13). That is a condition on a relation used to order alternatives. It is not, by itself, a moral proof that every allocator must rank the complete lives of everyone who may receive a resource. To reach that conclusion, one would need additional claims about which alternatives are being compared, why the formal condition is morally required, and how an ordering over allocations translates into a ranking of lives.

Sen explicitly distinguishes an ordering from the problem of choice. In his discussion of collective choice, he says, “For consistent choice it is not needed that the society should have an ordering” (Sen 1970, 47; reprinted in the 2017 expanded edition). His example is not a simple endorsement of incomplete rankings: it involves a nontransitive relation, and he notes that completeness may still be needed in some menus. In particular, if the available choice is only between two options that remain unranked, a decision-maker may be “in trouble” (Sen 1970, 47–48). Sen therefore does not prove that every incomplete decision rule is adequate. His more limited point is that choice and complete ordering are distinct formal tasks, and that whether an incomplete relation suffices depends on the choice situation.

The clinic’s lottery is meant to fill the specific gap Sen identifies. The equality of the claims does not produce a unique maximal option by itself. A tie procedure supplies an action rule. It need not extend the incomplete relation into a complete ranking of lives. A person who insists that every rational or morally justified choice must be based on a complete ordering may reject this procedure. But that insistence is an additional normative or rationality requirement. It is not a conclusion contained in the bare fact that the clinic selected someone.

## 4. The lottery needs its own defense

It would be a mistake to infer from this distinction that a lottery is always fair. John Taurek famously suggests giving each of six people an equal chance when one treatment can save either one person or a group of five: “Why not give each person an equal chance to survive? Perhaps I could flip a coin” (Taurek 1977, 303). His proposal is an argument about how to express equal concern in a conflict between claims, not an uncontested rule.

Derek Parfit’s reply presses a serious challenge. Equal concern, he argues, can support giving equal weight to each person’s loss, and this may favor saving the larger number: “Each counts for one. That is why more count for more” (Parfit 1978, 301). The dispute shows that the phrase “everyone counts equally” does not settle how to distribute a scarce resource. An equal chance for every individual and an equal weight for every individual can lead to different decisions when claims differ in number. A lottery that ignores the additional claims of five people may be unfair, even if it does not rank their lives as more or less valuable.

The clinic example avoids deciding that dispute. It involves one claim on each side, not one person versus five; it stipulates equal urgency, equal expected benefit, equal rights, and equal relevant history. The lottery is defensible here only if an independent principle says that equally strong claims to an indivisible resource deserve equal chances when no further relevant reason breaks the tie. On that account, a public draw prevents officials from favoring one claimant without a reason, treats the claims symmetrically, and makes the decision procedure open to both. The justification comes from the fairness of resolving an acknowledged tie, not from a theory that Ada’s life is better or that Ben’s is worse.

Even this defense has limits. A fair chance at one moment can become unfair over repeated decisions if the same person repeatedly loses, if past access is unjust, or if there are good reasons to rotate access or protect a particular right. A patient may also have a claim that is more urgent despite equal predicted benefit. In such cases, the facts are not equal in the morally relevant sense, and the lottery cannot be defended by simply repeating that both people matter. The example shows a possible way to resolve a narrowly specified tie. It does not establish that any real allocation is such a tie.

The difference between choice and life-ranking remains even when the lottery is rejected. If a clinic instead uses a justified rule that selects the patient with the stronger right or more urgent need, it may still be comparing claims to treatment rather than complete lives. Yet if it argues that one person should be chosen because that person’s total life would be better, then it has introduced a whole-life premise. The argument’s form, not its label, determines whether the premise is present.

## 5. Parity and the limits of “incomparability”

The claim should not be defended by saying that Ada and Ben’s lives are incomparable. That conclusion would go further than the argument and would misstate an important debate. Ruth Chang argues that the familiar relations of better, worse, and equally good do not exhaust every possible evaluative comparison. Two items may be “on a par” without either being better, worse, or equally good (Chang 2002, 659–67). She also emphasizes that comparisons require a covering consideration: we need to know what respect or value is at issue. As she notes, “items can be compared with respect to fulfilling an obligation or duty” (Chang 2002, 667).

Chang’s account blocks a tempting but invalid move: from “the allocation did not establish which life is better” to “the lives are incomparable.” Those are different claims. A parity relation might hold between the lives. There might instead be a fact of the matter that the decision-maker does not know, a borderline comparison, or genuine incomparability. The allocation decision alone settles none of these possibilities. My thesis concerns the absence of an entailment to a complete weak-betterness ordering, not the truth of any specific alternative relation.

Chang’s idea of a covering consideration helps make the allocation distinction precise. The clinic may compare Ada’s and Ben’s claims with respect to fulfilling a duty of fair access, while refusing to combine every feature of their lives into one overall welfare scale. That comparison is still substantive: the clinic must explain why fair access is relevant and what the duty requires. But the relation under discussion is one of action or obligation, not automatically a ranking of complete lives. A choice can therefore rest on a comparison of a different kind without pretending that no comparison exists at all.

## 6. A bridge audit for allocation arguments

When an allocation rule appears to rank people’s lives, ask four questions.

**What are the objects being compared?** Are they complete lives, expected treatment outcomes, current claims, needs, rights, or feasible actions? A sentence such as “Ada should get the slot” concerns a decision. A sentence such as “Ada’s life is better than Ben’s” concerns comparative well-being. One does not translate into the other without argument.

**What is the covering reason?** Is priority based on survival probability, urgency, equality of access, a duty, a right, lifetime disadvantage, or something else? The rule must state which facts it treats as relevant and why. Calling a reason “local” does not defend it.

**What bridge premise connects the reason to a whole-life ranking?** A rule may say that a claimant with the greater chance of immediate benefit takes priority. That is not yet the claim that their life as a whole is better. A policy may add that expected lifetime welfare determines priority. If so, it should defend that premise openly. If no such bridge is defended, a conclusion about whole-life betterness should not be attributed to the allocation result.

**What would make the rule fail?** A missing history, a right, an unequal treatment benefit, an unstable tie, or a cycle in the policy’s priority relation can defeat or unsettle its conclusion. A coherent rule may still be unjust; a just-sounding set of pairwise reasons may still conflict. If several options remain equally eligible, the policy must justify its tie procedure or acknowledge that it has not resolved the case.

This audit is a modest clarification, not a new theory of justice. It helps expose the additional premise that is often hidden when institutions convert an action-guiding reason into a sweeping statement about a person’s life. It also reveals when the distinction will not protect a policy. If a decision-maker already relies on an all-things-considered welfare function over complete lives, then the rule may indeed contain the ranking the audit seeks to identify. The non-entailment claim is not a shield against accurate criticism of such a policy.

## 7. History can change the answer

The equal-claims example deliberately fixes the facts that might otherwise make the claims unequal. Real allocation decisions rarely permit that simplicity. A person may have been repeatedly denied treatment, may be worse off over a lifetime, or may now face more urgent need. These histories can matter in different ways. In his discussion of equality and priority, Parfit emphasizes that decision-makers may need to know how people’s situations arose and distinguishes lifetime disadvantage from other grounds for giving priority (Parfit 2000, 83, 101–03). The point is not that one measure is always correct, but that a policy must say which disadvantage it aims to address.

This history problem exposes the weakness of simple corrective-access rules. Counting how many comparable treatments each person has already received may be an administratively clear proxy. But the count may miss differences in need, severity, effectiveness, or the value of past benefits. It may also treat dissimilar procedures as equivalent. A clinic cannot establish justice simply by showing that its rule produces a coherent priority ordering. It must defend the measure of disadvantage and explain how it interacts with competing claims.

Nor does the argument imply that whole-life comparison is never required. A prioritarian or aggregative theory may hold that benefits matter more for people who are worse off over their lives as a whole, or that total welfare across recipients should determine the allocation. Such a theory can require facts about entire lives and can make those facts decisive. If its premises are justified, the resulting decision may express or depend on a whole-life comparison. The limited claim defended here does not refute those theories. It says that their extra premise must be stated and defended rather than smuggled in by treating every act of selection as if it already proved a life-ranking.

## 8. Objections and replies

### “A rational choice must rank every option.”

One objection is that rational decision-making requires a complete ordering. If a policy does not rank every pair of possible allocations, it cannot choose consistently. This objection points to a real formal standard, as Arrow’s completeness condition illustrates. But the conclusion still does not follow as stated. First, an ordering over allocations is not a ranking of recipients’ complete lives. Second, a choice procedure can select from a menu without supplying a complete ordering over every pair of lives. Sen’s discussion shows both the possibility and the limitation: incomplete relations can suffice for some choice problems but fail when the menu contains only unranked alternatives. The clinic’s lottery is introduced precisely as an additional procedure for a tie.

An objector may insist that a lottery is itself an arbitrary choice and that a complete comparison would be more rational. The clinic can answer only by defending its procedural principle: when two claims are genuinely equal under the adopted rule, a public equal-chance draw treats them symmetrically and prevents an official from inventing a priority. Whether this is the best account of rational choice is open. But even if the objector is right that a complete ranking is required, it remains a further requirement and not a consequence of the selected name alone. A complete ranking of actions, moreover, still would not automatically rank lives unless the action alternatives and life-comparisons are connected by a premise.

### “The policy’s reasons hide a life-ranking.”

A second objection says that no allocation reason is truly independent of whole-life value. A treatment benefit matters because of the good life it enables. Urgency matters because some lives face greater loss. Prior access matters because of the life each person has led. The rule may therefore conceal a comparison of life prospects under narrower language.

Sometimes this objection is correct. A policy that ranks expected lifetime benefit or total welfare depends on whole-life consequences. A rule that weights disadvantage over a complete life uses such facts. The bridge audit is meant to uncover exactly those cases. But the objection cannot establish a universal entailment. A right to a promised service, an equal claim under a fair procedure, or a duty to honor equal access can give a reason for action without comparing the recipients’ lives as wholes. A critic may argue that these duties are not ultimately defensible unless supported by a theory of value. That is a substantive challenge to those duties, not a logical derivation from selecting one recipient.

### “The lottery gives equal value to the lives.”

The third objection treats equal odds as a judgment that each life has equal value. But probabilities and welfare comparisons have different meanings. The clinic’s procedure gives equal access chances because its policy recognizes equal claims under specified conditions. That premise does not say the complete lives have equal welfare, nor that the patients are interchangeable in every respect. A lottery can express equal standing before a decision rule without asserting equal outcomes or a total comparison.

Taurek and Parfit show why this reply has limits. Their argument concerns equal concern when one option saves more people. Taurek’s equal-chance proposal and Parfit’s equal-weight reply can disagree even while each takes individuals seriously. In a one-versus-many case, the lottery may fail to account for the additional claims. So the equal-claims case must not be generalized to unequal numbers, unequal losses, or unequal rights. The procedure is defensible only if equal claim strength has been established and the relevant policy treats equal chance as a fair way to resolve that exact tie.

### “Without a life-ranking, the decision is underdetermined.”

Finally, a critic may say that if the recipients’ lives are not fully ranked, the clinic cannot know whom to choose. That can be true if no rule resolves the available options. In the example, the clinic begins with equal claims and then invokes a separately defended tie procedure. If the procedure is not accepted, or if omitted facts defeat the tie, the decision may indeed remain underdetermined. The thesis does not promise a determinate allocation in every case. It distinguishes two conclusions: a policy may select an action without ranking whole lives, but it may not be justified in doing so unless its action rule is independently defensible.

## 9. What follows

The clinic’s draw, if its premises hold, gives a limited conclusion: Ada is the selected recipient under a fair procedure for equal claims. It does not establish that Ada’s life is better, that Ben’s life is worse, that their lives are equally good, or that they are incomparable. To reach any such conclusion, the decision-maker would need further premises and a clear account of the relation being asserted.

The general point is a discipline of inference. A reason to choose an action, a reason to give someone priority for a resource, and a judgment about the comparative quality of complete lives may be related, but they are not interchangeable. Formal social-choice work makes room for distinguishing orderings from choice rules, while Chang’s account of parity warns against equating a missing better/worse/equal judgment with incomparability. The dispute between Taurek and Parfit shows that even equal concern leaves important allocation questions unsettled. Parfit’s discussion of history shows that a real priority rule may need facts the simple example holds fixed.

What survives is modest. When a claim-sensitive allocation rule is coherent and independently justified, the act of selecting an intervention does not logically entail a complete weak-betterness ordering of the recipients’ whole lives. A lottery can illustrate that possibility among genuinely equal claims, but only when its fairness is defended for that case. If the policy rests on lifetime welfare, unequal claims, hidden histories, or an unresolved tie, the conclusion must change. Nothing here certifies the justice of a real clinic rule or establishes that this clarification is original. The defensible demand is simpler: state what the allocation compares, defend the reason for priority, and do not describe the result as a judgment about whole lives unless an additional argument supports that description.
