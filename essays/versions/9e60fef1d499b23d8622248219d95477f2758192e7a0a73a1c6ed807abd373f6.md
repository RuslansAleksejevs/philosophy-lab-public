# What Wrongful Ignorance Cannot Settle

A museum cannot establish whether it bought a painting or borrowed it. Its former managers deliberately destroyed the relevant acquisition records to reduce storage costs, disregarding a clear obligation to preserve them. They did not inspect the records before destruction, so their conduct supplies no special reason to think this particular acquisition was improper. The present trustees were uninvolved, have disclosed the destruction, and have exhausted reasonable ways of recovering the information. A family presents credible but inconclusive evidence that the painting was a loan. The trustees reply: we do not know that you own it; therefore, you have not established a claim against us.

The first statement may be impeccable. The inference to the second, understood as a sufficient reason to close the case, is not. The institution's responsibility for an obstacle to proof can change what it may demand of a claimant without changing what its trustees should believe. But that thought has a neglected limit. Suppose a second family presents an equally credible, incompatible claim. A reason for relaxing the first family's burden against the museum cannot simply become a reason for preferring that family to the second. The museum may have lost a defense without either family having acquired a verdict against the other.

I defend an account of this asymmetry. An institution that wrongfully undermines a protected opportunity to establish a claim may lose its entitlement to insist on the resulting failure of proof. This limitation survives an innocent change of personnel. Its justificatory force, however, is relational: it concerns the institution's treatment of the affected claimant. It does not automatically authorize factual certification, adverse treatment of another claimant, or punishment of an alleged wrongdoer. A remedy may therefore require surrendering control over how a dispute is resolved while leaving the underlying factual question open. The problem is not how to inherit somebody else's guilt. It is how to prevent an inherited evidential advantage from acquiring authority it does not possess.

## 1. Three questions concealed in an excuse

There are three distinct questions about the trustees. What does their evidence support? Are they blameworthy for lacking better evidence? What may they do with the institution's resources and powers? Their answers need not coincide.

The distinction between the first two questions is familiar. Rosen's discussion of derivative responsibility asks when an agent's ignorance can be traced to her earlier failure to take required precautions. His trespass example distinguishes having done everything possible from having done what was reasonably required. That account is a proposal about culpability, not a general rule giving ignorance priority over every competing obligation (Rosen 1999, §III). Harman challenges expansive claims that moral ignorance exculpates (2011, §§1–2). We need not settle that dispute. The present case stipulates successors who neither caused nor endorsed the evidential wrong and who now investigate conscientiously. It does not infer innocence from the mere fact that they regard themselves as conscientious.

The third question remains. A blameless trustee can inherit a duty to pay a debt incurred before she joined the board. She can also inherit a restriction on exercising an office: an undertaking to consult depositors, a contractual limitation on investment, or a requirement to preserve entrusted property. Performing these duties does not express guilt. It expresses what holding that position now involves.

Reiff explicitly separates reparative obligations of continuing institutions and successors from the assignment of guilt to their current members (2024, §§2.4–2.5). The general separation is therefore not the proposal here. The further question is what survives when the institution's original liability is itself uncertain. A duty to pay a known debt is easy to transmit. Can a restriction on demanding proof survive when nobody knows whether that debt exists?

We should resist two shortcuts. The first says that because somebody culpably caused the ignorance, the trustees ought to believe the claimant. Culpability is not a device for reconstructing missing information. The second says that because the trustees cannot rationally believe the claimant, their institution has no obligation to respond. That treats an appropriate attitude toward a proposition as sufficient authority for an institutional decision. It is the second shortcut that makes the first seem necessary.

## 2. The dependence of a proof requirement

Consider a simpler case. An organization requires customers to use its internal register as the authoritative record of deposits and repayments. Customers are entitled to rely on its promised preservation of that register. It then wrongfully destroys the register. A customer can establish that she made a deposit, but whether she was subsequently repaid is unclear. The organization insists that she establish the very fact its recordkeeping arrangement was supposed to make ascertainable.

The customer's objection is not that she must be believed because she has suffered. It is that the organization is invoking a condition while disregarding a connected obligation that made imposing that condition acceptable. Its authority to insist on the stipulated proof was supported by a package: the customer must establish the debt, and the organization must preserve the agreed means of establishing it. The organization cannot remove its side of the package while treating the customer's side as intact.

This argument does not require an explicit promise in every case. A comparable dependency can arise where an institution reasonably controls the only effective records, knows that people's claims depend on them, and owes those people a duty to preserve access. But control alone is insufficient. A passerby who happens to remember a transaction has not necessarily undertaken indefinite responsibility for its proof. Nor does every lost document defeat every demand for evidence. The claimant must show a relevant connection between the protected evidential opportunity, its wrongful impairment, and the disputed requirement.

The resulting principle is deliberately limited:

**When an institution has wrongfully impaired a claimant's protected opportunity to satisfy a proof requirement, it may not treat the resulting failure to satisfy that requirement as sufficient to dispose of the claim, unless it supplies an adequate substitute or has an independent justification for closure.**

A protected opportunity is an opportunity that the institution owed it to the claimant to maintain. Its loss need not establish that the claimant would have won. It must, however, bear on the issue for which relief is requested. Losing a receipt for a deposit does not by itself support a claim concerning an unrelated injury. If another independently reliable copy remains readily available, the relevant opportunity may not have been lost at all. If the claimant deliberately frustrated its recovery, the institution may have an independent justification for refusing further accommodation.

Why give protection to an opportunity that might have yielded defeat? Because the value of an adjudicative opportunity does not depend entirely on the result it would have produced. A person can be entitled to a hearing without being entitled to win it. More specifically, the organization has made the opportunity part of the justification for a disadvantageous decision rule. Once that justification fails through its own breach, it owes an account of why the rule should still control. Otherwise the requirement becomes a means of converting the organization's failure into the customer's default.

This principle does not invent liability for lost evidence. Porat and Stein's evidential-damage theory already defends compensation and shifts of the burden of persuasion; it distinguishes the allocation of error risk from mere deficiencies in available information (1999, §§IV(b), VI(b)). Friedman objects to treating a single tort remedy as the general solution (1997, introductory discussion). Both points matter here. There can be an obligation arising from evidential impairment, and its proper discharge need not take one uniform form. My principle identifies a restriction on the justification of closure rather than prescribing automatic victory, a particular evidential threshold, or a universal compensation formula.

## 3. Why the restriction survives

Suppose the organization replaces every person involved in the destruction. Nothing relevant to the customer's protected opportunity has improved. She still faces the same missing register, imposed by the same institution to which she entrusted her money. If the replacement restored the organization's right to reject her for want of that register, a change in personnel would cancel a restriction without repairing what justified it.

There are two reasons to reject this cancellation. First, the original recordkeeping obligation ran to the customer. Its point was to secure her opportunity to establish the transaction, not merely to improve the character of particular clerks. Their departure therefore does not discharge it. Second, the successor continues to exercise the institutional power whose acceptable use depended on that opportunity. She cannot inherit the register's authority as the standard of proof while declining the limitation generated by its wrongful destruction. The power is inherited with its conditions of exercise.

This reasoning applies most securely to continuing institutions and offices. It does not say that sharing a nationality, profession, or distant ancestry makes someone responsible for every past evidential wrong. Nor does it settle when a newly formed entity is sufficiently continuous with an old one. The narrow case is enough: replacing the administrators of an otherwise continuing arrangement does not restore an entitlement the arrangement had lost.

One might prefer to assign the duty entirely to the institution and say that the trustees inherit nothing. That vocabulary is harmless if it leaves their practical responsibilities intact. Someone must decide what the institution does. The trustees' duty is to administer its powers under their existing constraints; it is not to experience remorse on somebody else's behalf.

The account also explains a contrast with innocent loss. If the register disappears despite appropriate precautions, the institution may still owe help under its promises, role, or ability to repair the damage. But the particular argument from wrongful removal of a condition does not automatically apply. A recordkeeping arrangement can fairly allocate some unavoidable risk of loss. By contrast, an arrangement permitting the custodian to create that loss wrongfully and then enforce the customer's unchanged burden would give the custodian unilateral control over the practical value of the customer's protection. Such an arrangement requires a special justification; ordinary appeals to the need for evidence do not provide one.

## 4. Why the restriction does not travel everywhere

Return to the museum. Assume its stewardship duties included preserving acquisition records for people with a credible connection to an acquisition. Two families, Alder and Birch, now claim the same painting. The museum might have acquired valid title, borrowed it from Alder, or borrowed it from Birch. All three histories remain serious possibilities. Nothing in the surviving evidence ranks the families' accounts. Neither family participated in the destruction.

For simplicity, suppose that an appropriate remedy in a dispute involving only Alder would prevent the museum from relying on Alder's inability to produce the missing record. The same supposition is true of Birch. Now consider an instruction to accept each family's ownership claim as true. That instruction yields an impossibility: the painting cannot have been exclusively owned by Alder and exclusively owned by Birch in the stipulated circumstances. A remedy directed at the museum has been mistaken for a method of discovering the painting's history.

The distinction is not rescued by calling each acceptance a presumption rather than a belief. If a presumption for Alder is used to exclude Birch, it has practical force against someone who did not owe Alder the lost evidential opportunity. We need a reason for that additional force. The museum's wrong supplies none by itself. It wronged both families in the relevant respect.

The point can be stated as a constraint on remedial justification:

**A reason to relax a person's proof burden against the party responsible for its impairment does not, solely in virtue of that reason, justify disadvantaging another nonresponsible person whose competing claim is independently credible.**

This is not an absolute prohibition on imposing costs on innocent people. The museum's expenditures may reduce dividends to innocent investors or the funds available for future exhibitions. Such incidence requires ordinary distributive justification, but it is not identical to treating Birch's competing ownership claim as defeated. The constraint concerns an attempted extension of a particular reason. Alder's impaired opportunity gives the museum a reason to accommodate Alder; without more, it gives the museum no reason to treat Birch as less entitled.

The same distinction appears if relief takes a monetary form. An institution might undertake to pay both of two customers even though only one original debt existed. There need be no contradiction. Two present remedial obligations can arise from an evidential wrong concerning mutually exclusive historical claims. Whether full payment to both is proportionate is a further question. The crucial point is that a reason for making both payments need not establish that both original debts existed.

With an indivisible object, the difference becomes unavoidable. The institution cannot manufacture a second painting to satisfy both asserted titles. Treating reparative acceptance as historical certification conceals this difficulty rather than solving it. It turns an asymmetry between institution and claimant into an unsupported asymmetry between claimants.

## 5. Repair without a fictitious verdict

The two principles jointly exclude a familiar pair of responses. The museum cannot simply close both claims because the records are missing. It also cannot pick the first credible claimant, declare that family's account vindicated by the destruction, and use the resulting declaration against the other. What remains?

The immediate requirement is to distinguish control over the resolution from ownership of the object. The museum has had control of both. Its evidential wrong can undermine its entitlement to use that control for unilateral closure, even if the wrong does not establish that it lacks title. It should therefore fund and participate in a procedure whose authority does not depend on the museum treating its own evidential default as decisive.

In the hypothetical case, that could involve independent assessment of the remaining evidence, participation by both families in choosing practicable interim arrangements, preservation of the painting, and restrictions on sale while the dispute is considered. The museum's ability to retain revenues or determine exclusive access should be evaluated within that process, rather than assumed as the automatic consequence of possession. These measures do not presuppose that every allegation warrants an indefinite veto. Their basis is the conjunction of credible competing claims, the institution's established evidential breach, and a demonstrated impairment of the means of assessment.

Suppose the facts remain irrecoverable. A further decision may still be necessary: shared access, an agreed transfer, a sale with distribution of proceeds, or some other arrangement. The principles do not uniquely choose among these possibilities. A lottery might be fair under some conditions, but equal evidential support does not itself prove equal chances of historical ownership, and a lottery cannot be described as discovering the rightful owner. Its justification would concern a fair way to decide under an acknowledged constraint. If the parties' interests resist division and agreement is impossible, some reparative loss may remain uncompensated.

This is a substantive limit, not an embarrassment to be concealed. Wrongful destruction can make full repair impossible. A theory that always supplies a clean title by switching a burden has not necessarily repaired more; it may simply have erased the remaining person's complaint from its description of the outcome.

Boult argues that epistemic repair must attend to the relations within which people are heard and to the resources treated as authoritative, rather than assuming that those resources provide a neutral setting for repair (2026, §§2–3). The present argument identifies one precise institutional application. The museum cannot appoint its damaged recordkeeping procedure as the unquestionable arbiter of whether the damage matters. Yet replacing that procedure does not authorize belief beyond the evidence. What changes first is who may close the matter, on what grounds, and with whose participation.

There is also a duty concerning how the outcome is described. A decision might appropriately say that the museum must relinquish exclusive control because it disabled a fair assessment. It must not silently redescribe that decision as a discovery that Alder's version was true. Otherwise the remedial decision can later be used as evidence in a different dispute, where the original justification does not apply. A restriction on the museum becomes a supposed fact about Birch. Preserving the basis of the remedy helps prevent this migration of authority.

## 6. Four objections

The first objection is that the account merely repeats the maxim that nobody should benefit from wrongdoing. The maxim is an important starting point, but it does not distinguish the two families. Nor does it specify what an innocent successor must relinquish when she does not personally benefit. The dependence argument identifies the relevant institutional advantage: authority to treat a claimant's failure to use a protected means of proof as dispositive after disabling that means. The second principle limits what follows from removing that advantage. The combination is more informative than a general injunction against profit from wrongdoing.

The second objection concerns evidence. Destruction often does support an adverse factual inference. Someone who reads an unfavorable document and then burns it behaves differently from someone who would have burned any document. Why refuse to learn from this?

We should learn from it. The opening stipulation isolates an evidential wrong whose occurrence does not discriminate among the disputed acquisition histories. When destruction is selective, its pattern may increase the evidential support for a particular claimant. That support can bear on competing claims in the ordinary way. What cannot travel automatically is the separate reparative reason. If Alder has stronger evidence, including evidence supplied by the manner of destruction, treating Alder differently from Birch need not violate the constraint. It is justified by something the simplified case lacks.

The third objection says that the theory rewards fabrication and creates endless proceedings. Once a record has been lost, anybody can tell a story and demand an expensive remedy.

The principle requires more than a story and a missing record. There must be evidence of the institutional obligation and its breach, a credible connection between claimant and transaction, and a specific impairment of the relevant opportunity. These conditions must not themselves require the inaccessible record; that would reproduce the original obstacle. They can instead be supported by surviving correspondence, independently established dealings, credible testimony, or evidence about an identifiable class of affected transactions. Institutions remain entitled to examine that material and reject opportunistic claims on independent grounds.

Nor is the required response unlimited inquiry. Reasonable attempts at recovery can end. A procedure can employ proportionate deadlines and reach a binding practical resolution. The point is that the institution's own breach cannot be the sufficient justification for refusing accommodation. Finality may be justified by the costs of continued dispute, the rights of other parties, or the fairness of an agreed procedure. Those are additional reasons that must be defended. They are not supplied merely by announcing ignorance.

The fourth objection is the hardest. Under scarcity, even an independent process may have to decide against an innocent claimant. Has the second principle prohibited a decision that must be made?

No. It prohibits using the institution's evidential wrong, by itself, to justify a comparative disadvantage that it does not support. Scarcity can supply reasons for choosing among incompatible claims, but those reasons must concern the claimants and the scarce good: their evidence, urgent interests, fair prospects of access, or legitimate agreements. Responsibility for the evidential loss can separately determine who owes the costs of the procedure or of available compensation. The mistake is to treat the second determination as if it had already accomplished the first.

This separation may not produce a satisfying outcome in every case. It does make an unsatisfying outcome assessable. Birch can be told that a practical allocation was selected under unavoidable uncertainty, while retaining a complaint about the loss that prevented a better determination. Birch should not be told that the museum's wrong against Alder has refuted Birch's history. A justified resolution need not pretend to extinguish every moral remainder.

## 7. What successors must carry

Innocent successors need not inherit a belief, a memory, or another person's guilt. They can inherit a restriction on the use of institutional authority. Where a proof requirement depended on an opportunity that the institution wrongfully removed, the failure to meet that requirement no longer supplies an unqualified reason for closure. The institution must repair the opportunity, accommodate its loss, or justify closure on other grounds.

That restriction has a boundary. It does not make the claimant's historical account true, and it does not give the institution authority to use a reparative presumption against an independently credible rival. The institution may have to surrender unilateral control without being able to deliver a factual verdict. Such a result is neither acquittal by ignorance nor conviction by compensation. It is recognition that responsibility for uncertainty changes the terms on which decisions may be imposed, while leaving unanswered facts unanswered.
