# The Author of the Alternatives

## Consent, Baseline Capture, and Wrongs Without Wrongful Parts

---

## 1. The problem

Consent does remarkable normative work. A surgeon's incision is battery without it and treatment with it; taking your bicycle is theft without it and borrowing with it. Philosophers have spent considerable effort specifying when this transformation succeeds. The standard answer is a list: the consenter must be decisionally capacitated, adequately informed and understanding, and must act voluntarily — free of coercion, undue inducement, and problematic pressure (Eyal 2019, §1).

What these conditions have in common is that they are *synchronic*. Each concerns the state of the agent and her situation at the moment of consent. History enters only instrumentally, insofar as it bears on whether she is *now* competent, informed and unpressured. On the dominant picture, if the synchronic conditions are met, the consent is valid, and if the consent is valid it does its justificatory job.

I shall argue that this is wrong, and that the gap it leaves is not marginal. There is a structure — I call it a **ratchet** — in which one party obtains, through a sequence of transactions each of which satisfies every synchronic condition, terms that it could not have obtained at the outset and could not obtain by any single offer. Ratchets are not exotic. They are the normal form of long employment relationships, sponsored migration, platform dependence, franchising, and some kinds of intimate control.

Four claims, in order of increasing strength:

1. **Historicity.** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on the properties of the consent but on the causal history of the option set relative to which it was given — specifically, on A's contribution to that history.
2. **Comparative triviality.** The reason is that consent certifies only a comparison: that what was accepted beat the alternative. Where the offeror authored the alternative, the comparison is one he staged, and its satisfaction tells us nothing.
3. **Inverse reliance.** The justificatory weight A may place on B's consent varies inversely with A's contribution to the impoverishment of B's alternatives.
4. **Commitment.** A party whose course of dealing predictably impoverishes the other's alternatives can restore the justificatory force of the other's later consent only by having bound itself in advance. Consent-based justification therefore has a commitment precondition, which cannot be discharged by disclosure alone.

Along the way I offer a reading of Mill's argument against the slavery contract on which that argument proves considerably more than Mill drew from it.

## 2. Vesting

Mara is a software engineer. In 2016 she takes a job with Aurum, a large firm. Four episodes follow, each separated by roughly two years. Each is a single, discrete, plainly perceptible event; nothing here turns on imperceptible increments or on sorites reasoning.

**(1) Relocation.** Aurum offers Mara a substantial relocation payment and a subsidised mortgage to move to the small city where its main campus sits. She accepts. She is better off: she has money she would not have had and a house she could not otherwise have afforded. She is also, now, a specialist in a labour market with one dominant buyer.

**(2) Specialisation.** Aurum offers her a funded two-year programme in its proprietary toolchain, together with a promotion. She accepts. She is better off: the work is more interesting and the pay is higher. Her human capital is now substantially firm-specific; her value to Aurum has risen faster than her value to anyone else.

**(3) Deferral.** Aurum offers her a large equity grant vesting over eight years, in place of the smaller cash raise she would otherwise have received. On any reasonable estimate the grant is worth more. She accepts. Leaving now costs her the unvested remainder.

**(4) The ask.** Aurum asks Mara to take over a team whose product she believes to be harmful and whose engineering she considers shoddy. The hours are longer. There is no raise. Refusal will not get her fired; it will end her advancement. She accepts, and she is right to: given where she stands, accepting is better than refusing by a wide margin, and she knows it.

Stipulate that Aurum never lied, never threatened, never prevented Mara from leaving, and at steps (1)–(3) conferred real benefits that she wanted and that were, at the time, competitively priced. Stipulate that Mara was at every point competent, informed about the terms on offer, and under no pressure beyond the ordinary pressure of wanting things. Stipulate that her preferences never changed, that they were transitive throughout, and that at no point did she reason badly.

And note the comparative facts. At step (4), Aurum obtains labour on terms that Mara would have refused flatly in 2016. It obtains them without any of the devices that consent theory is built to detect. Had Aurum offered the whole package as a package in 2016 — relocation, specialisation, deferral, and then the ask — Mara would have declined, and Aurum knew it.

What, if anything, has gone wrong?

## 3. The toolkit acquits

Consider the resources standardly available.

**Coercion.** On the family of accounts descending from Nozick, a proposal is a threat rather than an offer if, compared with an appropriate baseline, it worsens the recipient's situation (Anderson 2023, §2.2). Aurum's proposals at (1)–(3) improve Mara's situation on any baseline, and (4) is a proposal she can refuse while keeping everything she has. The most promising refinement here is David Zimmerman's: in setting the baseline we should ask what the recipient would face "in the absence of special interference by the proposal maker" (Anderson 2023, §2.4). Zimmerman's illustration is a destitute islander offered a life-sustaining pittance by an employer who has been preventing the building of boats. That is exactly the right instinct, and it does not reach our case, for the decisive reason that Aurum *builds no fence*. It has not interfered with Mara at all. As the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry observes of Zimmerman's view, what appears to do the coercing there "seems to be vested in the offerer's actions that prevent the recipient from improving her situation or finding better offers elsewhere" (Anderson 2023, §2.4). Aurum performs no such actions. Every one of its contributions to Mara's position was a gift she accepted.

**Deception.** Nothing false was said. One might urge that Aurum withheld something: its assessment of where the sequence would end. But at steps (1)–(3) that was not a fact about the world; it was a fact about Aurum's own future discretion, which Aurum had not yet exercised and was not obliged to fix. A duty to disclose one's unexercised discretion is not a disclosure duty of the ordinary kind, and I shall argue in §8 that it is really a duty of a different species.

**Manipulation.** On the leading accounts, manipulation works through a defect: it induces beliefs, desires or emotions that violate the norms governing them (Noggle 2022, §2.2 on trickery), or exerts pressure toward akratic choice, or operates covertly in ways the target "could not easily become aware of" (Noggle 2022, §2.1, reporting Susser, Roessler and Nissenbaum). None of this fits. Mara's beliefs are true, her desires are her own and unchanged, her choices are not akratic, and she can see each transaction perfectly well. The *Encyclopedia*'s survey is revealing on a further point: the reviewed accounts are accounts of influences and acts. Manipulation has not generally been theorised as a property of extended sequences.

**Exploitation.** Exploitation theories locate a wrong in the unfair distribution of a transaction's surplus. Here each transaction was competitively priced at the time it was made. More tellingly, the discussion of exploitation has been dominated by the **nonworseness claim**, standardly credited to Alan Wertheimer: a transaction that is consensual, mutually beneficial, and harmless to third parties cannot be morally worse than not transacting at all. Every one of Aurum's four transactions satisfies the antecedent. The claim therefore acquits each of them — and, as we shall see, that is precisely the difficulty. It is also worth noting that in the exploitation literature the victim's want of acceptable alternatives is characteristically treated as something the exploiter *finds*: as Allen Wood puts it, the exploited party may lack acceptable alternatives "not because of the exploiter's act, but because background conditions deny the exploited acceptable alternatives." Aurum does not find Mara's want of alternatives. It makes it, out of benefits.

**Incapacity and adaptive preference.** Mara is not incompetent, and her preferences have not adapted to her cage. She wants in 2024 what she wanted in 2016. She simply cannot get it, and what she can get, she takes.

Every instrument in the kit returns the same verdict: permissible, permissible, permissible, permissible. I think that verdict is wrong, and that the interesting question is how it can be wrong when each of its conjuncts is defensible.

## 4. The structure of a ratchet

A small amount of apparatus makes the structure visible.

Let B deal with A at stages *i* = 1, …, *n*. At stage *i*, write **r**<sub>i</sub> for B's *reservation value*: the value to B of her best alternative to accepting A's proposal at that stage. Write **u**<sub>i</sub> for the value to B of accepting. A proposal is *acceptable* iff u<sub>i</sub> ≥ r<sub>i</sub>, and B's surplus from the transaction is u<sub>i</sub> − r<sub>i</sub>; A takes the rest.

The standard picture treats reservation values as **exogenous**: B brings her alternatives to the table, A's proposal is assessed against them, and consent given u<sub>i</sub> ≥ r<sub>i</sub> transfers permission. A ratchet is what happens when reservation values are **endogenous** — when accepting at stage *i* itself lowers r<sub>i+1</sub>.

That is exactly what Mara's four episodes accomplish. Relocation thins the set of employers she can reach; specialisation raises her Aurum-specific productivity relative to her general productivity; deferral attaches an explicit price to exit. Each is a benefit conferred and accepted. Each lowers the value of *not dealing with Aurum next time*.

Two observations follow.

**Observation 1 (failure of composition).** The property *consensual, mutually beneficial, and no worse for B than not transacting* is closed under sequential composition only if reservation values are exogenous. Where they are endogenous, u<sub>i</sub> ≥ r<sub>i</sub> can hold at every stage while u<sub>n</sub> < r<sub>1</sub>. The nonworseness claim, applied stage by stage, does not entail the nonworseness claim applied to the sequence.

This is not a quibble about aggregation. It means that a party who can influence the counterparty's alternatives can satisfy the nonworseness claim *at every step while walking the counterparty downhill*. There is no step at which the claim is violated, and no step at which the claim's satisfaction is any evidence that the counterparty is being well treated.

**Observation 2 (monotone capture).** Because A's share at stage *i* is σ<sub>i</sub> − (u<sub>i</sub> − r<sub>i</sub>), a falling r<sub>i</sub> lets A hold u<sub>i</sub> down while keeping the proposal acceptable. A can therefore capture a rising share of the surplus without ever making an unacceptable proposal.

I want to distinguish this structure carefully from three neighbours, because the differences are what make the case philosophically new.

**Not Quinn's self-torturer.** Warren Quinn's self-torturer faces a device with 1001 settings, where "the increments in current are so small that the self-torturer cannot tell the difference between adjacent settings" but where he is paid $10,000 for each advance and cannot go back (Andreou 2023, §1.4). He therefore prefers *s*+1 to *s* for every *s* and yet prefers 0 to 1000: his preferences contain a loop, and are intransitive. Everything philosophically load-bearing in that case comes from imperceptibility and intransitivity. Mara's case has neither. Her steps are large and discrete, her preferences transitive, her comparisons accurate. The self-torturer's problem is a defect in his preference structure; Mara's problem is a feature of her situation. And Quinn's puzzle is a puzzle about prudence, with no second party who benefits.

**Not a money pump.** The money-pump argument, from Davidson, McKinsey and Suppes, shows that an agent with intransitive preferences can be led through a series of individually preferred trades that leave her where she began with less money (Andreou 2023, §1.4). Again the engine is intransitivity, and again the victim is rationally defective. Mara is not. A ratchet pumps a perfectly coherent agent.

**Not Parfit's harmless torturers.** Parfit's cases of imperceptible contributions (Parfit 1984, ch. 3) distribute a harm across many agents each of whose contribution makes no perceptible difference. A ratchet involves two parties and perceptible differences throughout.

The closest structural relative is not in the ethics of consent at all. It is G. A. Cohen's analysis of proletarian unfreedom, to which I return in §8.

## 5. Why the consent goes idle

Grant the structure. What exactly has failed?

Begin with what consent tells us. When B consents to A's proposal, she certifies something — but something remarkably thin. She certifies a *comparison*: that, as she sees it, accepting beats her best alternative. She does not certify that the terms are fair, that the transaction is good for her, or that A has treated her decently. Ordinarily this thin certification is nonetheless powerful, because the alternative against which the comparison is made is *hers*: it is constituted by the world, by her other relationships, by her skills and savings and the offers of other people. A proposal that beats *that* is, for that reason, likely to be worth having, and the fact that she preferred it is a fact about her own situation to which deference is owed.

Now suppose A authored the alternative. Then B's "yes" certifies that A's offer beat A's arrangement, and *any* offeror who can lower the alternative can make the certification come out true. Call this the:

> **Comparative Triviality Principle.** Consent certifies that the consented-to option beat the consenter's best alternative. To the extent that the offeror authored that alternative, the certification is one the offeror can secure at will, and is to that extent justificatorily idle.

The analogy I find most exact is electoral. That a candidate won a free and fair vote is powerful evidence of the electorate's will — unless the candidate controlled the ballot. A landslide in a race whose other entrants you excluded tells us about you, not about the electorate. No individual ballot was invalid; every voter was competent, informed about the candidates who were standing, and unpressured. The election is nonetheless useless as a warrant for the winner's authority, and it is useless for a reason entirely internal to what elections are for.

This lets me say precisely what is and is not being claimed.

*Mara's consent is valid.* She really did permit Aurum to do what it did; Aurum commits no battery, no trespass, no breach. The normative power was exercised and it worked.

*Mara's consent does not justify.* Validity and justificatory force come apart. Aurum's defence of step (4) — "she agreed" — is unavailable, not because the agreement was defective but because the agreement's content is a comparison Aurum arranged. Aurum must therefore justify step (4) some other way: by showing that its terms are defensible against a baseline it did not author.

So the conclusion is a **burden shift**, not an automatic verdict of wrongdoing. If Aurum can show that Mara in 2024 is being paid what someone with her talents would command had she never dealt with Aurum, it has justified itself. If it cannot, it has wronged her. That is a substantive, checkable claim, and it is the shape that severance rules, notice periods, portability requirements and non-compete bans already have.

## 6. Mill's argument, generalised

There is a canonical passage that, read carefully, supports all of this — and that has been read as supporting far less.

In Chapter V of *On Liberty*, Mill considers why a contract of self-enslavement should be void. His reasoning is worth quoting in full, because everything turns on the rationale rather than the verdict:

> "The reason for not interfering, unless for the sake of others, with a person's voluntary acts, is consideration for his liberty. His voluntary choice is evidence that what he so chooses is desirable, or at the least endurable, to him, and his good is on the whole best provided for by allowing him to take his own means of pursuing it. But by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it, beyond that single act. He therefore defeats, in his own case, the very purpose which is the justification of allowing him to dispose of himself. He is no longer free; but is thenceforth in a position which has no longer the presumption in its favour, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it. The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom. These reasons, the force of which is so conspicuous in this peculiar case, are evidently of far wider application." (Mill 1859 [Gutenberg edn], pp. 194–95)

Three features of this argument deserve more attention than they usually get.

First, Mill's ground is **evidential**. Choice earns deference because it is *evidence* of the chooser's good. That is a striking thing to say, and it means the deference is defeasible in exactly the way evidence is: if the inference from choice to good is blocked, the deference lapses.

Second, the evidence is **renewed by occupancy**. Mill's complaint against the slave's position is that it "has no longer the presumption in its favour, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it." The warrant for treating someone's situation as good for them comes not from a single past act of choosing but from the *continuing* fact that they could leave and do not.

Third, Mill announces that the reasons are "of far wider application" — and then immediately retreats, observing that "a limit is everywhere set to them by the necessities of life, which continually require, not indeed that we should resign our freedom, but that we should consent to this and the other limitation of it" (p. 195).

I think the retreat is a mistake, and that Mill's own two premises show why. He is looking for a *threshold*: a quantity of freedom so large that alienating it defeats the point of protecting it. That is why he can find only the limit case. But the premises do not deliver a threshold; they deliver a *gradient*. If the warrant for deference is that choice is evidence of the chooser's good, and if that evidence is sustained by the ability to leave, then the warrant degrades continuously as the ability to leave degrades. Nothing in Mill's reasoning requires the ability to reach zero before the evidence becomes worthless.

Worse — and this is the part Mill did not see — his second premise is *exploitable*. If the presumption is afforded by voluntarily remaining, then anyone who can raise the cost of leaving can manufacture the presumption. Mara voluntarily remains, every day, at every stage. Her remaining is exactly as voluntary as Mill requires. It is also exactly as uninformative as the slave's would be, because Aurum has bought up the value of her going.

The right generalisation of Mill's argument is therefore not a bound on *how much* liberty one may alienate. It is a condition on *who arranged the alternatives*. The slavery contract is the limit case of a structure whose general form is the ratchet, and Mill's own reasoning condemns the general form.

A Kantian will recognise a cousin of this thought. Christine Korsgaard's criterion — quoted by Japa Pallikkathayil — is that "you treat someone as a mere means whenever you treat him in a way to which he could not possibly consent" (Pallikkathayil, §I, quoting Korsgaard). Aurum's case is disquieting precisely because it is the mirror image: Mara *actually* consents, at every stage, and something in the vicinity of the Kantian worry survives intact. Actual consent that the agent had no real alternative but to give does not obviously do better, as a mark of respect, than treatment one could not consent to. If the possible-consent criterion is meant to track whether another's agency has been co-opted, then a structure that procures actual consent by reshaping what the agent can possibly refuse is a case the criterion ought to catch and, as stated, does not.

## 7. Three principles

I can now state the positive view.

> **Historicity of Consent (HC).** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on the synchronic properties of the consent — competence, information, voluntariness — but on the causal history of B's option set, and specifically on A's contribution to it.

> **Inverse Reliance (IR).** The justificatory weight A may place on B's consent varies inversely with A's causal contribution to the impoverishment of B's alternatives.

IR needs a discriminating criterion, since *every* relationship alters the parties' alternatives. Deep friendships, marriages, apprenticeships and citizenship all reduce the attractiveness of exit; that is partly what they are for. The criterion is conjunctive:

> A's course of dealing with B **ratchets** iff (i) it predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A, and (ii) A uses the lowered reservation value to obtain terms more favourable to A than B's prior reservation value would have permitted.

Benign entanglements satisfy (i) and fail (ii). A spouse whose partner has given up a career for the marriage has acquired enormous bargaining power and characteristically does not use it; a mentor whose student's skills are idiosyncratically shaped by the mentor's own practice does not thereby extract worse terms. Indeed the natural thought is that relationships satisfying (i) generate a duty to abstain from (ii) — a fiduciary duty of *forbearance from baseline capture*. This inverts a common intuition: dependence does not license reliance on the dependent party's agreement; it withdraws that licence.

Finally, and most consequentially:

> **Commitment Condition (CC).** Where A's course of dealing predictably impoverishes B's alternatives, A may restore the justificatory force of B's later consent only by having bound itself in advance, in ways B can rely on, against using that impoverishment to extract better terms.

CC is where the account becomes interesting, because it shows that the natural remedy — *disclose more* — cannot work. Suppose Aurum in 2016 had told Mara the truth about relocation: "this will make you cheaper to us in eight years." That is a fact she can price. But what she would be pricing is Aurum's *future discretion*, which at that moment does not exist as a fact and which Aurum has not settled. Informing someone about a discretion is not the same as fixing it. What Mara needs at step (1) is not information; it is a *promise*. She needs Aurum to give up some of the latitude that the relocation creates.

So the duty generated by a ratchet is not a duty to inform but a duty to *bind*. This is unusual: most conditions on valid consent are conditions on the consenter's epistemic or volitional state. CC is a condition on the *offeror's* normative situation. It says that certain parties may only avail themselves of the consent defence if they have first disabled themselves.

## 8. Objections

**(a) "Mara was simply imprudent."** Perhaps she should have foreseen the trajectory in 2016. Grant it. The claim at issue is not that Mara is blameless but that *Aurum's* justification fails, and those are independent. A defective performance by B does not manufacture a warrant for A. Compare the money pump: whatever we say about the intransitive agent's rationality, it does not follow that pumping her is justified by the fact that she agreed to each trade. In any case the concession is smaller than it looks. Foreseeing at step (1) that Aurum *might* behave as it later did does not tell Mara what to do, since declining step (1) is worse than accepting it on every estimate available to her, and Aurum has not committed to the bad trajectory.

**(b) "This overgeneralises: all relationships shape alternatives."** Answered by the conjunctive criterion in §7. What distinguishes the ratchet is not the entanglement but the appropriation.

**(c) "Then which step is impermissible?"** This is the objection I take most seriously, and I want to answer it by widening the target. At least one of the following must go:

  (i) Each of Aurum's four acts is permissible.
  (ii) Permissibility agglomerates over sequences of acts by a single agent.
  (iii) Aurum's course of dealing is permissible.

I reject (ii). The property that makes the course of dealing objectionable — that Aurum's appeals to consent have gone justificatorily idle because Aurum authored the comparisons — is a relational and historical property that no single act instantiates. Compare: each belief in a set may be justified while the set is inconsistent; each coin toss in a game may be fair while the game is rigged; each shot in a boxing match may be legal while the fight is fixed.

But I do not need to insist. Suppose you prefer to reject (i) and say that step (4) is the wrong one. Then I ask *why*. Every synchronic fact about step (4) is benign: truthful proposal, competent consenter, free refusal, mutual benefit, no third-party harm. The only available answer cites the history — the fact that Mara's alternatives at step (4) are Aurum's product. But that answer concedes Historicity, which is the thesis. The disjunction is therefore stable: either permissibility fails to agglomerate, or synchronic consent-facts fail to determine permissibility. Either way the standard picture is false. What one may *not* say is that the synchronic facts settle each act and the acts settle the sequence.

Note also that a sorites diagnosis is unavailable. There are four steps, not a thousand; each is large; none is borderline. Nothing here is vague.

**(d) "You are covertly appealing to Aurum's intentions."** I am not, and this matters. The criterion in §7 refers to what a course of dealing *predictably does* and what a party *obtains by it*, not to what anyone meant. This is deliberate, because the most important ratchets have no designer. A platform whose network effects and data lock-in steadily reduce users' exit options, and whose terms deteriorate as a competitive response, ratchets without anyone intending it. A migration system that ties visa status to a single sponsor ratchets by operation of law. On my account these are genuine cases, and the duties they generate fall on whoever occupies the ratchet position and is able to act on it. There can be *blameless ratcheting* that nonetheless defeats the consent defence — because the defence is defeated by a structural fact about justification, not by anyone's culpability.

**(e) "This is paternalism."** It is the opposite. Paternalist conclusions restrict what the weaker party may do. My conclusion restricts what the stronger party may *say in its own defence*. Mara may accept step (4); nothing in this account interferes with her. What the account denies is that Aurum may cite her acceptance as a complete justification. Restricting a justification is not restricting an option, and the distinction is sharp: it is the difference between telling Mara she may not sign and telling Aurum that her signature will not, by itself, exonerate it.

**(f) "This is just Cohen's collective unfreedom."** It is an interesting cousin, and the differences are instructive. Cohen asks us to imagine ten people in a locked room with a single heavy key: "whoever picks up this key … and takes it to the door will find … a way to open the door and leave the room. But if he does so he alone will be able to leave it" (Cohen 1983, p. 9). Since nobody tries, each person is individually free to go, and yet "necessarily at least nine will remain" (p. 9). Cohen concludes that "though each is individually free to leave, he suffers with the rest from what I shall call collective unfreedom" (p. 11). His freedom of each is "conditional": each is free "only on condition that the others do not exercise their similarly conditional freedom" (p. 11).

This is a failure of aggregation *across persons and at a time*. Mine is a failure across *times for one person*. Cohen is also careful to insist that fully explicit attributions of freedom carry two temporal indices — one is forced *at t* to act *at t+n* (p. 8) — which is precisely the resource a diachronic account needs, and which he deploys to argue that most proletarians are *not* forced, because escape routes remain open long enough. The ratchet is the complement of his case: the exits are open at every moment and the agent nonetheless ends up where nobody would have sent her, because the person she is dealing with has been quietly buying the exits. Cohen's jailer installs photoelectric devices; Aurum installs nothing. It only gives.

**(g) "She was compensated for each erosion."** Suppose the relocation bonus, training and equity were priced to include a premium for lock-in, so that Mara received the full expected value of the bargaining power she surrendered. Does the complaint vanish? The *distributive* complaint may. The justificatory complaint does not, and seeing why is the sharpest test of the view. Payment does not undo staging. However well Aurum paid Mara in 2016, her "yes" in 2024 still certifies only that Aurum's offer beat Aurum's arrangement, and that certification is still one Aurum could have secured whatever it offered. What would dissolve the complaint is not payment but commitment — Aurum's binding itself, at step (1), against exploiting the lock-in it was creating. Which is CC, and which shows that CC is not a decorative addition to the view but its operative core.

## 9. What follows

Three things, I think.

**For the theory of normative powers.** Powers are usually individuated by their conditions of successful exercise, and consent's conditions have been theorised synchronically. The ratchet shows that a power can be successfully exercised and yet fail to transmit justification, because its *point* — here, the epistemic and authoritative relation between a person's choices and her good — has been defeated by the provenance of the conditions under which it was exercised. That is a general lesson about normative powers: their justificatory yield can be laundered. Promises, waivers, authorisations and delegations are all candidates for analogous treatment.

**For practice.** The burden shift of §5 has determinate institutional shape. Where a party predictably erodes another's alternatives, we should expect, and can defend, requirements that reconstruct an exogenous baseline: portable rather than firm-specific credentials, vesting on departure, notice periods and severance scaled to specific investment, bans on non-competes, data portability, visa status detached from a single employer, and cooling-off rules in long-term consumer contracts. These are usually defended as welfare measures or as corrections for bargaining power. The present argument gives them a different and stronger warrant: they are the conditions under which the stronger party's appeal to the weaker party's consent remains honest. They are not restrictions on freedom of contract but preconditions of its justificatory work.

**For moral theory more generally.** We are well supplied with vocabulary for wrongs whose wrongness is located in an act, and reasonably well supplied for wrongs located in a character or a policy. We are poorly supplied for wrongs located in a *sequence* whose every member is innocent. Parfit taught us that harms can be assembled from imperceptible parts. The ratchet teaches something adjacent but distinct: that *injustices can be assembled from perceptible, consensual, mutually beneficial parts*, without imperceptibility, without irrationality, and without anyone doing anything wrong at the time they do it.

Two uncertainties remain, and I do not want to disguise them. The first is measurement: the burden-shifted justification in §5 appeals to a counterfactual baseline — what B's alternatives would have been absent A's course of dealing — and such counterfactuals are hard to estimate and easy to contest. I take this to be a problem of implementation rather than of principle; courts already estimate comparable counterfactuals in wrongful-dismissal and reliance damages. The second is scope: I have argued that consent's justificatory force is history-sensitive, but I have not given a general theory of which histories matter. The conjunctive criterion of §7 is offered as a sufficient condition for a particular defect, not as an analysis. I suspect the family is larger — that promising, authorising and forgiving are all vulnerable to structurally similar capture — and that the general theory would be a theory of when a normative power's exercise is *independent* of the party who stands to benefit from it. That is the project this essay is a first instalment of.

Meanwhile the immediate lesson is blunt enough. If you want to know whether someone's agreement licenses what you are doing to them, do not only ask whether they agreed freely. Ask who wrote the alternatives.
