# What Asking Does: Requests, Answerability, and the Deliberative Agenda

*Final manuscript (final-003), condensed from final-002 and final-001; revised from `draft-001.md` in the light of `critique.md`. Earlier versions are preserved unchanged.*

## 1. Cheap to refuse, expensive to ignore

A colleague stops you in the corridor: "Could you look at my draft this weekend?"

Two things are true of this small event, and they do not sit together comfortably.

The first is that the request is cheap to refuse. "Sorry, I'm swamped" will do; so, often, will "Sorry, I'd rather not." You need not show that your weekend outweighs your colleague's need. You need not weigh anything at all.

The second is that the request is expensive to ignore. If you look through your colleague as though nothing had been said and walk on, you have wronged them — and you have wronged them even if, had you considered the request, you would have been entitled to refuse. The failure is not that you gave a consideration too little weight. It is that you did not take the matter up.

Something structural emerges. Whatever a request contributes is so slight that almost anything defeats it; yet it is so insistent that it cannot be passed over in silence. Considerations that almost anything defeats are normally the very ones we are permitted to ignore. Requests are not.

My thesis has three parts.

**(T1)** The act of requesting adds no weight to the first-order balance of reasons bearing on the requested act. Whatever favours your reading the draft — your colleague's need, your friendship, the good of the profession — would favour it exactly as strongly had the request never been made.

**(T2)** Requesting is nonetheless the exercise of a normative power on a second dimension. It puts an option on the addressee's *deliberative agenda* and makes the addressee *answerable* to the requester for what becomes of it.

**(T3)** The requirement so created is neither a first-order reason nor quite what Joseph Raz called a second-order reason. Raz defined second-order reasons as reasons to act, or to refrain from acting, *for* a reason. A requirement to put a question on one's agenda is neither. It bears on deliberative *uptake*, and deserves a category of its own.

T1 and T2 are independent; one could accept T2 while holding that requests also add weight. I shall argue that the second dimension does all the work that made the first seem necessary, and that the residual weight cannot be found. Some of morality's work is done not by adding weights but by settling what must be weighed at all.

## 2. The debate and its shared assumption

The literature asks one question: when I ask you to φ, does my asking *create* a reason for you to φ, or merely *reveal* one you had?

David Enoch's answer is that requests give reasons "robustly": the requester must intend to give a reason, communicate that intention, intend its recognition, and intend the addressee's reason to depend on that recognition — thereby making true the antecedent of a background conditional of the form "if X asks Y to Z, Y has a reason to Z."[^1] James Lewis adds that the request creates a reason only if the addressee places sufficient *discretionary value* in the requester — an attitude nobody is obliged to hold. Peter Schaber grounds the power in value, and confines it to respectful requests.[^2] Against these stands Daniel Weltman's epistemic account, on which a request creates no reasons and gives reasons only by revealing information: "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking" (Weltman 2023, §6).

One position matters more than its billing suggests. Micha Gläser groups Enoch, Lewis, and Lance and Kukla together as offering a "mere addition account" of requests, and argues — on the reports of two authors who have read him — that requests "do not create additional reasons, they rather put the requestees under an obligation."[^3] He is the nearest thing in the literature to someone joining a negative claim about added reasons to a positive claim about a distinct deontic change. I could not read him; §8 says where I must differ if the reports are right.

What unites these views is an assumption about where the action is. All ask what a request contributes to the *first-order balance*: a reason, or nothing, or an obligation. All answer one question.

The alternative surfaces once and is waved away. Considering the suggestion of N. G. Laskowski and Kenneth Silver that a request may make the addressee attend to reasons already possessed, Weltman replies that in such cases "there is nothing normatively special about the request"; a footnote concedes that a request "may technically have normative power insofar as you have more reason to attend to a reason brought to your attention, because (for instance) it would be rude to ignore someone's having brought it to your attention. But this normative power has nothing to do with the request being a request rather than any other action" (Weltman 2023, §5.2 and n. 40).

That footnote is where I plant a flag. The attention-directing effect of a request is not a by-product with a normative shadow. It is the whole of the request's distinctive work, and it has everything to do with the request being a request.

There is an older witness. Thomas Reid divided the powers of the mind into the solitary and the social, the latter being "such operations as necessarily suppose an intercourse with some other intelligent being" — among them asking a favour, giving a command, bearing testimony, promising. "To ask a question," he writes, "is as simple an operation as to judge or to reason; yet it is neither judgment nor reasoning, nor simple apprehension, nor is it any composition of these." His complaint was that in the usual divisions of the mind's operations "the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain."[^4] Reid's point was about the philosophy of mind; mine is its normative analogue. In the standard divisions of practical reasons — weights, defeaters, cancellers, enablers — the social operations have again been thrown behind the curtain, because the only place the theory has for them is the balance, and the balance is not where they live.

## 3. Five things to explain

**(D1) Cheap defeat.** A request may be refused for reasons that do not match its apparent importance, and without showing that the competing reason is weightier. As Lance and Kukla put it, "it is essential to the notion of a request that this reason is not an obligation."[^5]

**(D2) Costly silence.** Requests must be taken up. Treating one as though it had not been made is a distinctive failure — and a failure *even when refusal would have been permissible*. One can defy a request without disobeying it, which already suggests that requests make demands that are not demands to act.

**(D3) Repetition does not accumulate.** Asked three times, my reason to comply does not triple; if anything the case degrades into nagging. Ten people's needs aggregate, on most views; ten askings do not.

**(D4) The residue of refusal is an account, not a repair.** Breaking a promise calls for apology and often compensation. Refusing a request calls, at most, for an account of oneself — sometimes for a little more, help finding another helper, or an apology for curtness — but not for restitution, since nothing was taken.

**(D5) The silent sufferer.** Someone is drowning and cannot call out. Your reason to save them is not one jot weaker than if they could shout "Help!" Generalising: take a case where refusing seems seriously wrong, delete the request while holding fixed the underlying facts and your knowledge of them, and the wrong of not acting appears undiminished.

D5 supports T1 and is my most exposed claim; §6 tests it. D1 and D2 together are the explanandum.

## 4. Why weights are not enough

Call a view a **Weight Model** if it holds that a request contributes a first-order pro tanto reason of weight *w* to do the requested act. Enoch, Lewis and Schaber hold versions of this — though, and my earlier draft obscured this, none holds that contributing such a reason is *all* a request does. A hybrid is available off the shelf, and Schaber's requests already multi-task: they create non-obligatory reasons robustly, and can create obligatory ones by triggering background conditional obligations. So what follows is not a refutation. It is an argument that weights *alone* cannot deliver D2, and hence that a second dimension is needed whatever else is true.

**Horn 1: substantial weight.** If *w* is comparable to a modest inconvenience, D1 fails: I could not rightly refuse merely because I would prefer to see a film. Making *w* large enough to explain why requests grip us makes it too large to explain why they release us so easily.

**Horn 2: negligible weight.** So let *w* be an epsilon. Now consider two principles. *Idleness*: a consideration is idle in a context if the balance of reasons there is the same whether or not it is included. *Permitted Inattention*: a deliberator is not open to criticism for failing to attend to a consideration she reasonably takes to be idle. The second is not a concession to human frailty but a condition of deliberation: every choice sits in a field of vanishingly small considerations, and someone who attended to all of them would never act.

An epsilon is idle wherever some weightier consideration is present and the rest do not exactly balance. So in most cases you would be permitted to give the request no attention at all. That contradicts D2. On a hard deadline I know in advance that the request cannot change what I ought to do; the epsilon model therefore licenses leaving the message unopened and unanswered, which is exactly what one may not do. (An epsilon can break ties, which is why the model gets D1 right; but ties are rare and not identifiable in advance, so the reply saves the verdicts in a narrow class of cases while leaving the phenomenon unexplained. Nor does letting *w* vary with stakes and relationship help: the dilemma is pointwise.)

**Horn 3: the deontic retreat.** There is a better reply, and it is the one the literature makes. Lewis and Schaber explain discretion not by low weight but by a deontic category: "A reason is non-obligatory if it would not be blameworthy for one not to act on it, and obligatory if it would be blameworthy not to act on it."[^6] A reason can be weighty and non-obligatory; that is the point of the category. So the inference "refusal is blameless, therefore *w* is tiny, therefore idle, therefore ignorable" never starts. My earlier draft missed this.

But see what the retreat concedes. Requests are said to create reasons such that it is *never blameworthy not to act on them*. D2 says it *is* blameworthy not to attend to them. So we have a consideration that carries no liability to blame on the question of action and a firm liability to blame on the question of attention.

That combination is unavailable to a one-dimensional theory. On any weight-based account, the point of attending to a reason is to get the action right: inattention is criticisable because it risks acting wrongly. If failing to act on the request is never blameworthy, inattention cannot be criticisable for risking a wrong act — there is no wrong act to risk. The blame must have another ground. Locating it is my whole project, and the retreat has conceded that there is one. Nor is "non-obligatory reason" a resting place: it is not a weight but a deontic status defined by where blame falls, so whoever invokes it has already left the scale.

**What we defend against.** Set the dilemma aside and consider what we protect. We install do-not-disturb settings, unsubscribe links, gatekeepers, office hours, and conventions of indirection whose function is to let a request be withdrawn before it lands. We say "don't ask me that," and "I'm not going to ask, but —," precisely to prevent an agenda item from being created. None of this makes sense if a request adds a small weight: we do not build institutions against small increments to our reasons. All of it makes sense if a request conscripts attention and makes silence culpable. This is the strongest independent evidence for the two-dimensional picture, because it appeals not to intuitions about cases but to the shape of our practices.

## 5. The Agenda Thesis

> In requesting that S φ, A exercises a power to bring it about that:
> **(i) Uptake** — S is required to take up the question whether to φ, under the description "what A has asked of me";
> **(ii) Answerability** — if S does not φ, S must fail to φ *for* a reason she can offer A as the reason on which she acted;
> **(iii) Account** — S must actually render that account to A where A would otherwise be left without one.

**A burden of production, not of persuasion.** Before the request, S owed A nothing on the question whether to φ; it was not hers to answer. After it, she must put something forward — but only a reason, not a *weightier* reason. That is the shape of D1 and D2 together: the burden is easy to discharge and impossible to ignore, because discharging and ignoring are different relations to it.

This handles a worry about permissiveness. If any reason discharges the burden, does bigotry? "I won't help because I dislike people like you" is a reason, and the bigot can state it. But discharging a burden is not acting well: a burden of production is met by producing something of the right *type*, and does not certify what is produced. Compare a litigant who meets his burden with vile testimony — he has met it and disgraced himself. Discharging the request-burden makes S non-culpable *for ignoring A*; whether she is culpable *for refusing* is settled by the first-order balance the request never touched. That division of labour is the two-dimensionality in miniature.

**Clause (iii) is derived, not bolted on.** My earlier draft made Answerability counterfactual — S need only have a reason she *could* avow — and denied that actual speech was required. That will not do. S hears the request, silently considers it, silently declines because she is on deadline, and walks on without a word: counterfactual avowability is satisfied in full, yet this is precisely the wrong of looking through your colleague.

The response duty is not a further postulate. Answerability is a *relation to another person*: a standing liability to be called to account, discharged in whatever way circumstances allow. Where A cannot know whether S has taken the question up except by being told — the ordinary case, since A cannot see into S's head — the liability can be discharged only by S's telling her. So the response duty is what answerability comes to under normal epistemic conditions; and the account predicts, correctly, that it lapses where compliance is itself manifest, where A has said "no need to reply," where A is unreachable, or where reply would be absurd. A theory that simply postulated "you must reply" could not explain the exceptions.

**Sincerity, not mere avowability.** The reason offered must be the reason acted on. So stated this is a *sincerity* condition, and the name matters. It rules out pretexts: declining because one dislikes A while pleading busyness does not discharge the burden. It does not rule out randomising — "I had two equally good uses of Saturday and picked by lot" is an adequate account, since the lottery was adopted for a reason, namely indifference, and that reason is the one offered. What it rules out is drift: never engaging with the question, then finding afterwards that some excellent reason to refuse was available. Weights attach to the objective situation, not to what the agent acts on; burdens are discharged by agents, not by facts.

**Standing is a permission, not a success condition.** A stranger may ask me for directions but not for my afternoon. Schaber treats respectfulness as a condition on the *creation* of reasons; I take the other option. The ill-standing request lands, and that is why it is an imposition: the wrong is not that a reason was given and outweighed but that the requester conscripted attention and demanded an accounting without title. If nothing were created there would be nothing to complain of, and "you have no business asking" would be idle rather than apt — a challenge to standing, not a weighing, which presupposes that something was done. This also yields a *rival* explanation of Laskowski and Silver's datum that one can wrong someone precisely by requesting something of them: requesting is always the exercise of a power over another's deliberation, and powers can be abused.

**The structure of the second dimension.** Three questions, briefly. *Defeat*: the uptake requirement is defeasible, but not by the pointlessness of uptake (knowing I shall refuse is no licence to ignore) nor by small costs; it is defeated by incapacity and by more urgent conscriptions of attention. These are threshold conditions, not proportionality conditions. And when uptake is defeated, answerability is not extinguished but *deferred*: out of surgery I must still say, "Sorry — I was in theatre." Outweighed reasons leave no such residue. *Conflict*: ten requests in a morning compete for scarce attention, and I concede that something weight-like orders them — but weight governs the *order* of the queue, not its *membership*. On a pure weight model the ninth request is swamped and disappears; on mine it waits, and I owe the ninth person an answer as much as the first. *Interaction*: agenda requirements do not compete with first-order reasons. Exclusionary reasons *remove* inputs from the balance; requests do not touch the inputs, but require that a question be run through deliberation and that the agent stand behind its output. The relation is composition, not addition.

D3 now falls out: burdens do not stack, and one cannot become more answerable by being asked twice. Idempotence is relative to the question, which is the right prediction — asking again after six months, or after circumstances change, *does* renew the claim. Repetition within an unchanged situation adds nothing, which is why it reads as pressure rather than reason-giving. I withdraw my earlier jibe that a diminishing-returns function is "ad hoc": the honest comparison is that the burden model derives idempotence where the weight model must fit a curve.

## 6. Does asking add weight? Three hard cases

T1 rests on D5, not on §4, and the drowning swimmer is a weak argument: the underlying reason is overwhelming, so any increment would be swamped. So here are three cases built to break T1.

**Near-indifference.** Two strangers need a lift; one asks, the other visibly needs it and says nothing. I can take one. Has the asker the stronger claim? Something in the vicinity is true, but it is not weight. If I take the silent one I owe the asker an answer; if I take the asker I owe the silent one nothing. That is an asymmetry in *transaction cost*, and easy is easily mistaken for right. Test it: if the request added weight, driving the person in greater need would be choosing the less-supported option and would need justification on the merits — I would owe the asker not just an answer but a *defence*. I do not think I do. "Sorry, I'm taking her — she's further and it's raining" closes the matter.

**Withdrawal.** A asks, then says "actually, please don't." If asking adds no weight, withdrawing should subtract none; yet withdrawal can make φ-ing objectionable. This conflates two acts. "Please don't" is not cancellation but a second request with opposite content, which of course puts *not*-φ-ing on my agenda. Consider instead a genuine retraction — "never mind, forget I asked." There my reasons do revert to what my information supports, and the residue is what the account predicts: I am *released from answerability*, so reading the draft anyway is at worst officious. Retraction removes a liability; counter-request creates one; neither adds or subtracts a weight.

**Content-creating requests.** "Please don't tell anyone." Telling afterwards is betrayal; telling when I had merely surmised she would prefer silence is thoughtlessness. My earlier draft replied that such cases are consent rather than request — borrowed rather than argued. Here is a better reply. When A asks and I say nothing and let her go on, A has relied on me, and *reasonably* relied, because I did not demur. My silence functioned as acceptance, and the extra gravity comes from the undertaking, not the asking. Test it: if I say "I'm not promising anything — I'm a terrible gossip," and A tells me regardless, then telling later is indiscretion, not betrayal. Gravity tracks uptake, not address.

This matters twice over. T1 says the *act of asking* adds no weight; it does not say that nothing near asking ever does. Accepted requests create commitments, and commitments are weighty — refusal is cheap precisely because acceptance is what obligates. And the Agenda Thesis explains why tacit acceptance is possible at all: silence can count as acceptance only where silence is not neutral, and it is a request that makes saying nothing a move — the visible non-exercise of an available objection. On a weight model, silence before a request is no more significant than silence before a penny on the pavement, and the ubiquity of tacit undertakings is a mystery.

I do not claim this settles T1. It is a universal negative, defended case by case, and one clear counterexample defeats it. I claim only that the hardest cases I could construct come out right, and that in each the apparent weight of the asking belongs to something else: to information, to transaction cost, to a second address, or to an undertaking.

## 7. Address without a claim

How do we distinguish the Agenda Thesis from the epistemic account? Not by the obvious test of being asked versus overhearing the same need, which I have abandoned: overhearing varies common knowledge rather than mere information, and higher-order information is still information; and it brings independent norms against acting on what one was not meant to hear.

A cleaner instrument is already in the literature. Raz observes that requesting is not the communication of information, since "[i]t is possible ... to tell a person that, while I would like him to do something for me, I am not asking and am not going to ask him to do it."[^7]

> **The Disclaimed-Request Test.** (a) A says to S, "Could you read my draft this weekend?" (b) A says to S, "I'd love you to read my draft — your comments are always so helpful — but I'm not asking, and I'm not going to."

Here there is full information, full common knowledge, a genuine addressee, and no eavesdropping. Raz notes that requesting is intelligible precisely under such conditions, which "explains why one might request even when one knows that the other person knows of one's need and of one's desire for help."

What differs? Not whether S must acknowledge that A spoke: in both cases S must say *something*, since norms of conversational uptake apply to any address — which is exactly why they cannot distinguish the cases. The difference is that in (a) S must *close the question*: help, or decline. "I'll think about it" is a permissible deferral, but leaving the matter permanently open is itself a failure. In (b) S need not open the question. "That's kind of you to say" is a complete response; S may silently decide never to read the draft, never mention it again, and has wronged A in no way — though she may be criticisable for stinginess, a different charge on different grounds.

So the asymmetry is neither informational nor a matter of politeness. It is a difference in whether S is *answerable to A on a question*.

The epistemic account has a ready reply, already made by its author. Weltman writes that "when I request something, and someone acts on my request, I am to some extent responsible for this, whereas when someone acts on a wish, I am not," and calls this "a big difference." A footnote brackets it: "It is also a normatively important difference, but not in the sense of the normativity we are interested in when we ask why requests are normative. Similarly, if I accidentally reveal what cards I am holding in a game of bridge, I am less culpable than if I intentionally do so, but in each case the cards I reveal give my opponents identical reasons to play certain cards in order to win."[^8]

Three replies. First, on the question Weltman poses he is right, and T1 agrees: the request does not alter how strongly S is favoured toward φ-ing. We disagree about whether that question exhausts the subject. Second, the bracket is a definition of the topic, not a finding about requests. "The normativity we are interested in" is stipulated as the addressee's reasons to φ — precisely the axis I say is wrong. A question belongs to practical normativity if it concerns what an agent is required to do and criticisable for failing to do; the duty to answer is a duty to perform an action, failing it is a practical failure, and it wrongs a specific person with a standing to complain that nobody else has. Third, the bridge analogy misdescribes the structure. There the difference in culpability attaches to *me*, the revealer; my opponents' reasons are untouched and nothing is required of them. In the request case the liability falls on the *addressee*: it is S, not A, who must now do something she need not otherwise have done. One may bracket the speaker's responsibility for the hearer's conduct and still owe an account of a requirement falling on the hearer.

## 8. Where this belongs among second-order reasons

My earlier draft claimed that Raz's definition names a genus with two species, that he developed only the negative one, and that the positive slot was unoccupied and unnamed. The second claim is right; the third is wrong, and correcting it improves the view.

Raz's definition, as quoted by Christian Piller, runs: "A second-order reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason."[^9] The positive species is neither nameless nor empty: it is standardly called a *positive second-order reason*. Daniel Whiting, attacking the apparatus, frames the target as the idea that there are second-order reasons to φ, or not to φ, on the basis of certain first-order reasons; Kenta Sekine has recently defended the category with a fourfold typology of positive second-order reasons, sorted by whether the further value is contingent or constitutive and whether it coincides with the basic one.[^10] The slot is furnished.

What survives is better. Set the Agenda Thesis's clauses against Raz's definition. Clause (ii) — S must fail to φ only for a reason she can offer as her own — *is* a second-order reason in Raz's sense: it constrains the reasons for which S may act, and it is positive. Clause (i) — S must take up the question — is *not*: considering is not acting for a reason, and a requirement to put a question on the agenda bears on *deliberation*, on what gets weighed, rather than on the reasons for which one acts. Raz's disjunctive definition does not reach it in either direction.

I therefore drop the label "inclusionary reason" I earlier proposed, and the symmetry it suggested. The better claim is less tidy and more interesting: requests reveal a category of *agenda reasons* — reasons bearing on deliberative uptake — that the standard taxonomy does not contain.

That the category is missing rather than undeveloped shows inside Raz's own motivating case. Piller's exposition of the tired investor is that the agent's refusal "is based on a reason not to consider the merits of the offer" — a reason about uptake, then classified as a reason not to *act for* the excluded considerations. The two are run together at the point of origin. And requests are where the received Razian picture finds nothing to say: Michael Moore, surveying the phenomena Raz took exclusionary reasons to illuminate, notes that threats and requests are illuminated by the *absence* of such reasons, since "unlike a promise, say, or an order issued by one in authority, a mere request does not preclude one from weighing the pros and cons of doing the action requested."[^11] That is right, and exactly half the story. A request excludes nothing. It also does something second-order, on an axis the framework lacks.

Two acknowledgements. The coherence of second-order reasons is under live attack; Whiting argues the idea is incoherent. My account is not hostage to that dispute, which is a virtue rather than an evasion: clause (i), which does most of the work, is not a reason to act for a reason at all and survives untouched; only clause (ii) would need recasting, as a condition on what counts as discharging the burden. And "answerability" is a term of art in the theory of moral responsibility, naming one member of a trichotomy alongside attributability and accountability; I use it narrowly, for a standing liability to be called to account by a particular person on a particular question, and intend no commitment there. The nearest relative of my positive thesis is Stephen Darwall's claim that authority requires answerability and that second-personal address is sui generis; I could read neither him nor Raz's reply, and record the debt as unpaid.[^12]

Finally, Gläser. If the reports are right that he denies requests create additional reasons and holds that they obligate the requestee, we agree on the negative claim and differ on the positive one — and the difference is testable against D1, since an obligation to φ predicts that non-compliance is blameworthy, which is just what the Lewis–Schaber category of non-obligatory reasons denies. What is obligatory, on my account, is not φ-ing but taking the question up and standing behind the answer. Whether Gläser would say something similar I do not know; this is where an informed reader should press hardest.

## 9. Conclusion

If agenda reasons exist, requests are not their only source. A question creates an obligation to answer that is cheap to discharge ("I don't know") and, where the asker has standing, costly to ignore — the standing apparatus doing the expected work, since rhetorical, hostile and intrusive questions may be ignored precisely because the asker lacks title. Legal standing, the right to be heard and the right to petition are powers to put a claim on an institution's agenda rather than powers to win, which is why a hearing one was always going to lose is nonetheless owed; I offer this as a puzzle the picture is placed to address, not as a verdict on a literature I have not read. And much protest is not an attempt to shift a balance of reasons — those are usually known already — but an attempt to force a question onto an agenda. The complaint of the unheard is not "you weighed us too lightly" but "you did not even consider us": unintelligible if weight is the only dimension, straightforward if it is not.

Asking adds nothing to the scales. It puts a question on your agenda and makes you answerable for what becomes of it — not a deflationary result but a redirection, since the power to conscript another's attention and make their silence culpable is among the most consequential we have over one another. The cost is a symmetry I would have liked to claim and cannot: what a request creates is not the mirror image of a Razian exclusionary reason but something the standard taxonomy has no slot for. Reid thought the social operations of mind had been thrown behind the curtain. So, I think, have the social operations of practical reason.
