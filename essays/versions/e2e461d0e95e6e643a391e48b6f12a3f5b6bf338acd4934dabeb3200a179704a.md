# Inquiry Before the Question

*Final manuscript (final-001), revising draft-001 in light of the critique.*

**Abstract.** Norms of inquiry are standardly indexed to questions: what an inquirer ought to do is
fixed relative to some Q. I argue that this indexing cannot govern the stage at which formulating
the question is itself the work, and that a question-indexed norm reaches that stage only by
borrowing its content from elsewhere. I propose that inquiries are individuated, and their norms
indexed, by *aporiai*: sets of an agent's own commitments that she cannot, from her present
position, jointly discharge. Because an aporia is fixed by commitments she already has, it can be
the parameter of a norm governing the very work that produces the question — which no question can
be. The model recovers question-indexed norms as the case of informational gaps, separates the apt
double-checker from the compulsive one, makes zetetic negligence intelligible, and answers Meno's
second horn. Dewey anticipated much of the diagnosis; the indexing claim and the machinery are new.

---

## 1. The unit of inquiry

Epistemology has been enlarging its subject matter. Instead of asking only what an agent should
believe at a moment, it asks how she should conduct the extended activity that produces her beliefs.
Jane Friedman calls the norms governing that activity *zetetic*, and her complaint against the older
picture is well put: there is "a kind of temporal parochialism to normative epistemology as it's
currently done that is not obviously well motivated. What's so special about the moment of belief
formation?" (Friedman 2020a: 26). The complaint is right. My claim is that the framework built to
answer it inherits a version of the same defect, one stage further back.

That framework treats a *question* as the unit. Friedman's zetetic instrumental principle says: "If
one wants to figure out Q?, then one ought to take the necessary means to figuring out Q?" (2020a:
503). Her ignorance norm says that "subjects shouldn't both know Q? at t and have an interrogative
attitude towards Q? at t" (2020b: §3.1). Her diagram of an inquiry runs from the moment a question
goes onto the agenda, through active investigation, to settlement (2020b: §2). Call the underlying
commitment **Erotetic Primacy**:

> **(EP-i)** *Constitutive.* To inquire at t is to be, at t, in an interrogative attitude towards
> some question Q.
>
> **(EP-ii)** *Indexing.* The norms governing an inquiry take that question as a parameter: what the
> inquirer ought to do, and whether she is doing well, is fixed relative to Q.

Friedman does not assert (EP-i) unrestrictedly. She notes that "[t]here may well be parts of inquiry
during which we don't quite have a clear question before our minds", her focus being on
"determinately question-directed inquiry" (2020a: 9–10); she does not "mean to be committing to the
claim that all inquiry is question-directed in this way" (2020b: n. 1); and she wonders whether
inquiry might begin "before S has even really formulated a question they want to answer and is just
poking around somewhat more aimlessly", adding: "I'm not sure there's a clear answer" (2020b: §3.2).

So the target is not a straw man, and the hedge is not innocent either: it protects (EP-i) and
leaves (EP-ii) where it was. Every norm specific to inquiry *as inquiry* — the instrumental
principle, the ignorance norm, the coherence constraints on research agendas — takes a question in
its parameter slot, and each is therefore silent in the region Friedman brackets.

One qualification, which draft-001 of this paper got wrong. Not every proposed norm of inquiry is
question-indexed. David Thorstad's general account is not: its parameter is an act and the reasons
the agent possesses, so that "S's X-ing at t is rational if and only if in X-ing at t, S responds
correctly to the normative reasons that she possesses at t" (Thorstad 2023: §2). But that is the
absence of an indexing rather than a rival one — inquiry becomes one activity among others, and the
account is deliberately not an account of what individuates an inquiry, as Thorstad grants in facing
the objection that he "succeeds by changing the subject" (2023: §6). Among accounts that treat
inquiry as a distinctive normative kind, question-indexing is the standard; the alternative on offer
is to give up the kind, not to re-index it.

I want to re-index it. What individuates an inquiry, and what its norms should be indexed to, is an
**aporia**: a set of the agent's own commitments that she cannot, from the position she is in,
jointly discharge. Inquiry is the activity of removing an aporia by improving that position.
Questions are instruments for that work, but they are products of inquiry at least as often as
starting points. The thesis is a generalization, not a replacement: question-indexed norms are the
special case that arises when the obstruction is a missing item of information within a space the
agent already commands, and the model says what that case is a special case *of*.

## 2. The formulation problem

**(1) Ana.** Ana comes away from her weekly meeting with a colleague feeling flattened. This happens
for months. She believes nothing false about the meetings; she has no complaint she could state.
Something is wrong and she cannot say what. She replays exchanges, tries descriptions that do not
fit ("he interrupts me" — he doesn't; "he disagrees with me" — he does, but so do others,
harmlessly), notices a pattern in *when* the flattening occurs, and eventually arrives at a
formulation: *is he treating my objections as complaints?* Once she has that question, answering it
takes one meeting. The months were not a prelude to the inquiry. They were the inquiry.

**(2) The uneasy reader.** A reader works through an argument that seems valid, whose premises seem
true, and whose conclusion he cannot accept. He suspects no particular step. After a week he locates
an equivocation between the second and fourth premises. Before that there was no question he was
trying to answer; afterwards, none left to answer.

**(3) The intermittent fault — a boundary case.** An engineer is handed a service that fails once a
day, with no pattern in the logs. Draft-001 offered this as a third case of the same kind; it is
not. If the failure lies in a space of components and conditions she already commands, she has a
good question — *which component?* — with a surveyable answer-set, and bisection is a textbook case
of taking the necessary means to it. Her case becomes Ana's only when no partition she commands has
a true cell, so that she must make a distinction rather than locate a value.

Cases (1) and (2) share what I call the **formulation problem**. At the times that matter there is
no question Q such that (a) the agent has an interrogative attitude towards Q and (b) her activity
is well described as taking the means to figuring out Q. This is not because she is idling. She is
working, and working well or badly: Ana could have been lazier or less honest; the reader could have
given up. There is zetetic quality on display, and none of it is quality *relative to a question*.

I do not need the further claim, made in draft-001, that this is where most inquiry happens; that
rested on vignettes plus an unsourced remark about scientific anomalies, and both are cut. The
generalization thesis needs only that the region is real and that competence in it is assessable.

## 3. Why the question cannot be the parameter

### 3.1 The dilemma of grain

First reply: Ana does have a question, namely *what is going on in these meetings?* So (EP-ii)
applies. But the guidance a question-indexed norm delivers is a function of the answer-set the
question determines — the partition its complete answers induce. So: either the agent's question
determines an answer-set she grasps, or it does not.

Take the first horn. In these cases she does not. Ana cannot, at month two, characterise the
candidate complete answers to *what is going on?*; if she could she would be nearly finished, since
what she eventually achieves *is* the specification of one candidate. To say she grasps the
answer-set is to say she already has what the inquiry was for. This horn is the important one, and
nothing below touches it.

Take the second horn: she has the question, but its complete answers are unsurveyable. Draft-001
said the norms then go idle, since almost nothing is a necessary means to figuring such a question
out. That was too quick in two ways.

First, necessity is modal, and coarse ends do have necessary means. Ana cannot figure out what is
going on without attending to the meetings, retaining what was said, and declining to deceive
herself about it; the instrumental principle forbids omitting these. But these are the necessary
means to *any* coarse end whatever. Such a norm is not vacuous; it is non-discriminating, which is
the defect that matters. What separates Ana's good months from her bad ones is not whether she
attended but *what she attended to*.

Second, Friedman flags that the necessary-means formulation is a deliberate weakening: "While on the
question of strength I am partial to a maximizing norm, we only need a weaker principle for the
arguments to come, so I've formulated ZIP as a demand to take the necessary means to our epistemic
ends" (2020a, preprint §3). A maximizing norm — take the *best* available means to figuring out Q? —
does discriminate. Does it rescue the architecture?

No, and seeing why is the heart of the matter. To rank means as better or worse *for figuring out Q*
is to rank them by expected progress towards settling Q. Where Q's answer-set is unsurveyable, the
agent has no independent handle on expected progress. What she has instead is a judgement about
where the trouble lies: she tracks *when* the flattening occurs rather than cataloguing *what was
said*, because she suspects the defect is in uptake rather than content. That judgement does the
ranking. So the maximizing norm is not false in the pre-question region; it is **parasitic** there,
converting a judgement about the defect into a verdict about means and contributing nothing to the
judgement. Hence a generalization rather than a refutation: question-indexed norms work on their own
where the answer-set is surveyable, and on loan where it is not. A theory of inquiry should say what
the loan is taken out against.

### 3.2 The externally individuated question

Second reply: the question individuates the inquiry from outside; Ana's inquiry was all along into
whether her colleague treats her objections as complaints, whether or not she could say so. Three
difficulties. Instrumental norms bind through the agent's own end, and one cannot take the necessary
means to an end one cannot identify. Identity becomes retrospective and success-dependent: if Ana
never reaches the formulation, hers was not a failed inquiry but a non-inquiry — yet failed gropings
are criticisable in the same terms as successes. And it under-individuates, since an inquirer passes
through several questions on the way and nothing in her pre-formulation state selects one of them.

## 4. The formal rescue

Decision theory can evaluate questions by the expected value of the information they would yield,
and Friedman notes that this machinery may bear on norms for opening questions (2020b: n. 12). But
it takes as given exactly what the inquirer lacks: expected value of information is computed over a
fixed algebra with a prior on it, and a candidate question must already be a partition of that
algebra. What Ana needs is not a ranking of the partitions in her current space but a new
distinction — not an update on an algebra but a change of algebra. Nor can one evade this by putting
the finer partition into the algebra from the start, since to have it in the algebra *is* to have
the concept: an agent who can represent *he treats my objections as complaints* well enough to
assign it a prior has already done Ana's months of work.

The gap is recognised in work on awareness growth. As Joe Roussos puts it, "in Bayesian models all
resolutions of uncertainty take place by updating pre-existing beliefs … and so Bayesianism leaves
no room for agents to learn about genuinely new states of affairs" (Roussos forthcoming: §1); he
distinguishes *awareness revision*, "the formation of a new subjective awareness state, or space of
subjective possibilities", from *belief extension* on the new space (§1). His own contribution is "a
formal model of awareness revision", offered as "a partial proposal" (abstract), so the point is not
that nobody models awareness revision; it is that such a model is not yet a norm an inquirer can act
under, since it says how awareness states are related and not which distinction this agent, stuck in
this way, has reason to look for.

This is the formal echo of Meno's second horn: "And how will you enquire, Socrates, into that which
you do not know? … And if you find what you want, how will you ever know that this is the thing
which you did not know?" (*Meno* 80d, trans. Jowett). Where the answer-set is the achievement, a
criterion requiring it in advance cannot be applied; §6.3 supplies another.

## 5. The aporetic model

### 5.1 Position, commitment, aporia

By an agent's **commitments** I mean whatever she is answerable for: her beliefs and acceptances,
the presuppositions of her plans and of the questions she does ask, her intentions, her
expectations, and the standards to which she holds herself. Commitments are *discharged* when they
are met — a belief by being true, a plan by being executed, a standard by being satisfied. By her
**position** I mean three things together: her information; her conceptual repertoire — the
distinctions she can draw and the propositions she can entertain; and the modes in which she holds
what she holds, that is, with what security, on what basis, and checked to what degree.

> **Aporia.** A set Γ of S's commitments is an aporia for S at t iff there is no exercise of S's
> capacities from her position at t that discharges every member of Γ.

> **Inquiry.** Inquiry is activity aimed at removing an aporia by improving one's position.

Joint dischargeability can also be restored by abandoning a commitment or by changing one's
circumstances. Those are repairs but not inquiries. Inquiry is the species of repair that proceeds
by finding out, by distinguishing, or by coming to understand.

This is one notion with a structured parameter, which answers a charge draft-001's version invited:
that "cannot discharge as she stands" was silently disjunctive, meaning *the contents cannot all
hold* in some cases and *she cannot see how to hold them together* in others. There is one notion —
impossibility of joint discharge from a position — and three ways the position can be deficient.

**Conflict.** The contents cannot all hold, so no position would do. The uneasy reader's case is of
this kind: he accepts each premise, accepts the validity, and rejects the conclusion.

**Gap.** The contents are consistent, but her position does not determine what discharge requires.
Gaps come in two kinds, and the distinction carries much of the weight of this paper. An
*informational* gap is a missing value in a space she commands: her plan specifies one action if p
and another if not-p, and she cannot tell which. A *conceptual* gap is a missing space: the
reconciling description is not in her repertoire. Ana's is a conceptual gap. Her three commitments —
to her colleague's good faith, to her own reliability as a reader of a room, and to the flattening
being real — are perfectly consistent, as her resolution shows; what she cannot do is discharge all
three at once, having no description under which they sit together. (Draft-001 called her case a
Conflict. That was an error, and it is what made the taxonomy look disjunctive.)

**Strain.** The content is fine and the information is there, but the *mode* of holding falls short
of a standard she also holds: she believes the calculation is right, and also holds herself to not
acting on unchecked arithmetic where a mistake would cost someone money.

The taxonomy predicts the reach of the erotetic framework. Informational gaps are exactly the case
in which a question with a surveyable answer-set is already available — which is why its stock
examples (where are my keys, how many windows) are all of that kind. Conflicts and conceptual gaps
are the cases in which the question is the achievement.

### 5.2 Where questions come in

A question is not what an inquiry is *of* but a tool it may acquire. To formulate one is to commit
to a *shape* for the defect: to say that the obstruction will go once we settle which cell of this
partition obtains. A good question is one whose settlement would restore dischargeability and which
the inquirer can settle; a bad one is well formed, answerable, and beside the point. This explains
why arriving at a question can be an inquiry's main achievement — it encodes a diagnosis — and why
inquiries succeed without answering any question posable at the outset. An aporia can be removed by
answering a question, but also by rejecting a presupposition (there was no fault: the monitor was
miscounting), by revising a standard, or by drawing a distinction that lets the conflicting
commitments live together (he is not disputing my objections, he is receiving them under a different
description). Only the first is question-answering.

### 5.3 The norms

> **(AX) Indexing.** The parameter of a zetetic norm is an aporia. An agent has reason to inquire at
> t only if she then has an aporia whose removal the inquiry would serve, and it is that aporia, not
> any question, that fixes what counts as proceeding well.

> **(AIP) Aporetic instrumental principle.** If one has an aporia, one ought to take the necessary
> means to removing it.

> **(LOC) Localization.** Where one cannot articulate one's aporia, articulating it is a necessary
> means to removing it. So (AIP) requires localization work, and ranks probes that discriminate
> between candidate localizations above probes that do not.

> **(FEAS) Feasibility.** Inquiry is the apt repair for an aporia only if some attainable
> improvement in one's position would remove it. Where none would, the aporia calls for revision of
> the commitments, not for inquiry.

> **(PR) Priority.** The urgency of an aporia is a function of the range of one's other commitments
> that cannot be discharged while it stands.

### 5.4 Why an aporia can be a parameter where a question cannot

The obvious objection is that the dilemma of grain recurs. Either Ana grasps her aporia finely, in
which case she has most of the diagnosis and is nearly done; or she grasps it coarsely — "something
here doesn't fit" — which is true of every reflective adult at all times and tells her nothing.
Draft-001 had a reply in reserve: that an agent has standing access to her own commitments in a way
she does not have to an answer-set she has not conceived. I withdraw it. It is false in the flagship
case: the commitment Ana must articulate — *I am a reliable reader of what is going on in a room* —
is one she discovers by finding it under strain, and stating it is about as hard as stating the
question.

The asymmetry is not about access but about what a norm's parameter can be fixed by. A norm can be
indexed to a parameter the agent cannot articulate, provided the parameter is fixed by something she
already has. An aporia is so fixed: it is constituted by commitments she has and the position she is
in. So there is a fact about which of her commitments are not jointly dischargeable, independent of
her ability to state it — which is why her candidate articulations are answerable to something and
can be wrong. "He interrupts me" is not an unpromising question; it is a *false localization*,
falsified by the very commitments that constitute the defect: she attends to the next meeting, he
does not interrupt, and the flattening persists. A question of the right grain is not so fixed.
Before she makes it, there is no fact about which of the indefinitely many refinements of *what is
going on?* is hers. A norm indexed to the fine question has no parameter to take, and a norm indexed
to the coarse question reaches the choice of refinement only parasitically.

That asymmetry converts into guidance of a modest kind, via (LOC). Ana at month two might replay
exchanges, try further descriptions, or track when the flattening occurs. The first two generate
candidate localizations; the third discriminates between them, so once candidates are on hand (LOC)
ranks it above them.

Here is a verdict the two models do not share. Suppose Ana at month three adopts the question *does
he interrupt me?* and settles it: he does not. On the erotetic model this is a completed inquiry — Q
was opened, the means taken, a complete answer is known, and the ignorance norm now forbids
inquiring into it further. Nothing marks the episode as a failure, because by those lights nothing
failed. On the aporetic model the inquiry failed: the aporia stands. And the model says what to do
with the failure — the persistence of the flattening after a true negative answer is evidence about
the *location* of the defect, and directs the next localization away from conversational mechanics
and towards uptake. The erotetic model can only say "open another question".

So the guidance on offer is comparative and local: it ranks probes, it says what a failed
localization is evidence for, and — via (FEAS) — it says when to stop inquiring and start revising.
That is less than an algorithm and more than a redescription.

## 6. Three payoffs

### 6.1 Double-checking, and when to stop

Elise Woodard has argued persuasively that one can know that p and rationally double-check p,
seeking certainty, resilience, or sensitivity to error (Woodard 2024: §§1–3). The model endorses her
data. Draft-001 called her diagnosis — that there are epistemic goods beyond knowledge worth seeking
— "true but unlimited", and claimed to improve on it by supplying a stopping rule. That was unfair
and I withdraw it: her first footnote says that "at a certain point, checking again is irrational"
and refers the reader to "Friedman (2019a) and Woodard (forthcoming)". She brackets the question.

One of the works she points to answers it, and not in an agent-relative way. Friedman argues that
checking cycles through suspension and belief, and that a checker whose position keeps improving
eventually enters the SPECs zone — circumstances "too good or strong for suspension of judgment" —
after which "they can only continue checking by having an unjustified doxastic attitude" (Friedman
2019: §4, n. 21).

The aporetic model adds not a rival ceiling but the structure below it. Double-checking is apt when
there is a *strain* aporia: a standard the agent holds herself to that her present holding of p does
not meet. That is a claim about *this* check at *this* time, which an appeal to permanently
available epistemic goods cannot supply. It yields a scaling rule — raise the stakes, or introduce a
reason to fear a specific error, and you introduce a standard the current holding fails, hence a
fresh aporia and a fresh permission — and it yields a stopping point, since when the standard is met
the aporia is gone.

The hard case is the compulsive checker, and draft-001 got it wrong. Her standard — certainty that
the door is locked — is one no amount of checking meets, so she has a *standing* strain aporia; and
since it blocks her leaving the house and keeping appointments, (PR) ranks it *high*. The model
appeared to license, and even prioritise, the pathology. (FEAS) is the repair, and it is not ad hoc:
it falls out of inquiry's being the species of repair that proceeds by improving one's position. No
attainable improvement removes her aporia, so her checking is defective precisely *as* inquiry, and
what her urgent aporia requires is the feasible repair — revision of the standard. Friedman's own
description points the same way: pathological checkers are on "wavy trajectories", where
"information is gained at the check but then later lost" (2019: n. 10), which is a failure of
exactly the condition (FEAS) states.

The model also explains the appeal of the ignorance norm: for informational gaps the aporia just
*is* the ignorance, so the two agree, and the ignorance norm is a correct special case mistaken for
the general rule.

### 6.2 Zetetic negligence

We criticise people for not inquiring. A manager whose stated standards and whose actual practice
cannot both be honoured, and who has never once put the matter to himself, is open to a criticism
that is not merely moral. This is awkward for a question-indexed architecture, whose norms are
indexed to a question he does not have. Draft-001 said the natural repair would require "a norm for
opening questions that does not presuppose already having them, which is exactly what (EP-ii) cannot
supply without circularity". That overstated the charge. Friedman supplies coherence constraints
that generate requirements to open questions from other questions one has: "if you're wondering
about whether Joe or Ali went to the party, then plausibly you should also be wondering about
whether Joe went to the party or wondering about whether Ali went" (2020b: §3.1). Not circular —
limited, since it cannot reach the manager, who has no relevant question open. Limited is the
accurate charge.

On the aporetic model the criticism is straightforward and its ground is the agent's own. An aporia
is constituted by commitments he already has; it does not wait on his noticing. Negligence is the
failure to detect, or the refusal to acknowledge, a defect of one's own; and because aporiai need
not be felt, a person can be criticised precisely for the absence of the feeling. Whether this makes
the norms *epistemic* I leave open. Thorstad holds that "[t]here are no epistemic norms of inquiry.
We have no epistemic duties to open inquiry and no epistemic duties to close it" (Thorstad 2022:
§1). The model locates the ground of criticism in a defect internal to the agent's commitments
rather than in her interests, which is a more plausibly epistemic ground; but since commitments
include intentions and plans, joint undischargeability may equally look like a defect of agency.

### 6.3 A negative criterion of success

On the erotetic model success is believing a complete answer to Q, which requires possessing Q's
answer-set in advance. Where the answer-set is what the inquiry produces, that cannot be applied. On
the aporetic model the criterion is negative and local:

> **(NC)** An inquiry has succeeded at t when (i) the commitments that constituted the aporia are
> jointly dischargeable by S from her position at t, and (ii) that position accounts for the
> obstruction — it explains why they were not jointly dischargeable before.

You need no advance grasp of the answer, only the ability to tell that the obstruction is gone; and
you have defeasible access to that, because the obstruction was constituted by your own commitments.
Ana need not have known what she would find. She needs to be able to hold together, at last, her
reading of the meetings, her colleague's good faith and her own reliability — and to see why she
could not before.

Clause (ii) blocks an objection to the criterion as draft-001 stated it. Without it, plain
abandonment satisfies it: Ana drops her commitment to her own reliability, decides she is
oversensitive, and what remains is jointly dischargeable. Excluding abandonment from the definition
of *inquiry* does not exclude it from the criterion; clause (ii) does, because mere dropping
explains nothing. If "I am oversensitive here" is adopted because it explains the pattern, it is a
diagnosis and not an abandonment, and calling the inquiry successful is right. Two concessions: the
criterion is defeasible, since a repair can seem to restore dischargeability without doing so, and
the appearance of relief is what dogmatism manufactures; and a criterion is not a decision
procedure. What tests a repair is time — commitments include commitments to act, action on a false
repair fails, and the failure generates fresh undischargeability. That is not a positive criterion
smuggled in; it is the same criterion applied later.

Aristotle stated the payoff first, and draft-001 stopped one clause short of the sentence that says
so. Having said that "it is not possible to untie a knot of which one does not know", he adds:
"besides, a man does not otherwise know even whether he has at any given time found what he is
looking for or not; for the end is not clear to such a man, while to him who has first discussed the
difficulties it is clear" (*Metaphysics* 995a24 ff., trans. Ross).

## 7. Predecessors

Draft-001 named four predecessors it had not read. I have since read two, and the results require
narrowing its novelty claims.

**Peirce.** "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term
this struggle inquiry"; and, directly against the erotetic picture: "the mere putting of a
proposition into the interrogative form does not stimulate the mind to any struggle after belief.
There must be a real and living doubt, and without this all discussion is idle" (Peirce 1877: §IV).
Two contrasts survive: an aporia need not be felt, whereas Peirce's doubt is an irritation that ends
when the feeling does; and joint dischargeability is not settlement of opinion, which is why the
tenacious believer has not removed his aporia though he has quieted his doubt. A third claim of
draft-001's does not survive — that truth-aptness falls out of the model in a way Peirce cannot
match. He makes the same move in the same section, reasoning that beliefs should "truly guide our
actions", so that we reject any belief not "so formed as to insure this result. But it will only do
so by creating a doubt in the place of that belief" (§IV). The residue is narrow: Peirce routes the
correction through a created doubt, the model through undischargeability downstream.

**Dewey.** This is the serious anticipation, and draft-001's first novelty claim does not survive
it. Dewey defines inquiry as "the controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate
situation into one that is so determinate in its constituent distinctions and relations as to
convert the elements of the original situation into a unified whole"; the original situation "is
open in the sense that its constituents do not hang together" (Dewey 1938: 104–5). He
de-psychologises the starting point: "We are doubtful because the situation is inherently doubtful.
Personal states of doubt that are not evoked by and are not relative to some existential situation
are pathological" (106). And he states the formulation problem outright: "It is a familiar and
significant saying that a problem well put is half-solved. To find out what the problem and problems
are which a problematic situation presents to be inquired into, is to be well along in inquiry" —
and "[t]o mistake the problem involved is to cause subsequent inquiry to be irrelevant or to go
astray" (108). He even asks the guidance question — "How is the formation of a genuine problem so
controlled that further inquiries will move toward a solution?" — and answers with a localization
procedure: "search out the constituents of a given situation which, as constituents, are settled"
(108–9).

One difference is substantive and I rest on it. Dewey's unit is a *situation*, and situations are
precognitive: "[t]here is nothing intellectual or cognitive in the existence of such situations,
although they are the necessary condition of cognitive operations or inquiry" (107). Mine is a set
of the agent's own commitments together with her position. The model sits between Peirce and Dewey:
unlike Peirce's doubt it need not be felt; unlike Dewey's situation it is constituted by things the
agent is answerable for. That is what lets the defect ground criticism *of the inquirer*, and what
makes it a parameter an agent can be under a norm with respect to.

**Collingwood** belongs on the other side. He proposed to substitute "for a logic of propositions …
a logic of question and answer" (Collingwood 1939: 37), held that "the primacy belongs to the
questioning activity" (35), and endorsed the principle he found in Bacon and Descartes, that
"knowledge comes only by answering questions, and that these questions must be the right questions
and asked in the right order" (25) — registering the demand without an account of how it is met.

So, narrowed, I claim: (1) the indexing thesis, which is a claim about an architecture that did not
exist when Dewey wrote; (2) the argument of §5.4; and (3) the machinery — the position-relative
definition, the taxonomy with the informational/conceptual split that explains the reach of
question-indexing, and the norms (LOC), (FEAS) and (PR) with the payoffs in §6. I do not claim the
underlying picture, which is Dewey's; and I have not read Laudan or Hookway, so anticipation from
that quarter cannot be ruled out.

## 8. Objections

**Profligacy.** *Every agent's commitments contain countless undischargeable subsets, so the model
licenses inquiry into everything.* The first part is true and the second does not follow: (AX)
states a necessary condition and (PR) shapes what follows from it. An aporia that blocks nothing
else is not worth repairing; one that blocks a plan or a body of further beliefs is. This yields
something like clutter avoidance as a consequence rather than an axiom, and without appealing to the
triviality of subject matter — which matters, since Friedman denies that the information a zetetic
norm makes us ignore need be "clutter for us" (2020a: §4.5). What makes an aporia low priority is
not that its content is dull but that its persistence costs nothing.

**Idle curiosity.** *Wondering why boats are called* S.S. *something involves no defect.* It
involves an informational gap: a commitment to completeness on a topic one cares about, unmet. And
such cases are where inquiry most nearly *is* question-shaped, since the agent's grip on the
answer-set is what the wondering consists in — which is a point in the model's favour, not against
it, since they are the home region of question-indexing and the model says why.

**Agent-relativity.** *The stopping rule stops when the agent's own standard is met, and the model
cannot criticise standards.* Largely conceded. It criticises a standard only through that standard's
collisions with the agent's other commitments — which, with (FEAS), is enough for the compulsive
checker but is not a theory of which standards it is reasonable to hold. An external ceiling is
available and I have adopted one: Friedman's argument that checking in suspension-proof
circumstances requires an unjustified attitude does not depend on the agent's standards at all.

**Vagueness of "commitment".** *The notion carries the weight and is undefined.* It is defined by
its role — whatever the agent is answerable for — and the role is doing work rather than standing in
for it. Two consequences are checkable. Because commitments include standards, the model predicts
strain aporiai, hence Woodard's data together with the constraint that separates her cases from the
compulsive checker's; because commitments do not require notice, it predicts the intelligibility of
zetetic negligence. A vaguer notion would predict neither. I grant that a full theory owes an account
of how aporiai are counted and when two are the same; I do not have one.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The zetetic turn corrected epistemology's temporal parochialism by extending evaluation backwards
from the moment of belief to the activity that produces it. But it stopped at the question. Where
the question is the achievement, a question-indexed norm either has no parameter to take or takes a
coarse one and borrows its verdicts from a judgement about where the trouble lies. What an inquiry
is answerable to is a defect in the inquirer's own commitments. That parameter individuates
inquiries, including the ones that fail; it can be possessed before it can be stated, which is why
it can govern the work of stating it; it recovers question-indexed norms as the case of
informational gaps; and it survives Meno's second horn. The knot comes first. The question, when we
are lucky and skilful, is what we make of it.
