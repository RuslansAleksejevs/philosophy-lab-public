# What Counts as an Eye? Hooke, Cavendish, and the Levels of Fly Vision

## Abstract

Robert Hooke's celebrated estimate of nearly fourteen thousand “eyes” in a grey drone-fly appears to invite an easy inference: more eyes should mean more sight. Margaret Cavendish makes that inference explicit, then uses the fly's apparent failure to avoid a spider to question both the count and the microscope that produced it. Yet Hooke's own account in *Micrographia* already denies that many facets give a fly more whole-animal vision. This essay argues that the dispute is best understood by distinguishing three claims that the word “eye” can compress: that a repeated surface feature is visible; that it is a functionally relevant optical part; and that the animal's integrated visual capacity is thereby increased. Hooke offers substantial but defeasible grounds for the first two claims and expressly resists the third. Cavendish rightly demands that the passage from visible globules to eyes and from count to capacity be defended, but her behavioral examples do not by themselves disprove local optical function. The episode therefore supports a narrow principle of evidential discipline: observations of parts, claims about their function, and claims about a whole animal's capacity require different warrants.

## 1. The question behind the number

A grey drone-fly with almost fourteen thousand eyes seems a perfect example of what a microscope can reveal. It is also a good example of how one word can carry several claims. What exactly is counted? Is each smooth hemisphere a separate eye, a component of an eye, or simply a visible bump? Even if each hemisphere has an optical role, does the animal see fourteen thousand times, or see better than an animal with two eyes? These are not the same question.

In “Of the Eyes of Flies,” Margaret Cavendish raises them together. She questions whether the microscope has reliably shown about 14,000 eyes in a grey drone-fly. Perhaps the bright globules are only transparent, reflecting knobs. But she also argues that if the fly really has that many eyes, it ought to possess more “optic sense” than an animal with one or two. Why, then, does it fail to see a spider until the spider is near? Why can a fly in cold weather seem so insensible that it can be caught? Nature, she insists, does not make organs in vain (*Observations*, 59–60).

Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* is the primary target. His thirty-ninth observation counts nearly 14,000 “Pearls or Hemispheres” across the drone-fly's two eye clusters. Yet Hooke does not conclude that the fly has fourteen thousand independent visual subjects or enjoys greater total sight. He describes how each hemisphere might form a local image, then argues that most such images would be confused and that only one or a few would be distinct. He proposes an “observing faculty” that attends to one object at a time (Observation XXXIX). The principles that Hooke assigns to a facet and to the animal as a whole therefore differ.

I argue that this difference changes how we should read the dispute. Hooke has a serious, though defeasible, case that the facets are optical parts. He gives several kinds of evidence, not only a magnified picture. Cavendish is right that a count of visible structures does not on its own establish what they do, and especially not what the whole animal can see. But her behavioral test does not refute the local optical proposal: limited performance is compatible with numerous working parts. Her most forceful challenge falls on a simple move from “many eyes” to “more sight,” a move Hooke's own account declines to make. Their disagreement is not resolved by declaring either glass or unaided reason infallible. It is clarified by separating surface observation, part-function, and whole-animal capacity.

This is a limited reconstruction of the texts, not a claim that either author anticipated a modern theory of insect vision. “Part-function” here means only a proposed role within a larger sensory arrangement. The essay does not test Hooke's biology against contemporary entomology.

## 2. What Hooke observes, and what he infers

Hooke starts with a selected specimen: a large grey drone-fly whose head bears unusually large clusters. He varies the degree and direction of light and changes the specimen's position. He reports rows of hemispheres, trenches between them, and reflections of objects in their smooth surfaces. Moving reflected images lead him to conclude that the trenches are not perforated. He also notes that facets face different directions, that a living fly brushes its eye clusters with its forefeet, and that the facets vary in size. His estimate of nearly fourteen thousand comes from counting several rows in different ways and extrapolating across each cluster (Observation XXXIX).

This is not merely a dramatic magnified image. Hooke describes manipulations of light and angle, reflections, behavior, and an approximate count. He then dissects the cluster. He reports an outer flexible, transparent covering, a clear fluid beneath it, and a darker mucous lining. These observations matter because his classification of the cluster as an organ of sight does not rest solely on its resemblance to a human eye. He points to structural similarities and to the lack, in these animals, of another organ resembling familiar eyes. He also invokes crustaceans. He says that when he removes the movable knobs of crabs or lobsters, they no longer avoid nearby obstacles as they did before. He then argues by congruence that related structures in flies are probably eyes too.

The qualification is important: the removal test is reported for crustaceans, not for the grey drone-fly whose facets he counts. For the fly, Hooke's functional identification combines its observed surface, dissected layers, structural analogy, and the wider comparison. That is a reasoned inference, but not a direct demonstration that each facet functions separately in the living fly.

Hooke's optical argument adds a further level. He proposes that each hemisphere refracts light to a point not far inside it, where a retina might lie. If so, each would form an image. But a curved surface, on his account, focuses best the rays near its optical axis. Images from other directions would be confused. There could be many pictures across the cluster while only one or a few were distinct. He therefore denies the conclusion that a fly with many facets sees more than another creature. Because the fly cannot quickly redirect a pair of eyes as a human can, its head and clusters supply other ways to direct an optical line. The “observing faculty” that selects an object remains, he says, a deeper question (Observation XXXIX).

Hooke thus distinguishes a local optical picture from the animal's attentive seeing. “Perfect eye” names a proposed function of a facet, not a miniature creature that sees by itself. This reading does not make his theory certain. The “retina” is inferred by analogy; the optical path is a model; and Hooke openly marks parts of the explanation as probable. But it prevents us from treating his count as a claim that fourteen thousand facets yield fourteen thousand simultaneous fields of clear awareness.

## 3. Cavendish's challenge

Cavendish's chapter “Of Micrography, and of Magnifying and Multiplying Glasses” is often summarized as a rejection of microscope evidence. Her wording is more qualified. She says glasses “may, and do oftentimes” present a false picture, not that no glass ever presents a true one. Her worry is that magnification, varied light, reflection, refraction, and the glass's own form can alter the appearance. Even a picture that records an exterior may not tell us the object's hidden motions. Magnification makes a figure larger, but larger appearance is not automatically truer knowledge (*Observations*, 49–52).

This point bears directly on the fly. A cluster of reflective hemispheres may be real, while the interpretation of each as an eye remains uncertain. Cavendish suggests that the “pearls or globes” may instead be shining, transparent protuberances. She also questions whether a microscope can establish the natural form of the creature when the instrument changes scale and the appearance varies with light and position (*Observations*, 59–60). This is a genuine alternative explanation, although the mere availability of an alternative does not show it to be better than Hooke's account.

Cavendish then links the number to purpose. If the fly really has 14,000 eyes, she reasons, it must have more optic sense than an animal with one or two. She doubts that nature would make such a number “for no more use.” Its apparently poor response to a spider and its weakness in cold weather make the count seem useless. This is not simply an objection to lenses. It asks whether the proposed anatomy fits what the animal can do.

The behavioral examples have some force. If “14,000 eyes” suggests vastly improved, broadly alert vision, a fly that fails to detect danger until it is close is relevant counterevidence. Cavendish's objection warns against inferring a capacity from the number of components alone. But the examples do not isolate what causes the behavior. The animal may have many optical subparts and still have limited acuity, attention, or integration; its response in cold weather may reflect its general state rather than the absence of visual structures. Cavendish herself offers no alternative mechanism that would show how those factors work. Her examples therefore unsettle a simple capacity inference, but they do not decide whether the facets have an optical role.

There is also a textual complication. Hooke himself rejects the very inference Cavendish draws from the number to increased animal-level sight. He accepts many local images but says the fly is not thereby given a greater faculty of seeing. Cavendish is still challenging his decision to call each facet an eye, and she doubts the microscope's evidence for the count and anatomy. But her behavioral point is not a direct refutation of Hooke's conclusion about visual capacity, because Hooke does not make that conclusion.

## 4. Three levels of claim

The exchange becomes clearer if we separate three propositions.

**First, a surface claim:** the fly's head presents many regularly arranged, smooth hemispheres. Hooke's varying of light, reflections, and row-counting gives this claim more support than one picture alone would. Cavendish does not have to deny that there are visible globules to question whether the instrument and its interpretation reveal their nature.

**Second, a part-function claim:** each hemisphere is an eye or an image-forming optical part. Hooke supports this with anatomy and analogy, then adds a refraction model. The evidence is stronger than visual appearance alone but still defeasible: he does not report a controlled demonstration that each hemisphere forms a distinct image in the living fly. Cavendish correctly asks for a reason to classify a reflective knob as an eye rather than as another kind of structure. Her argument that no eye-count should be accepted without connecting the structure to what it does is a useful burden of proof. Still, lack of superior animal-level performance cannot by itself erase evidence about component function.

**Third, a whole-animal capacity claim:** the fly, because it has many facets, sees more, better, or more widely. Hooke's text is careful here. He notes that facets point in different directions, which may provide broad directional coverage, but he does not equate coverage with clear, attentive vision of more objects. Images can be numerous while distinct seeing remains limited. The integration between local signals and an animal's attention is not explained. On this third claim, Cavendish is right to ask whether a count has any consequence for animal behavior, but her assumption that many component eyes must produce more useful sight is not established.

This is not a simple equivocation charge against Hooke. In the key passages he signals the different levels: “eye” can name a local optical unit, while “faculty of seeing” refers to the animal's integrated capacity. He does not use these expressions as if they meant exactly the same thing. The problem is rather that the phrase “14,000 eyes” is easy to repeat without Hooke's qualification. The number can sound like a count of whole, independent organs, and then readers may infer more visual power than the argument warrants. A careful interpretation must keep the sense of “eye” visible.

Cavendish's side also resists a simple label. She challenges the instrument, proposes a non-eye interpretation, and assesses the count teleologically through the animal's apparent performance. Her challenge is strongest when directed at the jump from visible multiplicity to the role and use of the parts. It is weaker if treated as proof that the parts are not optical units simply because the fly sometimes fails to avoid danger. She applies an organism-level test to a local anatomical hypothesis without showing that local function must yield the expected behavior.

The distinction is philosophically useful because observation and function answer different questions. Seeing a repeatable surface form does not by itself identify its biological role. A layer that resembles the cornea or retina gives reason for a functional analogy but does not settle how the whole system operates. And even a working local optical part does not, without an account of coordination, imply more animal-level awareness. Different evidential steps require different tests: repeated observation can stabilize a surface description; dissection and comparison can support an anatomical hypothesis; behavioral and mechanistic evidence can test claims about the integrated animal. Neither an isolated image nor a single behavioral anecdote settles every level.

## 5. The strongest case against this reading

A strong objection is that the distinction adds little. Hooke plainly describes individual facets as local image-forming units and plainly says that their number does not increase the fly's overall capacity. There is no equivocation in his argument to expose. Cavendish, moreover, need not be answering Hooke's full theory: she is challenging the much broader experimental claim that each visible facet is an eye and that a high count discloses nature's design. If so, this essay merely restates what Hooke and Cavendish already say.

That objection limits the contribution, but it does not eliminate it. The point is not that Hooke commits a crude fallacy or that Cavendish overlooks every qualification. The point is that their exchange is easier to assess when we ask which level each argument supports. Hooke's statement that each hemisphere may form a local picture is a proposal about component optics. His statement that a fly does not see more is an account of organism-level capacity. Cavendish's behavioral test bears directly on the latter, while her doubts about reflections and anatomy bear on the former. Once these are separated, one can grant her challenge to the inference from count to superior sight without treating it as a refutation of every facet-level claim.

A second objection is that “eye” need not mean an independent subject. In ordinary biological usage, parts can be called eyes without implying that each separately thinks or sees. Hooke may simply be using a conventional functional analogy. This is plausible. The thesis does not depend on his having made an illicit switch in meaning. Instead it recommends explicit qualification: “facet,” “local optical eye,” and “animal's sight” identify different explanatory targets. Hooke often supplies those qualifications, but the headline count and Cavendish's response can obscure them.

A third objection is that Hooke's evidence is more than adequate. He inspects surface, dissects layers, compares related creatures, and reports behavioral effects when crustacean eye knobs are removed. It is reasonable to infer that fly facets are visual parts even if the exact mechanism is not demonstrated. I agree that this is the strongest rival view. The present essay does not deny the inference; it calls it plausible and defeasible. The crustacean experiment supports the broader analogy, but because Hooke reports it for movable crustacean knobs rather than for the grey drone-fly's individual facets, it does not directly establish every part of the fly's proposed optics. The distinction is a way to state what the evidence supports, not a verdict that Hooke's conclusion is false.

## 6. What the episode can establish

Recent scholarship has already examined the broad epistemology and rhetoric of Cavendish's response to microscopy. Stephen Clucas shows how Cavendish works with the variability of microscopic images and with Hooke's own accounts of shifting appearance, while turning the image's claims against the microscopists (Clucas, 2022, especially 20–24). Mary Purcell summarizes the fly-eye passage as an example of Cavendish's inner debate with experimental philosophy: Cavendish invokes the 14,000 count, doubts that the facets are eyes, and asks why flies do not see spiders sooner (Purcell, 2025, 56–57). These studies make a broad claim that Cavendish simply distrusts artificial instruments too familiar to sustain as this essay's contribution.

The narrower reading here depends on putting those observations beside the complete sequence of Hooke's Observation XXXIX. Hooke's own account distinguishes the facets' local image formation from the animal's overall seeing. The distinction can sharpen both sides without making them agree. Hooke's optical model remains a conjecture, and his account of the observing faculty is deliberately unresolved. Cavendish's suspicion about the eye-count remains relevant, but her behavior-based challenge does not show that reflective facets cannot be functionally involved in sight.

The result is not a general rule that instruments can reveal appearances but never functions. Hooke gives reasons to move beyond mere appearance, and Cavendish does not demonstrate that his account fails. Nor does this case establish that repeated parts in any system have to be individuated by organism-level behavior. It offers a local lesson about this particular dispute: a claim about countable structures is not yet a claim about what those structures do, and a claim about what a part does is not yet a claim about what the whole animal can perceive. Where a term like “eye” travels across those levels, a writer should say which level is meant and what evidence licenses the step.

## Conclusion

Cavendish's fly-eye challenge is strongest as a demand not to confuse a striking number with an explanation of vision. Hooke's response is more careful than a simple “many eyes, more sight” story: he reasons from visible facets and anatomical analogy to local optical function, then explicitly denies that the number gives a fly greater overall visual capacity. Cavendish's spider and cold examples undermine any unsupported promise of superior sight, but they cannot alone refute the local optical hypothesis. The episode invites a modest standard of interpretation: distinguish the visible part, the proposed function of that part, and the capacity of the whole animal. The count may be suggestive; it is not the whole argument.
