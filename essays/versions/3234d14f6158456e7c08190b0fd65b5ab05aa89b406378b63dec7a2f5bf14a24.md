# Being Right About What You Meant

An arts centre announces a competition: “Submit a black-and-white picture of the town.” Some entrants make photographs with shades of grey. Others construct images using only solid black and solid white. After the deadline, the curator explains that only the second kind was intended. A dated planning document confirms this. The curator has neither changed her mind nor invented an interpretation to favour a friend. She excludes every picture containing grey.

Suppose the announcement was addressed to both photographers and printmakers, each reading was reasonable in its setting, and the centre supplied nothing that settled the difference. Suppose, too, that entrants reasonably treated the announcement as sufficient guidance for beginning their work. These are features of the imagined case, not claims about what every use of “black and white” means. The question is whether establishing the curator’s original intention settles her right to exclude the photographs.

It does not. More surprisingly, the photographers need not defeat her account of the meaning to resist that conclusion. They can agree about what she meant and still have a claim concerning what the centre may now do. Nor does their claim necessarily require admitting their photographs. Doing that could disadvantage entrants who abandoned promising ideas because they understood grey to be forbidden. A clarification can identify one governing interpretation while leaving several competing claims unresolved.

I will defend a constraint on the authority of public clarification. When an issuer invites action through a direction, and materially different responses are reasonable under the guidance it provides, selecting the correct interpretation afterwards is insufficient to justify an adverse decision about those earlier responses. The issuer must also address whether recipients had a reasonable opportunity to avoid the disadvantage. Where the recipients compete, that inquiry concerns their relation to one another, not just the cost imposed on each separately.

This is a claim about the practical authority of interpretation. It does not make every reasonable reading true, turn every announcement into a promise, or require every mistaken participant to receive the benefit they sought. It specifies something an interpretive verdict cannot establish by itself.

Consider three questions about the arts centre. What did its announcement communicate? What were particular entrants justified in making before clarification? What may the centre now count against them? These questions are connected, but none is simply a restatement of another. The first concerns content. The second concerns the basis available for action at a particular time. The third concerns authority to use a standard in a decision affecting someone.

That separation matters even if we give the curator more than evidence about her intention. Suppose a defensible theory of utterance content establishes that the announcement really did direct entrants to use only two solid colours. The photographs then failed to conform to that content. It still does not follow that the centre may use this failure as a sufficient reason for exclusion. Accurate descriptions of nonconformity and justified consequences for nonconformity are different things. Conversely, if a theory instead holds that the public announcement had no uniquely settled content, the centre has even less reason to treat its preferred reading as an automatically decisive standard for earlier work. The argument does not depend on choosing between those theories.

Grice’s analysis helps locate the first question without settling the other two. He distinguishes producing an effect from communicating through an intended recognition of intention, extending the discussion beyond assertions to imperative cases. An audience’s response can therefore differ from the response a speaker meant to induce. But an account of what the speaker meant does not, without further premises, allocate the costs of that difference. Nothing here refutes Grice’s account of meaning. The mistake would be to use an answer to his question as though it answered every question about the encounter. See [Grice, “Meaning,” pp. 381–384](https://www.uh.edu/~garson/Grice_Meaning.pdf).

The same caution applies to common ground. Stalnaker distinguishes what participants share from what each takes them to share; he also distinguishes common ground from straightforward shared belief. His framework already permits failures of alignment. Once the curator clarifies, everyone might accept that she intended to exclude grey. That new agreement need not include the claim that her intention was previously accessible to the photographers. Present agreement about a past intention is not past agreement about that intention. A record of successful clarification should preserve this difference. See [Stalnaker, “Common Ground,” pp. 702–707](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/f07/pragmatics/stalnaker02.pdf).

The important asymmetry is temporal. Learning now that an instruction required an action can change what I should do next. It cannot by itself change which reasons I had when I acted yesterday. This is familiar as a point about knowledge. Public clarification gives it a further practical significance: the person supplying the new information may also control the consequences of having lacked it. The curator can settle tomorrow’s colour requirement and decide today’s eligibility. Holding these powers together makes it easy to treat the successful exercise of the first as sufficient authority for the second.

Why should that inference fail? The answer begins with the kind of activity the issuer has invited. A public direction offered as guidance invites recipients to organise their conduct around publicly available criteria. In doing so, the issuer asks them to substitute that guidance for some of the inquiry and negotiation they would otherwise need. An announcement that solicits finished work is an invitation to begin making it, unless the context marks the guidance as provisional or incomplete. The issuer does not guarantee perfect understanding. It does, however, present its words as usable for the invited purpose.

Now suppose a relevant difference between two responses resulted from an ambiguity for which the issuer supplied no adequate means of resolution. If both responses were reasonable ways of undertaking the invited activity, that difference cannot, just by being labelled a failure to follow instructions, establish a deficiency in one participant’s responsiveness to the guidance. The issuer has supplied a basis for action that does not reliably distinguish the conduct it later ranks. To use the disputed distinction as decisive without further justification is to make recipients answerable for a discriminating capacity the guidance did not provide.

This argument relies on a limited normative premise: when an institution presents a direction as a basis on which people can organise conduct for its assessment, it owes those people an assessment that takes account of the opportunities its direction actually gave them. That premise follows from the relationship the invitation establishes. The institution deliberately recruits people’s agency: their judgment, effort and ability to respond to what it says. It cannot claim the benefit of their responsiveness while treating a defect in its own guidance as though it straightforwardly measured a defect in their response. Such treatment misdescribes the basis on which the adverse decision is deserved, even where it accurately describes the work’s properties.

This is not an argument that every adverse outcome must be deserved. Some competitions use lotteries; others test performance under uncertainty. Those arrangements can be legitimate. But they must obtain their authority from that different basis. An issuer cannot invite people to respond to one kind of procedure and then treat an unannounced linguistic lottery as though it were ordinary assessment under adequately communicated requirements.

We can state the resulting constraint more precisely. Before using a clarified distinction as a sufficient ground for an adverse decision about earlier conduct, an issuer facing a reasonable ambiguity complaint must establish one of two things. Either recipients had an adequate opportunity, before the relevant commitment of effort, to identify the governing distinction or knowingly take its risk; or the procedure has been repaired so that the earlier difference in reasonable interpretation no longer does the adverse work. Where neither is established, the issuer needs an independent justification for imposing the disadvantage. Saying again what the words meant does not provide one.

The point of “sufficient” is crucial. A centre might lack the resources to repeat an event. A venue might possess a machine that can reproduce only solid black and white, making certain photographs unusable. Those facts can bear on what happens next. But then the explanation is about resources or the purpose of the event, and must face the corresponding questions of responsibility and repair. It is no longer simply that the photographers failed to follow a requirement they were adequately given. The constraint prevents a semantic fact from doing argumentative work it cannot do; it does not make practical necessity disappear.

Reasonableness here concerns action as well as interpretation. A possible reading is not enough. It must fit the words, their context, and the conventions reasonably available to this audience. Even a linguistically reasonable reading may be an unreasonable basis for a large irreversible investment. If a notice plainly directs applicants to consult a readily available specification, proceeding without it can be their responsibility. If someone notices a serious ambiguity and can resolve it with a brief inquiry, pressing ahead may amount to accepting the risk. The test looks at the circumstances before the outcome, not at whichever interpretation becomes convenient afterwards.

But the duty to inquire cannot be unlimited. An issuer would hollow out the point of public guidance if every foreseeable ambiguity made recipients responsible for privately reconstructing its intention. Sometimes an inquiry channel is unavailable; sometimes the issuer has told readers the notice contains everything needed; sometimes a reading is so normal in an invited audience that the audience has no reason to notice a rival. Anna Drożdżowicz treats clarification as a substantive activity involving interpretative responsibility, contextual resources and unevenly distributed capacities. Her discussion supports taking these conditions seriously rather than assuming that everyone who needed clarification could simply have obtained it. I do not infer from the existence of a misunderstanding that an issuer was negligent. See [“Making it precise,” section 4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-022-03544-x).

The proposed constraint is therefore narrower than an instruction to favour audiences. It assigns a burden of justification where a responsible invitation to act has met a reasonable divergence in response. The issuer may answer that burden by showing an explicit allocation of uncertainty, a neglected opportunity to check, or a defect unrelated to the disputed interpretation. Nor must it anticipate every idiosyncratic understanding. The relevant audience is the audience its communication addresses, with the interpretive resources it could reasonably expect that audience to use.

An obvious objection remains: this is just the familiar duty not to leave people worse off after inducing reliance. That objection identifies an important source of the argument. It also helps show why the most ambitious simple response—requiring performance under every reasonable interpretation—would be mistaken.

Kolodny and Wallace distinguish, in their discussion of Scanlon, a duty to prevent certain losses from the stronger duty of fidelity. The former can sometimes be discharged by warning or compensation rather than fulfilling an expectation; their discussion also recognises that context can assign the risk of changed plans to the person relying. I accept the importance of both distinctions. Invited reliance does not always entitle a recipient to the expected performance, and mere expectation does not settle who should bear its risk. See [“Promises and Practices Revisited,” pp. 125–130](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~ngkolodny/PandPR.pdf).

The present proposal adds a constraint needed when reliance runs through interpretation. One cannot determine which reliance deserves protection solely by asking which reading was eventually vindicated. That would let the conclusion of the clarification determine the legitimacy of the earlier response to an unresolved instruction. Nor can the remedy always be selected by considering each recipient separately. The arts competition shows why.

Suppose the centre admits the photographs containing grey as an exception. A printmaker objects: “I spent a week solving the problem without grey. I discarded a stronger design because I thought it was outside the rules.” The printmaker is not entitled to win. Neither is the photographer. Yet each has a claim that the centre must consider in arranging their comparison. The photographer reasonably took a broader range of techniques to be available. The printmaker organised the work around a narrower range. Admitting every image may protect the first against exclusion while leaving the second to compete against work made under a different practical constraint.

The readings themselves are not logically inconsistent in all their consequences. A two-colour image satisfies both. What conflicts is their use as a single standard of comparative assessment: whether work exploiting grey should compete on the same footing with work developed under its exclusion. This distinction matters. The problem does not require two people to demand an impossible conjunction of semantic contents. It requires decisions made under divergent understandings to remain present in the finished works that are now being compared.

Paying each entrant’s expenses does not automatically resolve that problem. They were invited to seek an opportunity to have their work assessed under announced constraints. Reimbursing paper and travel may leave that opportunity impaired. Conversely, giving everyone the opportunity they would have enjoyed under their own interpretation may be impossible when there is one prize and a fixed event. Treating the situation as a collection of separate compensation claims misses the relation in which the affected people now stand.

One remedy might be a new period in which everyone can adapt work under an explicit specification, with reasonable support for additional costs. Another might be separate categories if that fits the event’s purpose and can preserve meaningful opportunities. If neither is feasible, cancellation with reimbursement and a candid explanation may be preferable to an apparently clean verdict under the late clarification. None of these procedures guarantees the exact opportunity every entrant would have had without the error. Nor is any one of them always required. What matters is that their justification must consider how the different readings shaped competing performances. The curator’s intention cannot choose between these remedies for us.

This yields a useful test. Ask whether the proposed remedy would still seem adequate if we temporarily stopped calling one group’s reading “the correct one,” while retaining all the evidence about what each group could reasonably know and how each acted. This does not suppress the truth about meaning. It tests whether that truth is being used to dismiss a complaint about a different matter. If the only defence of disadvantaging one group is that its interpretation lost, the defence has not yet addressed the conditions under which that group was invited to act.

What if the printmakers simply say they would have made better work under the other reading? Such claims can be opportunistic and difficult to verify. The constraint does not require accepting them without evidence. Drafts, correspondence, the kinds of techniques the requirement excludes, and information about the invitation can help establish whether the interpretation materially shaped the work. Some claims will remain uncertain. Uncertainty may affect the remedy, but treating every losing interpretation as illegitimate is no solution: it replaces a difficult evidential question with an irrelevant semantic shortcut.

The case also clarifies a connection with retraction. Quill Kukla argues that retracting a speech act is not a reset: it may leave normative effects in place, and its success must be distinguished from its ethical permissibility. The present argument shares that resistance to erasing history. Its target, however, need involve no withdrawal of what was meant. The curator can retain her original direction and correctly explain it. Her problem is how to apply it after an audience reasonably acted under a different understanding. Even a clarification that succeeds without retracting anything leaves this question open. See [Kukla, “The Ethics of Retraction,” preprint v1, pp. 1–6 and 15–17](https://www.qeios.com/read/PDR3QV/pdf).

Does this make an ordinary moral duty into a linguistic commitment by verbal inflation? That danger should be avoided. I am not claiming that every direction contains an unspoken promise of compensation, or that the meaning of “black and white” includes a procedure for adjudicating mistakes. The obligation arises from using language in a practice that invites people to organise conduct for assessment. The linguistic act helps establish that relation; principles governing such a relation help determine its consequences. Those principles may also apply to nonverbal guidance. Nothing important depends on language having a monopoly over them.

Lewis’s account of conversational score is hospitable to this point. Score can include permissions and other practical statuses, and Lewis explicitly allows linguistic action to blend into wider social practices. His framework need not ignore the claims I have described. The demand is instead one of adequate specification: an account used to assess public clarification must preserve the relevant history and participant-specific claims. A register containing only the current agreed interpretation would be insufficient for that purpose, even if entirely adequate for a narrower semantic task. See [Lewis, “Scorekeeping in a Language Game,” pp. 345–346 and 356](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/s06/readings/lewis79.pdf).

The philosophical result is consequently compatible with familiar accounts of meaning, common ground and induced reliance. It is not an alternative foundation for all three. It identifies a point at which they must be connected carefully: a public interpretation can be correct while the authority to make it decisive for past conduct remains unsupported. In competitive settings, the missing support must take account of the interaction among reasonable readings, not merely the existence of individual loss.

A final objection concerns usefulness. Must every small correction now trigger a cumbersome process? No. Many clarifications arrive before anyone has acted, affect a detail that makes no difference to assessment, or allow an easy adjustment without burdening another participant. In those cases the distinction among the three questions remains, but there may be little practical work left to do. The burden grows when an issuer has invited substantial reliance, allowed a material ambiguity to persist, and then proposes to make the selected interpretation decisive. Those are precisely the cases in which an effortless declaration of meaning is least likely to be enough.

Return to the curator. She may be right about what she intended, right about what her words communicated, and right that future entries must contain no grey. A photographer can accept all three claims while asking for a decision that acknowledges how the invitation reasonably guided her earlier work. The printmaker can make a related demand concerning the terms of comparison. Neither needs ownership of the announcement’s meaning. They need the centre to answer for its use of that meaning.

The public value of clarification is that it lets people proceed with a better understanding. It should not turn that improvement into evidence that they ought already to have possessed it.
