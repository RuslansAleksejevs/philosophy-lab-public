# Baseline Capture

## Why Consent Stops Justifying When Its Beneficiary Has Shaped the Alternatives

---

## 1. Two pictures of consent

Consent does remarkable normative work. A surgeon's incision is battery without it and treatment with it; taking your bicycle is theft without it and borrowing with it. The standard account of when this transformation succeeds is a list of conditions on the consenter. Valid consent is “sufficiently capacitated, informed, and voluntary consent” (Eyal 2025, §1), and voluntariness is discussed under three headings: coercion, undue inducement, and the want of decent alternatives (§§5.1–5.3).

What these conditions have in common is that they are *synchronic*. Each concerns the agent and her situation at the moment of consent. History enters only instrumentally, insofar as it bears on whether she is now capacitated, informed and unpressured.

The tradition is not blind to option-poverty. Eyal's §5.3 is devoted to “‘no choice' situations”, and reports a proposal designed to keep such situations from swallowing consent whole: consent is insufficiently voluntary “only when the patient's options are unfairly curtailed by the offer itself” (Miller and Wertheimer 2010, 92, 97, as reported in Eyal 2025, §5.3). Notice the index. The test looks at the proposal on the table and asks whether *it* curtailed the recipient's options. That is still a test on a moment.

I shall argue that a party can decisively curtail another's options while no offer he ever makes curtails anything — because every one of his offers *widens* them. The structure that does this I call a **ratchet**: a sequence of transactions, each satisfying every synchronic condition, through which one party obtains terms it could not have obtained at the outset and could not obtain by any single offer. Ratchets are not exotic. They are the normal form of long employment relationships, sponsored migration, platform dependence, franchising, and some kinds of intimate control.

Four claims, in order of increasing strength:

1. **Historicity.** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on the properties of the consent but on the causal history of the option set relative to which it was given — specifically, on the role A's conduct plays in explaining that history.
2. **Self-serving certification.** The reason is that consent certifies only a comparison: that what was accepted beat the alternative. Consent has justificatory force because that comparison is normally *independent* of the party who invokes it. Where his conduct explains why the alternative was so poor, the certification is no longer independent evidence, and is to that extent idle.
3. **Inverse reliance.** The justificatory weight A may place on B's consent varies inversely with the degree to which A's conduct explains the poverty of B's alternatives.
4. **Commitment.** A party whose course of dealing predictably impoverishes the other's alternatives can restore the justificatory force of the other's later consent only by having bound itself in advance. Consent-based justification therefore has a commitment precondition, which disclosure alone cannot discharge.

The conclusion is a burden shift, not an automatic verdict of wrongdoing; and the essay is titled accordingly. I do not claim that ratchets assemble wrongs out of innocent parts. I claim something more precise and, I think, more defensible: that they assemble *defeated justifications* out of sound ones.

## 2. Vesting

Mara is a software engineer. In 2016 she takes a job with Aurum, a large firm. Four episodes follow, each separated by roughly two years. Each is a single, discrete, plainly perceptible event; nothing turns on imperceptible increments or on sorites reasoning.

**(1) Relocation.** Aurum offers Mara a substantial relocation payment and a subsidised mortgage to move to the small city where its main campus sits. She accepts. She is better off: she has money she would not have had and a house she could not otherwise have afforded. She is also, now, a specialist in a labour market with one dominant buyer.

**(2) Specialisation.** Aurum offers her a funded two-year programme in its proprietary toolchain, together with a promotion. She accepts. She is better off: the work is more interesting and the pay is higher. Her human capital is now substantially firm-specific; her value to Aurum has risen faster than her value to anyone else.

**(3) Deferral.** Aurum offers her a large equity grant vesting over eight years, in place of the smaller cash raise she would otherwise have received. On any reasonable estimate the grant is worth more. She accepts. Leaving now costs her the unvested remainder.

**(4) The ask.** Aurum asks Mara to take over a team whose product she believes to be harmful and whose engineering she considers shoddy. The hours are longer. There is no raise. Refusal will not get her fired; it will end her advancement. She accepts, and she is right to: given where she stands, accepting beats refusing by a wide margin, and she knows it.

Stipulate that Aurum never lied, never threatened, never prevented Mara from leaving, and that at steps (1)–(3) it conferred real benefits that she wanted and that were, at the time, competitively priced. Stipulate that Mara was at every point competent, informed about the terms on offer, and under no pressure beyond the ordinary pressure of wanting things. Stipulate that her preferences never changed, that they were transitive throughout, and that at no point did she reason badly.

Do **not** stipulate that step (4) is competitively priced. It is not, and the draft from which this essay descends said otherwise in one place, which was an error. Step (4) is where Aurum takes the surplus its earlier generosity created. That is the point of the case, and I shall return to it in §3.

Note the comparative facts. At step (4), Aurum obtains labour on terms Mara would have refused flatly in 2016. It obtains them without any of the devices consent theory is built to detect. Had Aurum offered the whole package as a package in 2016 — relocation, specialisation, deferral, and then the ask — Mara would have declined, and Aurum knew it.

What, if anything, has gone wrong?

## 3. What the existing vocabulary catches, and what it misses

**Coercion.** On the family of accounts descending from Nozick (1969), a proposal is a threat rather than an offer if, compared with an appropriate baseline, it worsens the recipient's situation (Anderson 2023, §2.2). Aurum's proposals at (1)–(3) improve Mara's situation on any baseline, and (4) is a proposal she can refuse while keeping everything she has. The most promising refinement is David Zimmerman's: the baseline should be what the recipient would face “in the absence of special interference by the proposal maker” (Zimmerman 1981, as reported in Anderson 2023, §2.4), his illustration being a destitute islander offered a life-sustaining pittance by an employer who has been preventing the building of boats.

Here I must correct something. The draft quoted only half of the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s gloss on this view. The full sentence runs: “If the offering party here has created the conditions that make even very unattractive offers still the best available, then the coercion, if there is any, seems to be vested in the offerer's actions that prevent the recipient from improving her situation or finding better offers elsewhere” (Anderson 2023, §2.4, emphasis added). The antecedent — *created the conditions* — is satisfied by Aurum on my own telling. So I cannot say the concept simply fails to reach the case. What I can say is that the consequent is not satisfied: the gloss locates the coercion in *actions that prevent*, and Aurum performs none. Its every contribution to Mara's position was a benefit she accepted. The entry itself marks the residue: Zimmerman “grants that even if the offering party does not cause the recipient to be in the position she's in, we may still want to criticize such offers” as exploitative, and it observes that “sometimes coercion is used to create or maintain one's bargaining advantages” (§2.4). The remaining question is what to say when bargaining advantages are created and maintained *without* coercion.

**Domination.** The nearest live rival is republican. On Pettit's view, “one is dominated by another if it is continually possible for another party to step in and impose his will on one's choices”, so that the dominated agent's “choices must always be made with an eye towards retaining the favor of those in a position to interfere” (Anderson 2023, §3.3, reporting Pettit 1996). Michael Garnett's freedom-as-nonsubjection is closer still: the entry records that “Garnett holds that such subjection can be found in some cases of offers, where the offeror takes advantage of the offeree's lack of an acceptable alternative” (§3.3, reporting Garnett 2018).

Mara does make her choices with an eye to retaining Aurum's favour, so the resemblance is real and I should have acknowledged it. But the structures differ in a way that matters. Domination is a relation of *power to interfere*. Aurum never acquires any such power: it cannot worsen Mara's position, and could not do so if it wished. What it acquires is the fact that nobody else can improve it. That is not a capacity Aurum holds over Mara; it is a feature of the world Aurum's benefits have made. Baseline capture is therefore possible between parties neither of whom could ever interfere with the other — which is why it survives in competitive markets with free entry, where domination characteristically does not. And the two theses have different objects. Non-domination is a claim about a condition that is bad for the subject. Mine is a claim about the *offeror's standing to justify*. A dominated person may be badly off while her consent still justifies; an undominated person's consent may fail to justify. Garnett's offeror who “takes advantage of the offeree's lack of an acceptable alternative” is my nearest neighbour, and my addition to him is the diachronic question of where that lack came from.

**Manipulation.** The draft claimed that the leading accounts all work through a defect in the target's mental states, and that manipulation “has not generally been theorised as a property of extended sequences”. Both claims are false, and correcting them improves the argument. Noggle's survey has a whole section on “Manipulating Persons versus Manipulating Situations” (2026, §4.1), which reports Riker's *heresthetic*, glossed as “structuring the world so you can win” (Riker 1986: ix); Warwick and Kelman's contrast between “environmental” and “psychic” manipulation; and Anne Barnhill's distinction between manipulation that “changes the options available to the person or changes the situation she's in, and thereby changes her attitudes” and manipulation that changes attitudes directly (Barnhill 2014: 53). §4.2 canvasses manipulation without conscious intent and by algorithms whose designers intended nothing of the kind; §4.3 notes that gaslighting and “negging” “may work to increase the target's vulnerability to further manipulation”, which is explicitly sequential.

So the category of situational, unintended, self-compounding influence exists. Why is Aurum not a case of it? Because of the clause I italicised in Barnhill's formulation. Situational manipulation changes options *and thereby changes attitudes*; Mara's attitudes are exactly what they were. Claudia Mills, drawing the same distinction, expressly reserves the label “manipulation” for interference with the “internal or subjective features” of the choice situation rather than the “external or objective” ones (Mills 1995: 97). And Raz's contrast, which the entry quotes, states the gap with unusual clarity: “manipulation, unlike coercion, does not interfere with a person's options. Instead it perverts the way that person reaches decisions, forms preferences, or adopts goals” (Raz 1988: 377). The taxonomy is exhaustive only if interference with options is coercion. Aurum's conduct alters the value of Mara's options without coercing, and leaves her deliberation immaculate. It falls between Raz's two chairs.

**Exploitation.** Here the draft misdescribed the field, and the correction sharpens rather than blunts the thesis. It is not true that the exploitation literature treats the victim's want of alternatives as something the exploiter merely finds. Hillel Steiner's account holds that “exploitation occurs when A gains more from an interaction, and B gains less, than they would have were it not for the existence of a prior injustice”, the illustration being an employer able to pay two dollars an hour “only because A (or someone else) has previously unjustly deprived B of alternative sources of labor” (Zwolinski, Ferguson and Wertheimer 2022, §2.2.2.2, reporting Steiner 1984). Roemer's property-relations account and Sample's are in the same family; Goodin, Vrousalis and Zwolinski are the dissenters, holding that the source of the vulnerability is irrelevant or that antecedent injustice is neither necessary nor sufficient (§2.2.2.2).

The ratchet is therefore best located as a counterexample to the historical accounts rather than as a gap in an incurious literature. Steiner requires a *prior injustice*. Aurum commits none: its contributions were benefits, competitively priced, freely accepted, and better than nothing on every estimate available at the time. Yet Mara's alternatives at step (4) are as impoverished as if someone had wronged her, and the impoverishment is fully explained by Aurum's conduct. What the case exhibits is baseline degradation with no antecedent injustice anywhere in the chain.

The **nonworseness claim** needs correcting too. Its canonical form is: “Interaction between A and B cannot be worse than non-interaction when A has a right not to interact with B at all, and when the interaction is mutually advantageous, consensual, and free from negative externalities” (Zwolinski, Ferguson and Wertheimer 2022, §3, crediting Wertheimer 1996, 2011; Zwolinski 2009; Powell and Zwolinski 2012). The draft dropped the first condition and overstated the claim's standing; in fact “most exploitation theorists are skeptical that the NWC is correct” (§3). Both corrections are useful. The dropped condition is one I should want: by step (4) it is arguable that Aurum no longer has a clean right not to interact with Mara at all, precisely because of what steps (1)–(3) did. That is a second and independent route to my conclusion.

Finally, I must concede a point the draft evaded. On Wertheimer's fair-market-value test — a fair price is the price goods “would fetch in a hypothetical competitive market that excludes asymmetries of information and undue pressure”, one at which neither party “takes special unfair advantage of… special vulnerabilities in the other party's situation” (§2.2.2.1, quoting Wertheimer 1996: 232) — step (4) looks like straightforward exploitation. I agree. But this is not a rival to my thesis; it is a demand for it. The test is silent about *which* hypothetical competitive market: the one Mara faced in 2016, or the one she faces in 2024 with skills only Aurum can use. Those give opposite verdicts, and nothing in the account chooses between them. What baseline capture supplies is exactly the missing index, together with a reason for it that is not distributive at all.

**The hold-up problem.** The mechanism is not new, and the draft should have said so. It is the hold-up problem from the economics of relational contracting. Klein, Crawford and Alchian identify “appropriable specialized quasi rents” as the source: the quasi-rent value of an asset is “the excess of its value over its salvage value, that is, its value in its next best use”, and “after a specific investment is made and such quasi rents are created, the possibility of opportunistic behavior is very real” (1978: 298). Two of their observations help me. First, “an appropriable quasi rent can occur with no market closure or restrictions placed on rival assets” (299) — confirming that lock-in requires no fence, no monopoly and no interference. Second, they treat the problem as one of efficiency: opportunism “is likely to result in a loss of efficiency and not just a wealth-distribution effect” (301), and the remedies are vertical integration or contracts.

The differences are where my contribution lies. Their opportunism “by definition refers to unanticipated non-fulfillment of the contract” (300); Aurum fulfils every contract it makes. Their question is what arrangements economise on the losses; mine is what the party who benefits may say for itself. An efficiency loss is a cost to be minimised. A defeated justification is something owed to a person.

## 4. The structure

Let B deal with A at stages *i* = 1, …, *n*. At stage *i*, write **r**ᵢ for B's *reservation value*, the value to B of her best alternative to accepting A's proposal; **u**ᵢ for the value to B of accepting; and **σ**ᵢ for the total gains from trade available at that stage, so that B's share is uᵢ − rᵢ and A's is σᵢ − (uᵢ − rᵢ). A proposal is *acceptable* iff uᵢ ≥ rᵢ.

The standard picture treats reservation values as **exogenous**: B brings her alternatives to the table, A's proposal is assessed against them, and consent given uᵢ ≥ rᵢ transfers permission. A ratchet is what happens when reservation values are **endogenous** — when accepting at stage *i* itself lowers rᵢ₊₁.

That is what Mara's four episodes accomplish. Relocation thins the set of employers she can reach; specialisation raises her Aurum-specific productivity relative to her general productivity; deferral attaches an explicit price to exit. Each is a benefit conferred and accepted. Each lowers the value of *not dealing with Aurum next time*.

> **Failure of composition.** The property *consensual, mutually beneficial, and no worse for B than not transacting* is closed under sequential composition only if reservation values are exogenous. Where they are endogenous, uᵢ ≥ rᵢ can hold at every stage while uₙ < r₁.

This is the sharpest formal point in the essay and I state it plainly rather than burying it. A party who can influence the counterparty's alternatives can satisfy the nonworseness condition *at every step while walking the counterparty downhill*. There is no step at which it is violated, and no step at which its satisfaction is any evidence that the counterparty is being well treated. (A corollary: because A's share is σᵢ − (uᵢ − rᵢ), a falling rᵢ lets A hold uᵢ down while keeping every proposal acceptable. This is capture without a single unacceptable offer. I claim no monotonicity result; that would require assumptions about σᵢ I have not made.)

Three near neighbours should be set aside. Quinn's **self-torturer** faces a device with 1001 settings where “the increments in current are so small that the self-torturer cannot tell the difference between adjacent settings”, is paid $10,000 for each advance, and can never permanently go back (Andreou 2025, §1.3, reporting Quinn 1993). Everything load-bearing there comes from imperceptibility and intransitivity; Mara's steps are large and discrete, her preferences transitive, her comparisons accurate, and no second party profits. The **money pump** (Davidson, McKinsey and Suppes 1955, as reported in Andreou 2025, §1.3) likewise runs on intransitivity: its victim is rationally defective, and Mara is not. A ratchet pumps a perfectly coherent agent. Parfit's **harmless torturers** (1984) distribute a harm across many agents whose individual contributions are imperceptible; a ratchet has two parties and perceptible differences throughout.

## 5. Independence, not authorship

Grant the structure. What exactly fails?

Begin with what consent tells us. When B consents, she certifies a *comparison*: that, as she sees it, accepting beats her best alternative. She does not certify that the terms are fair, that the transaction is good for her, or that A has treated her decently. Ordinarily this thin certification is nonetheless powerful, because the alternative against which the comparison is made is *hers*: constituted by the world, by her other relationships, by her skills and savings and the offers of other people. A proposal that beats *that* is likely to be worth having, and her preferring it is a fact about her own situation to which deference is owed.

Now ask what has to be true for that inference to work. Not that B's alternatives are good; they may be dreadful through no one's doing, and her consent still justifies. What is required is that the comparison be *independent of the party invoking it* — that its coming out in A's favour not be explained by A's own conduct. This is an instance of a familiar epistemic norm: evidence loses its force for a party in proportion as that party produced it.

> **Independence Principle.** Consent certifies that the consented-to option beat the consenter's best alternative. That certification supports A's justification only to the extent that the poverty of the alternative is not explained by A's conduct. Where it is, the certification is one A's conduct helped secure, and is to that extent justificatorily idle.

The draft said “authored” rather than “explained by”, and claimed that any offeror who can lower the alternative “can make the certification come out true” at will. That was wrong, and the objection is decisive against it. Aurum could not secure Mara's 2024 consent at will: it required four acceptances, each of which she was right to make and any of which she could have declined. Compare three ways B's outside option can fall: (a) A removes options B would otherwise have had; (b) B's own choices narrow her prospects, as when a generalist becomes a harpsichord specialist; (c) A offers B options whose acceptance predictably narrows her future prospects, and B accepts. Nobody thinks (b) undermines B's later consent; everybody thinks (a) does. Aurum is (c), and (c) is not (a).

But the evidential point does not run on causal unilaterality, and that is why the reframing is an improvement rather than a retreat. What defeats the inference from “she agreed” to “the terms are defensible” is that the comparison is *self-serving*, and a comparison is self-serving when the party citing it is the party whose conduct explains why one side of it is so low. Mediation through B's own rational acceptances does not break that explanation; it is part of it. Aurum selected proposals whose acceptance was foreseeable precisely because they were good for Mara. Nor does mediation help in case (b), for a different reason: in (b) no one is invoking the comparison in his own favour, so there is no conflict of interest to discount for.

The electoral analogy now says what I want it to say. That a candidate won a free and fair vote is powerful evidence of the electorate's will — unless the candidate shaped the ballot. No individual vote was invalid; every voter was competent, informed about the candidates standing, and unpressured. The election is useless as a warrant *for that candidate* for a reason internal to what elections are for, and the reason is conflict of interest, not causation. It does not matter whether he struck rivals off unilaterally or made them each an attractive offer to stand down.

Because explanation comes in degrees, so does independence, and so does the principle's bite. This is a virtue. It yields **Inverse Reliance** — the justificatory weight A may place on B's consent varies inversely with the degree to which A's conduct explains the poverty of B's alternatives — as a graded consequence rather than a stipulation.

Two clarifications.

*Mara's consent is valid.* She really did permit Aurum to do what it did; Aurum commits no battery, no trespass, no breach. The normative power was exercised and it worked.

*Mara's consent does not justify.* Validity and justificatory force come apart. And now I can say what needs justifying, which the draft left unsaid. The object is the **terms** Aurum obtains at step (4) — more exactly, Aurum's share of the surplus, the part of it that the fall in Mara's reservation value made available. Making a proposal is not in general an act requiring justification against a counterfactual baseline; taking a larger share because the other party's exit has become expensive is. Aurum's defence — “she agreed” — is unavailable for that, not because the agreement was defective but because the comparison it certifies is one Aurum's own conduct explains.

What must Aurum show instead? Not, as the draft suggested, that Mara is paid what someone with her talents would command had she never dealt with Aurum. That test is caught in a dilemma: it either prices her 2016 generalist skills, ignoring the productivity Aurum financed, or prices her present skills on a market that does not exist. The right benchmark is neither. It is the **division of the relationship-specific surplus**: Aurum must show that its share at step (4) is one it could have obtained when Mara's reservation value was still independent of the relationship — that is, that the terms track the bargain available ex ante rather than the leverage available ex post. This asks a question about a division, not about a phantom wage, and it is the shape that severance rules, notice periods, portability requirements and non-compete bans already have.

## 6. Mill, and the reliance that runs both ways

In Chapter V of *On Liberty*, Mill explains why a contract of self-enslavement should be void:

> “The reason for not interfering, unless for the sake of others, with a person's voluntary acts, is consideration for his liberty. His voluntary choice is evidence that what he so chooses is desirable, or at the least endurable, to him, and his good is on the whole best provided for by allowing him to take his own means of pursuing it. But by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it, beyond that single act. He therefore defeats, in his own case, the very purpose which is the justification of allowing him to dispose of himself. He is no longer free; but is thenceforth in a position which has no longer the presumption in its favour, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it.” (Mill 1859, pp. 194–95)

Two features deserve more attention than they get. First, Mill's ground is **evidential**: choice earns deference because it is *evidence* of the chooser's good. The deference is therefore defeasible exactly as evidence is. Second, the evidence is **renewed by occupancy**: the warrant for treating someone's situation as good for them comes not from a single past act of choosing but from the continuing fact that they could leave and do not.

Mill then writes that “[t]hese reasons, the force of which is so conspicuous in this peculiar case, are evidently of far wider application; yet a limit is everywhere set to them by the necessities of life, which continually require, not indeed that we should resign our freedom, but that we should consent to this and the other limitation of it” (p. 195). The draft described the second clause as a retreat. It is not; it is one semicolon-joined sentence, and the clause says only that the wider application cannot void every constraining engagement. I withdraw the charge.

What I retain is the argument. Mill is looking for a *threshold* — a quantity of freedom so large that alienating it defeats the point of protecting it — which is why he finds only the limit case. His premises deliver a *gradient* instead. If the warrant for deference is that choice is evidence of the chooser's good, and if that evidence is sustained by the ability to leave, then the warrant degrades continuously as the ability to leave degrades. I should not have presented this as unnoticed: variable voluntariness is a going concern in the literature, and Serena Olsaretti's analysis — “[a] choice is voluntary if and only if it is not made because there is no acceptable alternative to it” (Olsaretti 2004: 139, as quoted in Anderson 2023, §3.3) — is squarely in this territory, as is Feinberg's treatment (1986: 233–244, as cited in Eyal 2025, §5.3).

The draft also claimed Mill failed to see that his second premise is exploitable by anyone who can raise the cost of leaving. That was unfair, and the correction is the more interesting result. Two pages later Mill writes:

> “When a person, either by express promise or by conduct, has encouraged another to rely upon his continuing to act in a certain way—to build expectations and calculations, and stake any part of his plan of life upon that supposition, a new series of moral obligations arises on his part towards that person, which may possibly be overruled, but cannot be ignored.” (pp. 196–97)

This is a course of conduct, not a single act, generating obligations because of the reliance it induces. Mill saw the structure. What he did not do is apply it in the direction my case needs. His reliance principle constrains the party who would *withdraw* from a relationship on which another has staked her plan of life. The ratchet asks what is owed by the party who *benefits* from that staking. The two are complements, and the second follows from the same thought: if inducing reliance generates obligations because it shapes what the other can now do, then inducing reliance and then pricing against it generates obligations too. Mill's own principle, run forwards instead of backwards, is the Commitment Condition of §7.

A brief Kantian note, since the vocabulary of using persons is the natural home for this worry. Accounts of treating another merely as a means divide into those asking whether the other *could* consent — “an agent uses another merely as a means if the other cannot consent to her use of him” (O'Neill 1989: 113, as reported in Kerstein 2023, §3.2) — and those asking whether the other *has* consented (Nozick 1974: 30–31; Scanlon 2008: 107; Kleingeld 2020: 398, as reported in §3.3). Aurum's case is awkward for both branches, because Mara can consent and does. Neither branch asks who made refusing so costly. I do not claim to refute either; I note that the question my case raises is one the taxonomy does not contain a slot for.

## 7. The positive view

> **Historicity of Consent (HC).** Whether B's consent to A's φ-ing justifies A's φ-ing depends not only on the synchronic properties of the consent — capacity, information, voluntariness — but on the causal history of B's option set, and specifically on the role A's conduct plays in explaining it.

HC needs a discriminating criterion, since *every* relationship alters the parties' alternatives. Deep friendships, marriages, apprenticeships and citizenship all reduce the attractiveness of exit; that is partly what they are for. The draft's criterion was conduct-based: A ratchets iff its course of dealing predictably lowers B's reservation value *and* A uses the lowering to obtain better terms. That does not work. It catches every internal labour market, and it catches the firm that cuts wages because it is failing rather than because it can. Worse, “uses” smuggles in the intention that the account elsewhere disowns.

The right repair is to make the criterion **positional** rather than behavioural, on the model of fiduciary duty, where a party who acquires discretionary power over another's interests is barred from self-dealing regardless of intent and regardless of the beneficiary's agreement.

> **Baseline-capture position.** A occupies the baseline-capture position with respect to B when A's course of dealing with B predictably lowers B's reservation value in dealings with A.
>
> **Duty of forbearance.** One who occupies that position may not invoke B's consent as a complete justification for terms that B's prior reservation value would not have permitted. A must justify such terms by some independent showing.

Three consequences, each of which I accept.

First, this is intention-free, so it reaches the cases I care about most: a platform whose network effects and data lock-in steadily reduce users' exit options, and a migration system that ties visa status to a single sponsor. Nobody designs these. The duty falls on whoever occupies the position and is able to act on it. There can be **blameless baseline capture** that nonetheless defeats the consent defence.

Second, yes, every internal labour market occupies the position. I bite this bullet, because the conclusion is not that internal labour markets are impermissible but that they are a standing site of justificatory burden — which is exactly why we regulate them, and regulate them in the ways §8 describes. The failing firm occupies the position too, and the account gets it right: that firm also may not say “she agreed”; it must say “we were insolvent”. Which is what it would say, and which is a justification benchmarked to something other than the consent. The spouse whose partner gave up a career, and who then insists on staying in the city he prefers, likewise cannot rest on “you agreed”; he must say that the arrangement is fair given everything each has done. That, too, is what we actually think. A theory that predicts our practice here is doing better than one that exempts benign relationships by fiat.

Third, dependence does not license reliance on the dependent party's agreement; it withdraws that licence. This inverts a common intuition and is, I think, correct.

Finally:

> **Commitment Condition (CC).** Where A's course of dealing predictably impoverishes B's alternatives, A may restore the justificatory force of B's later consent only by having bound itself in advance, in ways B can rely on, against using that impoverishment to obtain better terms.

CC shows why the natural remedy — *disclose more* — cannot work. Suppose Aurum in 2016 had told Mara the truth: “this will make you cheaper to us in eight years.” The draft said she would have nothing to price, because Aurum's future discretion “does not exist as a fact”. That was too quick: agents price others' future discretion constantly, in at-will discounts and risk premia. The correct claim is different and, I think, stronger. Mara can indeed price a distribution over Aurum's future conduct, and a lock-in premium would compensate her for bearing that risk. But **pricing a risk is not authorising what eventuates.** A discount is money; consent is a permission; and there is at t₁ no determinate proposal for her to permit, only a range from which Aurum will later choose. Insurance against an outcome does not license it. So the duty generated by baseline capture is not a duty to inform but a duty to *bind* — to give up some of the latitude that the benefit creates.

This yields a stronger thesis than the one I set out with, and I state it as the essay's conclusion:

> **Consent is justificatorily effective only over option sets that the party invoking it cannot subsequently reshape, or has bound itself not to reshape.**

CC is unusual in a further way. Most conditions on valid consent are conditions on the consenter's epistemic or volitional state. CC is a condition on the *offeror's* normative situation: certain parties may avail themselves of the consent defence only if they have first disabled themselves. Its single-agent cousin is the literature on plans and resoluteness — Bratman, Gauthier, McClennen, Odysseus at the mast (Andreou 2025, §2.4). What is new here is that the commitment runs to another party's benefit and functions as a condition on the committer's standing to invoke consent at all.

## 8. Objections

**(a) “Mara was imprudent.”** Perhaps she should have foreseen the trajectory. Grant it. The claim at issue is that *Aurum's* justification fails, and a defective performance by B does not manufacture a warrant for A. In any case the concession is small: declining step (1) was worse than accepting on every estimate available to her, and Aurum had committed to nothing.

**(b) “Which step is impermissible?”** The question presupposes that my conclusion is a verdict of impermissibility. It is not; it is that a particular defence is unavailable for the step-(4) terms. Still, the underlying worry is real, so consider the options. One might hold that permissibility agglomerates over sequences, so that if each act is permissible the course of dealing is. I reject that; the property that defeats the consent defence — that the comparison Aurum invokes is one its own conduct explains — is relational and historical, and no single act instantiates it. Alternatively one might locate the failure at step (4) alone. But then ask *why* step (4) fails: every synchronic fact about it is benign, and the only available answer cites the history, which concedes Historicity. Either way the synchronic picture is false.

The two analogies the draft used here — a fair coin toss in a rigged game, a legal punch in a fixed fight — were bad, because each presupposes a further hidden wrongful act, which is what the case excludes. Better analogies are evidential. Every measurement may be made correctly with a properly functioning instrument, and the series still be worthless as evidence for the hypothesis of the person who calibrated the instrument. Every statement a witness makes may be true, and his testimony still worthless because he selected which events to witness. In neither case is any part faulty; in neither case is anything concealed; in both, the whole fails to support the conclusion of the party who assembled it.

**(c) “This is structural injustice, not a defeated justification.”** One might keep agglomeration, deny that the course of dealing has any deontic status over and above its parts, and say only that the *outcome* is unjust — a defect in the distribution or the institution, not in anyone's standing. This is the most serious objection, and §7's concession of blameless capture invites it. My reply distinguishes blame from answerability. To lose the consent defence is not to be blamed; it is to owe a justification. One can owe a justification without having done wrong, as anyone who has had to explain a decision knows. The structural reading, by contrast, leaves nobody answerable, and the case is built so that there is a determinate party who benefits, who is able to act, and to whom the question “why are these your terms?” can be addressed. It would be strange to reply “ask the institution”.

**(d) “You are covertly appealing to Aurum's intentions.”** No. The criterion in §7 is positional: it refers to what a course of dealing predictably does, not to what anyone meant. Intention may bear on blame; it does not bear on who must justify.

**(e) “This is paternalism.”** It is the opposite. Paternalist conclusions restrict what the weaker party may do. Mine restricts what the stronger party may *say in its own defence*. Mara may accept step (4); nothing here interferes with her. Telling Mara she may not sign and telling Aurum that her signature will not by itself exonerate it are different acts.

**(f) “She was compensated for each erosion.”** Suppose the relocation bonus, training and equity were priced to include a premium for lock-in, so that Mara received the full expected value of the bargaining power she surrendered. The distributive complaint may vanish. Does the justificatory one? The draft said no, on the ground that payment does not undo staging. That begged the question, as the objection presses: a fully priced 2016 bargain is itself struck against an independent baseline. So I concede more than the draft did. If in 2016 Aurum had *bound itself* to the whole trajectory, terms included, then Mara's 2016 consent would have been given against a baseline Aurum's conduct did not explain, and it would justify. What it did instead was buy an option and keep the discretion. Paying for a risk you retain the power to resolve in your own favour is not the same as selling that power. Pressed properly, then, the compensation objection does not refute CC; it converges on it.

**(g) “CC has a perverse implication: a firm that binds and then extracts is vindicated, while a firm that binds nothing but behaves decently is not.”** The implication is real but not perverse, because the account is about the availability of a defence, not a ranking of characters. A firm that bound itself in 2016 and extracts within the bound has a justification other than “she agreed”: the ex ante bargain, made when the baseline was independent. A firm that bound nothing but behaves decently is not condemned; it simply cannot rest on consent, and must instead show that its terms are reasonable — which, being reasonable, it easily can. Nobody is convicted by CC. Some parties are merely required to argue.

**(h) “This is Cohen's collective unfreedom.”** An instructive cousin. Cohen imagines ten people in a locked room with a single heavy key: whoever takes it can leave, but then “whatever happens, at least nine people will remain in the room” (Cohen 1983, p. 9). Each is individually free to go; together they suffer “collective unfreedom” (p. 11), each being free “only on condition that the others do not exercise their similarly conditional freedom” (p. 11). That is a failure of aggregation across persons at a time; mine is across times for one person. Cohen also insists that attributions of freedom carry two temporal indices — one is forced *at t* to act *at t+n* (p. 8) — and uses them to argue that most proletarians are not forced, because escape routes stay open long enough. The ratchet is the complement: the exits stay open at every moment, and the agent nonetheless ends where nobody would have sent her, because the party she deals with has been buying up the value of her going. Cohen's jailer installs photoelectric devices. Aurum installs nothing. It only gives.

## 9. What follows

**For normative powers.** Powers are usually individuated by their conditions of successful exercise, and consent's conditions have been theorised synchronically. The ratchet shows that a power can be successfully exercised and still fail to transmit justification, because its *point* — the evidential relation between a person's choices and her good — has been defeated by the provenance of the conditions under which it was exercised. The general lesson is that a normative power's justificatory yield can be laundered, and that the test is independence: whether the power's exercise is independent of the party who stands to benefit from it. Promises, waivers, authorisations and delegations are candidates for analogous treatment. I should flag that this conjecture is not virgin territory: bioethics already recognises “the good of independent decisionmaking” as something “no choice” situations can undermine even where they are not coercive (Millum and Garnett 2019, as reported in Eyal 2025, §5.3).

**For practice.** The burden shift has determinate institutional shape. Where a party predictably erodes another's alternatives, we should expect, and can defend, requirements that reconstruct an independent baseline: portable rather than firm-specific credentials, vesting on departure, notice periods and severance scaled to specific investment, bans on non-competes, data portability, visa status detached from a single employer, cooling-off rules in long-term consumer contracts. These are usually defended as welfare measures or as corrections for unequal bargaining power. The present argument gives them a different warrant: they are the conditions under which the stronger party's appeal to the weaker party's consent remains honest. They are not restrictions on freedom of contract but preconditions of its justificatory work. Note that they are also close relatives of the contractual solutions the hold-up literature recommends for efficiency reasons — which should not surprise us, since the same structure is being addressed, and is one reason to think the diagnosis is on to something real.

**For moral theory.** We are well supplied with vocabulary for wrongs located in an act, and reasonably well supplied for wrongs located in a character or a policy. We are poorly supplied for justificatory failures located in a *sequence* whose every member is sound. Parfit taught us that harms can be assembled from imperceptible parts. The ratchet teaches something adjacent: that a party can lose the right to rely on another's agreement through a course of dealing composed entirely of perceptible, consensual, mutually beneficial acts, with no imperceptibility, no irrationality, no deception and no antecedent injustice anywhere in the chain.

Three uncertainties remain. The first is measurement: the burden-shifted justification appeals to a counterfactual division of surplus, and such counterfactuals are contestable. I take this to be an implementation problem — courts estimate comparable counterfactuals in wrongful-dismissal and reliance damages — but I have not shown it. The second is scope: I have given a positional sufficient condition for one defect, not a general theory of which histories matter. The third is priority. I could not read Arto Laitinen's “Normative Powers, Agency, and Time”, and although its published description suggests a different question, I cannot certify non-overlap; and the searches behind my novelty claim are weak evidence at best.

Meanwhile the immediate lesson is blunt enough. If you want to know whether someone's agreement licenses what you are doing to them, do not only ask whether they agreed freely. Ask whether the alternatives they were comparing it against are ones your own conduct explains.
