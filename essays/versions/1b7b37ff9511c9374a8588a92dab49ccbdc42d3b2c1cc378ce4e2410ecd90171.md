# When a New Concept Makes Fewer Complaints

## Abstract

A conceptual reform can change both what a community means by a disputed term and what its procedures count as an objection. When the number of recorded objections falls after such a reform, the decline is compatible with at least two explanations: fewer people have reason to object, or the revised category and its surrounding procedures make objections harder to record or recognize. The count alone cannot distinguish them. This essay develops a limited warning about evidence: a reduction in recorded objections supports a claim of improved deliberation only when the reformers can show that relevant objections still have a sufficiently comparable route into the process, or can rely on independent evidence that tests the same question. This is not a general duty to preserve old concepts, a veto for dissenters, or a claim that a policy is unjustified whenever communication fails. Existing work on conceptual revision, epistemic injustice, and deliberative uptake already supplies much of the normative ground. The possible contribution is narrower: to identify how a change in the concept used to classify objections can make complaint counts non-comparable evidence of deliberative success. Whether that application is original remains unsettled.

## 1. A quiet record after reform

Imagine a public agency that distributes a monthly benefit. For years its review process accepts complaints described as unfair treatment. The agency then adopts a new concept of fairness: every eligible recipient receives the same cash amount. The new rule makes decisions easier to explain and may remove arbitrary differences. After the change, the agency records far fewer fairness complaints. Officials point to the decline as evidence that the reform improved deliberation: people are apparently more satisfied, and the old disputes have subsided.

The record admits another explanation. Some recipients may still face higher costs and less access to the benefit, but the new form accepts only complaints about whether equal payments were correctly issued. A person who can no longer make the old fairness claim may also be unable to get a claim about unequal access reviewed. The count falls because the rule now records fewer things as objections. The agency might have improved the policy; it has not yet shown that the deliberative process improved.

The issue is not whether the old meaning of “fair” must survive. It is not even whether the new rule is good. The issue is what evidence supports a particular claim about the process that produced it. A reform may improve outcomes while reducing opportunities to challenge those outcomes. Conversely, an accessible process may hear and reject a strong objection without losing deliberative value. Three questions must therefore be kept apart: Did the policy work? Was the decision process deliberatively adequate? What evidence supports either conclusion?

The modest thesis here concerns the third question. When defenders of a conceptual reform cite agreement, silence, or lower objection counts as evidence that deliberation improved, the count is informative only if the reform has not made relevant objections materially harder to express, record, or receive consideration—or if independent evidence addresses that change. A concept need not retain its former wording. An alternative route can preserve the objection’s role. Nor must every objection be accepted. The warning is about a particular inference from a quieter record, not a general condition on whether reform may proceed.

## 2. Changing a concept can change the evidence channel

Conceptual reform is not inherently suspicious. Sally Haslanger’s purpose-sensitive approach asks what work a concept should do and whether a different concept would better serve legitimate purposes in a particular context. This approach allows deliberate revision; it does not require that every familiar use be preserved. But concepts used in social and political life also shape what people can recognize and communicate. A proposed improvement must be assessed partly in the setting where it will operate (Haslanger 2000).

This point matters because disagreement about a term can also concern a worldly matter. Plunkett and Sundell argue that a dispute over how to use an expression can carry disagreements about the interests, recognition, or practical consequences at stake. A reformer cannot settle such a dispute by calling it merely verbal. At the same time, it is a mistake to assume that every disagreement survives unchanged when the term is replaced. The question is whether the reason or consequence that mattered can still enter the new discussion (Plunkett and Sundell 2019).

Work on communicative exclusion makes the general risk familiar. Young criticizes narrow discussion standards that can silence or devalue some groups. Fricker describes how unequal shared interpretive resources can leave people at a disadvantage in understanding or conveying their experience. Neither point implies that an old label must remain authoritative. They show why the presence of a sentence, form, or meeting is not by itself proof that a concern can become intelligible and matter in the decision (Young 1996; Fricker 2017).

Mary Scudder’s account of deliberative uptake gives the closest framework for this essay. Uptake, on her account, involves the fair consideration of what citizens communicate, not simply formal inclusion and not necessarily agreement. She also addresses how uptake might be assessed: participant perceptions and recurring complaints can help identify failures, but they are not conclusive, and further engagement may be needed. A considered objection can be rejected without giving its speaker a right to dictate the outcome (Scudder 2020). That framework already rules out a broad new principle that conceptual reformers must preserve a usable objection route. The remaining question is narrower: what happens when the reform changes the channel through which a process counts and evaluates objections?

A complaint count is produced by a filter. Someone must have a concern, choose or manage to express it, reach a reporting channel, have the report classified as a complaint, and have it included in the count. A lower count can reflect change at any of these points. The same number can also mean different things before and after a redefinition. If an agency previously counted objections about unequal access but now counts only incorrect payment amounts, the apparent decline is not a stable measure of the same class of objections.

This is a basic problem of comparison. Let a recorded objection be one that passes the current process’s steps for expression, recognition, and recording. A fall in recorded objections is evidence of fewer underlying objections only if the relation between relevant objections and recorded cases is sufficiently stable, or if the change in that relation is independently measured. Conceptual reform can change the relation itself. No sophisticated statistical model is required to see the point: if a new door label also changes which visitors are admitted, a shorter guest list cannot by itself show that fewer people wanted to attend.

For clarity, call a reform **expressively closing** for a particular objection when the reform’s vocabulary or procedures make that objection unavailable to the process that is being used to assess the reform. The label concerns a relation between an objection and a procedure, not a word by itself. Retiring a term does not establish closure if speakers can use another vocabulary and have the same reason recognized and considered. Conversely, a blank field for “other concerns” does not establish an adequate route if officials have no practice of treating those entries as reasons for review.

The evidence problem has two distinct versions. The first is **recording loss**: an objection cannot be entered or is not coded as one. The second is **failed uptake**: a concern reaches the forum but is not understood or considered as a relevant reason. Counts can reveal the first only when their categories cover the relevant concern. A tally alone cannot establish the second. The two should not be collapsed: recording fewer complaints does not show that the process heard the objections and found them unpersuasive.

## 3. The argument

The inference can be stated in four steps.

1. A claim that deliberation improved is a claim about how a decision was reached and how participants’ reasons could affect that process. It differs from a claim that the policy produced better outcomes.

2. A lower count of objections can support a claim about deliberative improvement only if the count remains responsive to the objections relevant to that claim. A measure that stops recording a class of objections cannot show that the class has disappeared.

3. A conceptual reform can change the categories or procedures used to state, receive, and record objections. This creates a live alternative explanation for a decline: the process may now capture less, not the participants may have less to object to.

4. Therefore, when a reform changes the evidence channel, a fall in the resulting count is not by itself evidence that deliberation improved. The inference needs evidence that the route remained comparable or an independent check that addresses the missing possibility.

The conclusion is conditional and defeasible. It does not say that objection counts are useless. If a reform changes terminology while maintaining a reliable route for relevant concerns, a lower count may be informative. If a process demonstrates that the new form captures the same access problems, or independently checks whether those problems continue, then the new count can contribute to an assessment. The point is that the count cannot validate its own changed classification rule.

The argument also needs a boundary around “relevant objections.” It does not require officials to anticipate every possible complaint. A concern is relevant here when it challenges a reason the reformers themselves offer, or points to a reasonably foreseeable effect that would count against a stated purpose. In the benefit example, unequal access is relevant if the reform is defended as making the benefit fairer or more usable. A complaint need not be correct to be relevant; the process can examine evidence and reject it. Relevance concerns whether it bears on the claim being assessed, not whether it wins.

That boundary cannot be settled solely by the reformers’ preferred definition. If the new concept excludes the very feature that its justification treats as important, the exclusion needs an explanation outside the label. Otherwise the reform makes its own success easier to declare by stipulation. Yet the requirement is limited: an objection that challenges neither the public reasons nor a foreseeable cost of the reform need not be included in this particular assessment. A process may have other obligations to its participants, but those are not established by the present argument.

## 4. A case that separates policy evidence from deliberative evidence

Return to the hypothetical benefit program. Under the old rule, recipients submit complaints under a broad fairness category. The agency changes to equal payments and replaces the form. The new form records only payment errors. It shows fewer complaints, but it does not ask whether the benefit remains accessible to recipients with unusual costs.

The form provides no basis for concluding that discussion improved. It could be that the new policy resolved the disagreements. It could be that recipients now describe the same issue as an access problem but have no recognized route for asking the agency to reconsider. The count does not distinguish these possibilities. The agency can still justify equal payments on other grounds, and the benefit may be distributed more reliably. But those would be policy or administrative claims; the decrease in complaints, without more, does not establish deliberative success.

Now change the process. Recipients may describe an access problem in their own words. Staff record those concerns, assess them under the same review system, and give a reasoned response. The old word “fairness” is no longer required. An objection may be rejected, and the agency may still conclude that uniform payments best serve its aims. In this version, the conceptual change has not by itself closed the route. Whether lower counts now indicate fewer objections depends on the quality of the channel and how it is compared over time, but the reform no longer earns suspicion merely because the label changed.

A further check might compare a sample of submissions under both categories, invite affected recipients to describe concerns without choosing from the agency’s labels, or audit whether the new system records foreseeable access costs. These are possible ways to test comparability, not a mandatory checklist or guarantee of fairness. No single method proves that every relevant objection was available. The necessary evidence depends on the reform’s own claim and on the known ways its categories could filter objections.

Finally, imagine that an independent audit finds the benefit now reaches recipients at similar rates, including those with unusually high costs. This may support the claim that the policy improved access. It does not by itself show that deliberation improved, because outcome evidence and process evidence answer different questions. If officials claim both, they need evidence for both. Keeping those claims separate prevents the warning from becoming a veto: a reform may be worthwhile even when its deliberative record is incomplete.

## 5. Objections and replies

### 5.1 “The literature already says this”

The strongest objection is that the proposed point adds nothing to existing accounts of conceptual reform, epistemic injustice, and uptake. Haslanger already makes conceptual choice purpose-sensitive and politically contextual. Young and Fricker analyze exclusion from communicative resources and practices. Scudder argues that formal inclusion does not secure fair consideration, explains that uptake differs from agreement, and discusses the use of complaints and participant perceptions when assessing uptake. A process that makes objections unrecordable already looks like a failure of inclusion or uptake. Recasting it as a problem about counts may simply redescribe those views.

This objection defeats any claim to a new general norm. The argument here accepts that result. It does not propose a rival theory of democratic inclusion, a new right to be heard, or a new standard for which concepts should be adopted. Its narrower aim is to isolate an inference that a process evaluator can make after a concept changes: a fall in objections classified by that concept can be caused by a change in classification, even when the count is presented as evidence of improved deliberation. The contribution, if there is one, is a measurement-focused application of uptake and conceptual-engineering ideas.

That application may also be already present in work not read here, including the full texts of works available only as metadata and parts of the deliberative literature not searched. The present argument therefore does not claim field-wide originality. If a fuller comparison finds this same inference and test already developed, this essay should be treated as an illustration rather than a new contribution.

### 5.2 “A complaint count was never evidence of agreement”

Correctly, a low complaint count is not identical to agreement. It can nonetheless be used as indirect evidence: an evaluator may infer that fewer people object, or that the process is producing less unresolved disagreement. Such evidence is always defeasible. The argument does not make it uniquely uncertain; it identifies a specific reason for caution when the category for recording objections changes at the same time as the count.

Nor does the argument insist on a fixed vocabulary. A new label can be a more accurate tool, and objections may remain expressible in other terms. The question is empirical and procedural: did the new route continue to capture the kinds of objection that matter to the reform’s claim? If so, a decline may be informative. If not, the count cannot bear the same interpretation without further support.

### 5.3 “Every objection need not receive uptake”

A process cannot be required to accept every claim, and some claims should not influence a decision. This essay requires neither acceptance nor unlimited agenda control. Uptake, as Scudder uses the term, is fair consideration rather than agreement or obedience. A process may conclude that an objection is unsupported, irrelevant to its remit, or outweighed by other reasons. It should not treat an objection as answered merely because a revised concept has made the objection’s wording inadmissible.

The proposed boundary also narrows the class of objections relevant to the inference. It covers objections that challenge a reason offered for the reform or identify a reasonably foreseeable cost bearing on its announced purpose. It does not make every imaginable possibility a deliberative failure. The distinction is between allowing a reason to be evaluated and letting the reason dictate the outcome.

### 5.4 “This invites an endless dispute about scope”

A reformer could define the aim narrowly, and a challenger could then demand another forum to contest that definition, followed by another forum to contest the next boundary. This is a real danger. The essay does not solve the general problem of legitimate agenda-setting. It limits its diagnostic to known concerns that bear on public reasons or foreseeable effects. If an agency says its aim is equal cash payments, an access objection is relevant only if access appears in its justification or is a foreseeable consequence that the agency has reason to consider. If access is deliberately excluded from the aim, the agency may still defend that scope, but it cannot also cite the absence of access complaints as evidence that the reform improved deliberation about access.

This is not a demand for endless reconsideration. It is a demand for accuracy about what the available evidence supports. Officials may proceed under time limits, reject objections with reasons, and use a bounded review scope. The count then supports claims only within that scope. A process that refuses all challenges to its own scope may still make a policy decision, but its internal record is weak evidence that affected participants accepted the excluded issue.

### 5.5 “A reform may be justified without a fully comparable measure”

Yes. The thesis concerns evidence for deliberative improvement, not permission to reform. Reformers may have independent evidence of better outcomes, a strong moral argument, or urgent reasons to act despite incomplete data. Those grounds may justify proceeding. The essay asks only that such grounds not be confused with evidence that participants had better deliberative opportunities or that silence reflects agreement.

## 6. Limits of the proposal

The argument has no empirical estimate of how often a conceptual change makes complaint counts non-comparable. The benefit program is hypothetical and illustrates a logical possibility rather than an observed case. The account also supplies no threshold for how much a reporting route may change before old and new counts become unusable. That threshold will depend on the question, the procedure, and what independent information is available.

The literature comparison is bounded. The sources considered include work on purpose-sensitive conceptual revision, metalinguistic negotiation, hermeneutical marginalization, communicative exclusion, and uptake. Selected excerpts and sections, rather than every full text, were read; some relevant works remained accessible only as metadata, and Young’s opening excerpt lacked a verified page location. Nothing here establishes that the measurement-specific warning is original. The argument should be assessed as a limited application unless further literature comparison shows a distinct and worthwhile difference.

## 7. Conclusion

Conceptual reform may improve a practice while changing the language through which objections are heard. If a new category also changes what the complaint system records, fewer recorded objections do not by themselves show that participants have fewer reasons to object or that the deliberative process improved. Reformers may still rely on the count when they show that the route remains comparable, validate an alternative route, or add independent evidence. They may also proceed on grounds that support the policy without supporting a claim about deliberative success.

The distinction is small but useful: a reform can change a concept without defeating its users, yet it should not cite the silence produced by its new recording rules as straightforward confirmation of deliberative success. Whether this is a novel contribution remains open. Its defensible claim is narrower: when concepts and objection measures change together, the measure needs validation before it can support the same conclusion.
