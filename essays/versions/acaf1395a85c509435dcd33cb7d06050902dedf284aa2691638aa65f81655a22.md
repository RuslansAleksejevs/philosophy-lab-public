# Who May Say the Review Is Finished?

A transit agency plans to move a bus stop. The route team checks whether buses can turn safely. The scheduling team checks journey times. The finance team checks the cost. During the review, a driver reports that passengers from a nearby supported-housing building will have to cross a busy road to reach the new stop. The pedestrian route was missing from the map used to prepare the proposal. The report is specific, plausible and potentially serious. But none of the three teams is assigned to assess pedestrian access.

Everyone completes their allotted work. Everyone’s report is accurate. The agency’s system marks the review finished when the three teams mark their tasks complete, and a finished review permits implementation without another meeting. The last employee to complete a task knows about the driver’s report. She has no expertise in pedestrian safety and no authority to choose the site. She can, however, leave the review open, or complete her task with a qualification that prevents it from counting as an unrestricted clearance. She chooses an unqualified completion. The stop is moved.

Suppose all this is common knowledge among the participants. Nobody believes that pedestrian access has been assessed. They regard its absence from the allocated work as enough to proceed. The problem therefore cannot be reduced to a false statement or a misunderstanding. What has happened is that a serious objection has lost its ability to require a response. It has disappeared from the decision while remaining in the record.

I will defend a limited duty governing that disappearance. When someone knowingly exercises control over whether an organizational review counts as finished, that person must not let a serious, unresolved objection lose its practical standing merely because no role has been assigned to address it. She must secure an appropriate disposition, establish a real transfer of the unfinished responsibility, or preserve the qualification in the authorization to proceed. This does not make her the pedestrian-safety expert, the author of the agency’s entire decision, or the guarantor of a good outcome. It makes her responsible for what she does to the objection.

This duty offers one way to locate individual responsibility within divided work. Dennis Thompson presents the problem of many hands as the difficulty of identifying individual responsibility when many officials contribute to public decisions. His response preserves a place for personal responsibility while attending to causation, ignorance and compulsion. Helen Nissenbaum similarly warns that complicated causal and decision structures can obscure accountability without eliminating it. These are good reasons to look carefully for the acts that disappear within apparently innocent role descriptions. Closing a review is one such act. [Thompson, p. 11](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/38306/excerpt/9780521838306_excerpt.pdf); [Nissenbaum, “Many Hands”](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/accountability.pdf).

To see what is distinctive about it, separate two questions. Has my assigned work ended? May others now rely on the process as having dealt with the concerns that needed attention? A person can correctly answer yes to the first while lacking grounds to answer yes to the second. Organizations often make a local completion do both jobs. A status change records the end of one person’s work and helps authorize the next stage. Its moral significance depends on both functions.

Calling an objection practically active means that it retains some determinate bearing on what the organization may do next: it requires assessment, a stated reason for proceeding without assessment, or an acknowledged qualification on the decision. An active objection need not win. It need not command everyone’s attention at every meeting. It must have somewhere to go other than the archive, and the organization must not treat the relevant work as unconditionally complete while leaving it there.

An objection can consequently lose practical standing without anyone deleting it. A report may be searchable forever while the system treats every relevant decision as already settled. Conversely, an objection can retain standing in an ordinary conversation, with no elaborate database, if the people who must act know who will address it and what remains conditional in the meantime. The issue is the distribution of permissions and obligations, not the durability of a document.

Nor does every expression of concern acquire this standing. An employee’s unexplained feeling that a timetable is unlucky does not require the same treatment as the driver’s report. The principle concerns an identifiable objection which, given the evidence reasonably available, presents a credible prospect of a serious wrong or harm and bears on the proposed action. It must be strong enough that a reasonable decision maker would need either to address it or to give a reason for proceeding without settling it. Repetitions of an objection already adequately answered do not automatically reopen a review. A new label for the same worry does not make it new evidence.

This threshold is a moral standard, not simply an organization’s admission rule. Otherwise an organization could evade the duty by declaring all inconvenient concerns inadmissible. Yet applying the threshold does not require the employee to resolve the disputed issue. One can recognize that an omitted pedestrian route needs assessment without knowing whether the route is safe. Recognition of the need for an answer can demand less knowledge than possession of the answer. Where even that preliminary recognition is reasonably beyond a person’s competence, this particular duty does not arise for them in the same way.

Why should control over closure generate a duty beyond the employee’s specialist assignment? Start with a simpler case. A single planner receives the driver’s report. She cannot justify treating pedestrian access as settled by making a list of other questions, answering those questions, and announcing that she has answered everything on her list. The omission might justify seeking assistance or revising her plan. It does not turn an unanswered reason into an answered one.

Now divide her work among three competent people. There are substantial advantages: better expertise, manageable tasks and reliable coordination. But division of the work does not, by itself, change the force of the driver’s objection. Nothing about the passengers’ exposure, the evidence or the proposed action has changed. A distribution of assignments is not an answer to a question about the action’s justification.

The organization may nevertheless possess a defensible policy for proceeding despite some unanswered questions. Delay can also harm people, and inquiry has costs. That possibility does not defeat the argument. It identifies another answer that someone must give: why acting without this assessment is warranted. The flaw in the transit case is precisely that there is no such answer. An omission from the assignment list is being made to do the work of a reason.

The person who controls closure stands at the point where this substitution takes effect. Before she acts, the review remains an unfinished basis for proceeding. After she acts, its completion can be invoked as permission to proceed. She does not merely fail to discover a danger elsewhere in the organization. She knowingly changes the status of the process containing the disclosed objection. Her limited technical remit explains why she cannot settle the question. It does not explain why she may remove the need for anyone to settle or otherwise address it.

The resulting duty is conditional and proportionate. Where an agent knows that her act will enable reliance on completed review, knows of a serious objection whose required treatment remains outstanding, and can reasonably preserve its status, she has a presumptive obligation to do so. If that preservation would impose disproportionate burdens or conflict with a more urgent obligation, those reasons enter the assessment. What cannot do the justificatory work on its own is the claim that the issue belongs to no assigned role.

Notice the limited object of the duty. The employee must preserve the difference between completed work and an uncompleted requirement. She need not guarantee that a superior will respect it. If she clearly communicates that her specialist checks are finished but the access concern remains unassessed, and the manager knowingly authorizes implementation anyway, she has not made the same closure as in the original case. The manager’s action now needs its own assessment. The employee might have further duties to object or refuse, but they are not established merely by the duty defended here.

This is why a transfer must be distinguished from a transmission. Forwarding a report changes who possesses the information. Transferring unfinished responsibility changes who must respond to it. An email to an unattended mailbox accomplishes only the first. So does copying a senior official who reads it as background information while assuming that a specialist is handling it.

A reasonable transfer requires grounds for believing that the receiving person or unit recognizes the concern as something it must address, has an appropriate remit and capacity, and will carry the outstanding qualification into the decision. The recipient may already have that responsibility through an established role; a fresh promise is not always needed. But the sender must have some basis for reliance beyond the fact that the message was delivered. A name in the “assigned to” field is not enough if the person cannot assess the matter, arrange an assessment or take responsibility for a justified decision under uncertainty.

The final condition is easy to overlook. Even an excellent recipient does not complete a transfer if the organization acts before the recipient can do anything and treats the question as settled in the meantime. Imagine that the agency’s safety officer accepts the report on Monday but will assess it on Friday, while the stop moves on Tuesday because all the original teams have finished. Responsibility for the assessment has a recipient, but the authorization has dropped its qualification. The handoff must connect the outstanding work to the action it is supposed to inform. It must establish whether implementation waits, proceeds conditionally, or proceeds through an explicit decision that the remaining uncertainty is acceptable.

This connection need not be expressed through formal language. “My route checks are finished; the pedestrian-access issue is with Mara, and launch still requires her response” can be enough in a setting where those words are understood and effective. In another setting, the employee will need to use a formal exception process. The same sentence entered in a field nobody can see may do nothing. What discharges the duty depends on the actual practice of reliance, rather than the availability of a phrase that sounds responsible.

A harder version of the case removes the final gatekeeper. Suppose a review has ten components. Once any eight are completed, the system allows implementation. Several employees know of the unassessed access report. No one can prevent closure alone, because the other nine might supply enough completions. Does the duty disappear?

It does not disappear merely because an individual lacks a veto. Someone can be responsible for lending her certification to a clearance without being able to stop the clearance. If she can qualify her certification so that it cannot properly be invoked as disposing of the access concern, she has an act available which directly concerns her own contribution. That others may proceed without it does not give her a reason to provide an unqualified clearance herself. This claim does not require assigning her responsibility for the whole collective outcome. The object of assessment remains her participation in withdrawing a requirement for further treatment.

But we should not slide from this point into a universal duty to police every use of one’s work. Suppose a technician records only a measurement, and everyone correctly understands that this entry carries no authority to close any review. The entry is just evidence. A manager later misuses the measurement as comprehensive clearance. The technician does not thereby acquire the closure duty described here. Knowledge of imminent misuse may create a duty to warn, but that is a different basis for responsibility. The distinction turns on what the person’s act actually authorizes within a known practice, not on the presence of a signature or a green box.

Some systems make even that distinction unavailable to their workers. They convert routine data entry into authorization through rules the workers could not reasonably know. In those cases the organizational design may be culpable while the workers are not. A theory that makes the nearest person with a keyboard responsible for the whole process would reproduce the unfairness it purports to correct. Responsibility for designing and maintaining a defective system remains with those whose knowledge and powers support it. The closure duty adds a local ground of assessment where its conditions are met; it does not redistribute all institutional fault downwards.

The most immediate objection is that this is just negligence in a special setting. Of course an employee should not ignore a serious warning. Why introduce another duty?

There is no need to treat the duty as an entirely new species of moral obligation. It can specify what care requires. But “do not be negligent” does not tell us whether this employee’s restricted remit excuses her, what exactly she should do, or what she has done wrong if the new stop turns out to be safe. The closure principle answers those questions together. Her duty concerns a status change within her control. Qualification or an adequate transfer can discharge it without a technical verdict. And the wrong can occur before, or without, the feared harm.

Consider two otherwise identical agencies. Both receive the report and do the same specialist checks. In one, an appropriate decision maker addresses the report and reasonably concludes that the existing crossing is adequate. In the other, nobody addresses it, but it happens that the crossing is adequate. Both stops are safe. The second agency has still substituted an administrative omission for the treatment owed to a credible concern about the people affected by its decision. It has imposed the decision through a procedure whose purported completion concealed an unfinished justificatory task. Luck about the crossing does not supply the missing treatment.

This does not imply a general right to whatever procedure one prefers, irrespective of outcomes. The claim rests on a more particular interest. People exposed to a serious organizational risk have reason to demand that a specific, credible objection be assessed or responsibly set aside, rather than eliminated solely by the way others divided their jobs. That demand would be the same if the employee worked alone. Dividing the task cannot make it vanish. When the employee knowingly helps make it vanish, she has a connection to its frustration that an arbitrary bystander lacks.

A related objection makes the duty one of avoiding misleading communication. Often it will be. But recall the version where every participant knows the concern remains unanswered. Nobody falsely believes it was investigated. All simply accept that the prescribed jobs are finished, and therefore nobody is required to do more. An unqualified completion then helps terminate an obligation within the agency’s practice without creating a false belief. Its defect is practical: it withdraws the requirement for treatment on an insufficient ground. Improving the accuracy of everyone’s beliefs would not fix it. Their beliefs are already accurate.

This account must also respect the fact that organizational participants experience their agency differently. Samuel Mortimer argues that a participant’s self-narrative can shape which collective actions are attributable to them, and that seeing oneself as passive can obscure opportunities to intervene. He nevertheless allows that someone whose contribution to collective wrongdoing was unintended may be culpable for negligence in enabling it. The present argument does not require rejecting these qualifications. It identifies a narrower act the employee knowingly performs: allowing her completion to extinguish an outstanding requirement. She may regard the agency’s choice as beyond her agency while understanding that she is closing this review. Where she cannot reasonably understand even that function of her act, the case for blaming her weakens accordingly. [Mortimer, §§3.2–4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/papa.70002).

The account is likewise compatible with Herlinde Pauer-Studer’s argument that a professional’s claim to be disengaged from an institution can conflict with the constitutive standards of the practice in which they participate. My claim, however, does not depend on demonstrating that the employee’s entire professional identity is incoherent. A person may work in a generally decent institution, accurately describe her modest role, and still perform one wrongful closure. The relevant inconsistency is between invoking completed review as a basis for action and knowingly leaving a qualifying objection untreated solely because it lacks an assigned assessor. [Pauer-Studer, §2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/japp.12320).

Deadlines present a more practical challenge. If keeping an objection active prevents a process from finishing, can a single persistent objector hold everyone hostage? A passenger may insist that no stop is acceptable until the agency eliminates every conceivable risk. An employee may raise a vague worry just before a deadline. If the response must always be further inquiry, the cost of the principle could be extreme.

The principle requires responsible treatment, which can include refusing a demand for further inquiry. An appropriate decision maker may explain that the objection lacks adequate support, has already been addressed, falls below a justified threshold for investigation, or is outweighed by the harms of delay. Those are contestable answers, and some will be wrong. Yet they address the objection in a way that an unassigned task does not. The duty does not make an objector’s satisfaction a condition of closure. It makes the absence of any responsible treatment a defeater of unqualified closure, unless stronger reasons justify that very omission.

Nor can an organization satisfy the duty by writing “all risks accepted” at the bottom of every form. A response must connect the relevant concern to the reasons for acting. If urgent repairs make the old stop unusable tomorrow, a manager might reasonably authorize a temporary move while arranging assistance for passengers and an immediate access assessment. What matters is not whether the file contains a ritual acceptance of responsibility, but whether the organization has made an identifiable decision about the actual uncertainty. Whether that decision is morally permissible remains a further question.

Even an inadequate response can matter for locating responsibility. Suppose a supervisor says, “Yes, I understand the access issue; I have decided that saving this small sum matters more.” If the employee correctly records this decision, she has not made the objection disappear through an assignment gap. The supervisor has given a morally objectionable answer. Preserving the distinction does not absolve the employee of every further duty. It allows us to criticize the decision for the right reason and identify the person who has actually taken it. A rule about preserving answerability is not a substitute for substantive moral judgment.

There is a nearby proposal which the argument should not claim to originate. In a recent working paper on research organizations, Peter Kahl distinguishes legitimate procedural closure from an illegitimate claim to epistemic finality. He argues that closing review need not put a judgment beyond later challenge. My proposal concerns an earlier and narrower failure. The transit agency might fully allow later appeals while still letting the already disclosed access objection lose its standing before implementation. Nobody need claim that any judgment is final; there may be no judgment on the objection at all. Reopenability is valuable, but the ability to raise a concern again does not automatically justify dropping its first, still-unanswered presentation. [Kahl, “Closure without finality”](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20439758).

Maximilian Kiener offers another useful distinction. He defends taking answerability—an obligation to answer and explain—without thereby taking liability to blame or punishment, and considers making answerability a condition of occupying certain roles. The duty developed here also avoids manufacturing a person who deserves punishment merely because someone must answer. But its basis is not a free-standing declaration that one accepts responsibility for everything the organization does. It is the exercise of a particular power over whether an outstanding requirement survives. That act supplies a specific ground for the obligation which a general desire to find someone answerable would lack. [Kiener, §§3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-022-10313-9).

These distinctions matter when organizations investigate themselves. Finding the last employee to click “complete” is not enough. An inquiry must establish what her act did, what she could reasonably know, what qualification or refusal was available, and what burdens those alternatives carried. Threats to employment, an inaccessible supervisor, or a system that punishes exception reporting may mitigate individual blame while increasing the responsibility of those who created or maintained those conditions. An employee’s failure to perform a duty does not settle how harshly others may judge her, still less what penalty would be justified.

The practical implication is nevertheless demanding. Organizations should make it possible to finish a specialist task without falsely finishing the unsettled questions around it. They should make transfers visible as transfers of obligations, with a bearing on authorization, rather than as movements of information. And when they choose to act with an issue unresolved, they should make the choice attributable to a decision rather than to the exhaustion of a checklist. These arrangements are not sufficient for justice or safety. They remove one way that otherwise recognizable duties become unlocatable.

The transit employee is right about her limits. She cannot determine every relevant fact, control the whole agency, or take over a specialist judgment by force of conscience. Those limits support a division of work. They do not support treating the boundaries between jobs as reasons why an objection no longer matters. The claim “I have finished what I was assigned” can be completely true. Whether one may use it to help say “the review is finished” is a further moral question.
