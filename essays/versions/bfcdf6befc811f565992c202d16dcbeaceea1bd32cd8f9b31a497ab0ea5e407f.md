# What Asking Does: Requests, Answerability, and the Deliberative Agenda

*Final manuscript (final-004), condensed from final-003/002/001; revised from `draft-001.md` in the light of `critique.md`. Earlier versions are preserved unchanged.*

## 1. The puzzle

A colleague stops you in the corridor: "Could you look at my draft this weekend?"

The request is cheap to refuse. "Sorry, I'm swamped" will do; so, often, will "Sorry, I'd rather not." You need not show that your weekend outweighs your colleague's need.

The request is also expensive to ignore. If you look through your colleague as though nothing had been said and walk on, you have wronged them — and wronged them even if, had you considered the request, you would have been entitled to refuse. The failure is not that you gave a consideration too little weight. It is that you did not take the matter up.

Whatever a request contributes is so slight that almost anything defeats it; yet it is so insistent that it cannot be passed over in silence. Considerations that almost anything defeats are normally the very ones we are permitted to ignore. Requests are not. My thesis:

**(T1)** Requesting adds no weight to the first-order balance bearing on the requested act. Whatever favours your reading the draft — need, friendship, the good of the profession — would favour it as strongly had the request never been made.

**(T2)** Requesting is nonetheless a normative power operating on a second dimension: it puts an option on the addressee's *deliberative agenda* and makes her *answerable* to the requester for what becomes of it.

**(T3)** The requirement so created is neither a first-order reason nor quite a Razian second-order reason. Raz defined second-order reasons as reasons to act, or refrain from acting, *for* a reason. A requirement to put a question on one's agenda is neither; it bears on deliberative *uptake*.

## 2. The debate, and what it assumes

The literature asks whether asking *creates* a reason or merely *reveals* one. David Enoch says requests give reasons "robustly" — the requester intends to give a reason, communicates that intention, and intends the addressee's reason to depend on recognising it — thereby making true the antecedent of a background conditional. James Lewis adds that this works only if the addressee places discretionary value in the requester. Peter Schaber grounds the power in the value of our all having it. Against them, Daniel Weltman's epistemic account holds that requests create nothing and give reasons only by conveying information: "requests aren't normative. They are a red herring, normatively speaking."[^1] Micha Gläser, whom I could not obtain, is reported by both Weltman and Schaber to deny that requests create additional reasons while holding that they place the requestee under an obligation.[^2]

All of these answer one question: what does a request contribute to the *first-order balance*? A reason, nothing, or an obligation.

The alternative surfaces once and is dismissed. Weltman concedes that a request "may technically have normative power insofar as you have more reason to attend to a reason brought to your attention, because (for instance) it would be rude to ignore someone's having brought it to your attention. But this normative power has nothing to do with the request being a request rather than any other action."[^3] I think the attention-directing effect is not a by-product with a normative shadow but the whole of the request's distinctive work — and that it has everything to do with the request being a request.

Thomas Reid saw the shape of the omission in the philosophy of mind. Some operations, he wrote, "necessarily suppose an intercourse with some other intelligent being": asking a favour, giving a command, bearing testimony, promising. "To ask a question is as simple an operation as to judge or to reason; yet it is neither judgment nor reasoning, nor simple apprehension, nor is it any composition of these." In the usual divisions of the mind's operations, "the social have been omitted, and thereby thrown behind the curtain."[^4] In the standard divisions of practical reasons — weights, defeaters, cancellers — the same has happened, because the only place the theory has for the social operations is the balance, and the balance is not where they live.

## 3. The data

**(D1) Cheap defeat.** A request may be refused for reasons that do not match its importance, and with no showing that the competing reason is weightier; as Lance and Kukla put it, "it is essential to the notion of a request that this reason is not an obligation."[^5] **(D2) Costly silence.** Requests must be taken up, and ignoring one is a failure *even where refusal would have been permissible*: one can defy a request without disobeying it. **(D3)** Repetition does not accumulate — asked three times, my reason does not triple; it degrades into nagging. **(D4)** The residue of refusal is an account, not a repair: nothing was taken, so nothing is owed by way of restitution. **(D5)** A drowning man who cannot call out gives you no weaker reason to save him than one who shouts. Generalising: delete a request while holding the facts and the addressee's knowledge fixed, and the wrong of not acting seems undiminished.

## 4. Why weights are not enough

Call a view a **Weight Model** if it holds that a request contributes a first-order pro tanto reason of weight *w*. My earlier draft claimed to refute such views. It does not; none of these philosophers says that contributing a reason is *all* a request does, and a hybrid is available off the shelf. What follows shows only that weights *alone* cannot deliver D2 — which is enough, since it establishes a second dimension.

**Substantial weight** loses D1: if the request weighs as much as a modest inconvenience, I cannot rightly refuse merely to see a film.

**Epsilon weight** loses D2. Say a consideration is *idle* in a context if the balance there is the same with or without it, and grant *Permitted Inattention*: one is not criticisable for failing to attend to what one reasonably takes to be idle. This is not a concession to frailty but a condition of deliberation; every choice sits in a field of vanishing considerations, and one who attended to all of them would never act. An epsilon is idle wherever a weightier consideration is present and the rest do not exactly balance — so, on a hard deadline, I could leave the message unopened, knowing in advance that it cannot change what I ought to do. That is exactly what one may not do. (Epsilons break ties, which is why the model gets D1 right; but ties are rare and not identifiable in advance. Nor does letting *w* vary with stakes help: the dilemma is pointwise.)

**The deontic retreat** is the better reply, and the one the literature makes. Lewis and Schaber explain discretion not by low weight but by a deontic category: "A reason is non-obligatory if it would not be blameworthy for one not to act on it, and obligatory if it would be blameworthy not to act on it."[^6] A reason can be weighty and non-obligatory. So the inference from cheap refusal to tiny weight never starts, and my earlier draft's talk of a "modest" constant weight was an interpolation.

But see what the retreat concedes. Requests create reasons such that it is *never blameworthy not to act on them*; D2 says it *is* blameworthy not to attend to them. On any weight-based account, the point of attending to a reason is to get the action right: inattention is criticisable because it risks acting wrongly. If failing to act on the request is never blameworthy, inattention cannot be criticisable for risking a wrong act — there is no wrong act to risk. The blame must have another ground. Locating it is my whole project, and the retreat concedes there is one. Nor is "non-obligatory reason" a resting place: it is not a weight but a status defined by where blame falls, so whoever invokes it has already left the scale.

**What we defend against.** Consider what we protect: do-not-disturb settings, unsubscribe links, gatekeepers, office hours, and conventions of indirection whose function is to let a request be withdrawn before it lands. We say "don't ask me that" precisely to stop an agenda item being created. We do not build institutions against small increments to our reasons. We do build them against having our attention conscripted and our silence made culpable. This is stronger evidence than the dilemma, because it appeals to the shape of our practices rather than to intuitions about cases.

## 5. The Agenda Thesis

> In requesting that S φ, A brings it about that:
> **(i) Uptake** — S must take up the question whether to φ, under the description "what A has asked of me";
> **(ii) Answerability** — if S does not φ, she must fail to φ *for* a reason she can offer A as the reason on which she acted;
> **(iii) Account** — S must actually render that account where A would otherwise be left without one.

**A burden of production, not of persuasion.** Before the request the question was not S's to answer; after it she must put something forward — but only a reason, not a *weightier* one. That is the shape of D1 and D2 together: easy to discharge, impossible to ignore, because discharging and ignoring are different relations to a burden. This also answers the worry that bigotry would discharge it. A burden of production is met by producing something of the right *type*; it does not certify what is produced. Discharging it makes S non-culpable *for ignoring A*; whether she is culpable *for refusing* is settled by the first-order balance the request never touched.

**Clause (iii) is derived, not bolted on.** My earlier draft made answerability merely counterfactual — S need only have a reason she *could* avow. That fails: S may silently consider, silently decline on deadline, and walk on, satisfying the condition while committing exactly the wrong at issue. But the response duty is no extra postulate. Answerability is a *relation*: a standing liability to be called to account, discharged as circumstances allow. Where A cannot know whether S took the question up except by being told — the ordinary case — it can be discharged only by telling. And the account correctly predicts the exceptions: no answer is owed where compliance is manifest, where A said "no need to reply," or where A is unreachable.

**Sincerity, not avowability.** The reason offered must be the reason acted on. So stated, this is a sincerity condition. It rules out pretexts; it does not rule out randomising ("I had two equally good uses of Saturday and picked by lot" is adequate, since the lottery was adopted for a reason). What it rules out is drift — never engaging, then finding afterwards that some good reason was available.

**Standing is a permission, not a success condition.** The ill-standing request lands; that is why it is an imposition. If nothing were created there would be nothing to complain of, and "you have no business asking" — a challenge to standing, not a weighing — would be idle rather than apt.

**Structure.** Uptake is defeasible, but not by pointlessness (knowing I shall refuse is no licence) or by small costs; it yields to incapacity and to more urgent conscriptions of attention. When defeated, answerability is *deferred*, not extinguished: out of surgery I must still say "Sorry — I was in theatre." Outweighed reasons leave no such residue. Where many requests compete, weight orders the *queue* but not its *membership*: on a pure weight model the ninth request is swamped and vanishes; on mine it waits. And agenda requirements do not compete with first-order reasons at all — exclusionary reasons remove inputs from the balance, whereas requests leave the inputs alone and require that a question be run through deliberation and its output owned. The relation is composition, not addition. D3 follows: burdens do not stack, and one cannot become more answerable by being asked twice — though asking again after circumstances change does renew the claim, since it is no longer the same question.

## 6. Three hard cases for T1

**Near-indifference.** Two strangers need a lift; one asks, one visibly needs it and says nothing. If I take the silent one I owe the asker an answer; if I take the asker I owe the other nothing. That is an asymmetry in *transaction cost*, not in the merits — and easy is easily mistaken for right. Were weight added, driving the needier person would require justification on the merits: I would owe the asker not just an answer but a *defence*. I do not think I do.

**Withdrawal.** "Actually, please don't" is not cancellation but a second request with opposite content, which of course changes things. Genuine retraction — "forget I asked" — releases me from answerability and nothing else; reading the draft anyway is at worst officious.

**"Please don't tell anyone."** Telling afterwards is betrayal; telling on mere surmise is thoughtlessness. My earlier draft borrowed the reply that these are consent. Better: when I say nothing and let her go on, she relies on me, and *reasonably*, because I did not demur. My silence functions as acceptance, and the gravity comes from the undertaking. Test it: say "I'm not promising anything — I'm a terrible gossip," and later telling is indiscretion, not betrayal. So T1 says only that the *act of asking* adds no weight; accepted requests create commitments, which is why refusal is cheap — acceptance is what obligates. And the Agenda Thesis explains why tacit acceptance is possible at all: silence counts as acceptance only where silence is not neutral, and it is the request that makes saying nothing a move.

This does not settle T1, which is a universal negative defeated by one clear counterexample. It shows that the hardest cases I could build come out right, the apparent weight belonging each time to something else: information, transaction cost, a second address, or an undertaking.

## 7. Address without a claim

Comparing being asked with overhearing does not isolate address: overhearing varies common knowledge, and higher-order information is still information. A cleaner instrument is Raz's observation that "[i]t is possible ... to tell a person that, while I would like him to do something for me, I am not asking and am not going to ask him to do it."[^7]

> **Disclaimed-Request Test.** (a) "Could you read my draft this weekend?" (b) "I'd love you to read my draft — your comments are always so helpful — but I'm not asking, and I'm not going to."

Full information, full common knowledge, a genuine addressee, no eavesdropping. In both cases S must say *something*, since conversational uptake is owed to any address — which is why politeness cannot be the difference. The difference is that in (a) S must *close the question*; leaving it permanently open is itself a failure. In (b) she need not open it: "that's kind of you to say" is complete, and she may silently decide never to read it without wronging A at all.

Weltman has a reply ready. He grants that "when I request something, and someone acts on my request, I am to some extent responsible for this, whereas when someone acts on a wish, I am not," calls it "a big difference," then brackets it: "It is also a normatively important difference, but not in the sense of the normativity we are interested in when we ask why requests are normative. Similarly, if I accidentally reveal what cards I am holding in a game of bridge, I am less culpable than if I intentionally do so, but in each case the cards I reveal give my opponents identical reasons to play certain cards in order to win."[^8]

Three replies. On his own question he is right, and T1 agrees. But the bracket defines the topic rather than reporting a finding: "the normativity we are interested in" is stipulated as the addressee's reasons to φ, which is the axis in dispute. And the bridge analogy misdescribes the structure: there the culpability attaches to the *revealer*, and nothing is required of his opponents. In the request case the liability falls on the *addressee* — it is S who must now do something she need not otherwise have done. One may bracket the speaker's responsibility and still owe an account of a requirement falling on the hearer.

## 8. Where this belongs

My earlier draft said Raz's genus has an empty, unnamed positive species. Raz's definition, quoted by Piller, is: "A second-order reason is any reason to act or to refrain from acting for a reason. An exclusionary reason is a second-order reason to refrain from acting for some reason."[^9] The positive species is neither nameless nor empty: it is standardly called a *positive second-order reason*, attacked by Daniel Whiting and recently defended and classified by Kenta Sekine.[^10]

What survives is better. Clause (ii) *is* a positive second-order reason: it constrains the reasons for which S may act. Clause (i) is *not*: considering is not acting for a reason, and a requirement to put a question on the agenda bears on deliberation, on what gets weighed. Raz's disjunctive definition does not reach it in either direction. So I drop the label "inclusionary reason" I proposed, and the symmetry it promised. The better claim is less tidy: requests reveal a category of *agenda reasons*, bearing on deliberative uptake, that the standard taxonomy lacks.

The gap shows inside Raz's own motivating case: Piller reports that the tired investor's refusal "is based on a reason not to consider the merits of the offer" — a reason about uptake, then classified as a reason not to act *for* the excluded considerations. And requests are where the received picture finds nothing to say. Michael Moore notes that threats and requests are illuminated by the *absence* of exclusionary reasons, since "unlike a promise, say, or an order issued by one in authority, a mere request does not preclude one from weighing the pros and cons of doing the action requested."[^11] Exactly half the story: a request excludes nothing, and also does something second-order, on an axis the framework lacks.

Two acknowledgements. Whiting argues that second-order reasons are incoherent; my account is not hostage to this, since clause (i) is not a reason to act for a reason and survives untouched. And "answerability" is a term of art in the theory of responsibility; I use it narrowly, for a standing liability to be called to account by a particular person on a particular question. The nearest relative of my positive thesis is Stephen Darwall's work on second-personal address and accountability, which I could not obtain and record as an unpaid debt.

Finally Gläser. If the reports are right, we agree on the negative claim and differ on the positive one — and the difference is testable against D1, since an obligation to φ predicts blameworthy non-compliance, which the category of non-obligatory reasons denies. What is obligatory, on my account, is not φ-ing but taking up the question and standing behind the answer.

## 9. Conclusion

Agenda reasons are not peculiar to requests. A question creates an obligation to answer that is cheap to discharge and, where the asker has standing, costly to ignore. Rights to be heard are powers to put a claim on an institution's agenda rather than powers to win, which is why a hearing one was always going to lose is nonetheless owed. And much protest aims not to shift a balance of reasons — those are usually known — but to force a question onto an agenda; the complaint of the unheard is not "you weighed us too lightly" but "you did not even consider us."

Asking adds nothing to the scales. It puts a question on your agenda and makes you answerable for what becomes of it. That is not a deflationary result but a redirection, since the power to conscript another's attention and make their silence culpable is among the most consequential we have over one another. The cost is a symmetry I would have liked to claim and cannot: what a request creates is not the mirror image of a Razian exclusionary reason but something the taxonomy has no slot for. Reid thought the social operations of mind had been thrown behind the curtain. So, I think, have the social operations of practical reason.
