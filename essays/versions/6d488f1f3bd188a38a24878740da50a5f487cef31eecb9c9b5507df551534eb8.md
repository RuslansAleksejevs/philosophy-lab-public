# Which Choice Was Predicted? Accuracy and the Epistemic Target of Public Forecasts

## Abstract

A public forecast can be accurate because it tracks an agent's choice, because the agent responds to it, or because both forecast and choice share a cause. The same record of perfect accuracy can fit all three stories. I argue for a modest target-indexing rule: before treating accuracy as evidence of predictive knowledge, specify whether the forecast concerns the choice under the announced policy or the choice that would occur without disclosure. An exhaustive search of 65,536 small deterministic models shows that perfect balanced accuracy under disclosure leaves the no-disclosure choice and the causal role of the message undetermined. This does not show that a self-fulfilling forecast cannot be knowledge. It blocks only an inference from success under one information regime to a different proposition about another.

## 1. The ambiguity of a perfect record

Suppose I publicly tell a person, before she chooses, “You will pick the blue card.” She picks blue. Repeat this pattern many times, with forecasts of both colors, and suppose every forecast is right. What has the record established?

One possibility is that I detect a prior preference. The forecast and the choice agree because each reflects a stable feature of the person. Another is that the person treats my forecast as an instruction or invitation and follows it. A third is that the person responds in a more complicated way: she may follow predictions of one kind, resist others, or act differently depending on how credible they seem. These explanations can yield the same visible forecast–choice pairs while giving different answers to the question, “What would she have chosen if she had not seen the forecast?”

This difference matters to an epistemic judgment. A person may know what another person will do after receiving a particular message while lacking knowledge of what that person would do without it. Conversely, the fact that a message helps cause the predicted action does not automatically show that the forecaster lacked knowledge of the action under the actual conditions. Accuracy alone does not settle either point.

My claim is deliberately narrow. When an agent can respond to a forecast, observed accuracy concerns an outcome under some disclosure arrangement. Unless additional assumptions or evidence connect that arrangement to a no-disclosure case, accuracy does not by itself warrant a claim about the latter. A forecast's causal contribution is a separate question: it can affect the outcome and still be an informed prediction of that outcome. This is not a new general theory of knowledge, a proof that self-fulfilling forecasts are epistemically defective, or a claim that accuracy is useless. It is a rule for keeping distinct targets distinct.

## 2. Two targets and three questions

Let F be a binary forecast shown to an agent before a binary choice. Let Y-exposed denote the agent's choice under a specified policy that shows this forecast. Let Y-withheld denote the choice under a specified intervention that withholds it. These are potential outcomes: for a given person, only one is ordinarily observed. The forecast can be compared with either outcome, but the comparisons answer different questions.

First, did the forecast correctly identify the choice under the disclosure policy? In a population, one measure is the proportion for which F equals Y-exposed. Second, would the same forecast have matched the choice if it had not been disclosed? That asks whether F would equal Y-withheld. Third, did changing the message itself change the choice? This asks about causal influence. The answer to one question does not answer the other two.

The distinction is not merely between a “real prediction” and an “intervention.” A prediction can be both informative and causally active. A forecaster might know that a patient will accept treatment if told a certain probability, or that a market will move in response to a published forecast. Such a prediction can be warranted by a reliable model of the response. The epistemic target is then the outcome under the described conditions. On the other hand, a model advertised as estimating what a person would do independently of its use cannot claim support for that counterfactual merely by showing accuracy after deployment.

For clarity, I use “no-disclosure” for one specified intervention: the target does not receive this forecast. It is not a claim about a world with no predictions, beliefs, institutions, prior information, or causes. In real settings, even defining the intervention requires care. The forecast may be available through rumors, intermediaries, or earlier decisions. A useful counterfactual must say exactly which communication and policy are withheld.

## 3. A finite counterexample search

I tested whether perfect disclosed accuracy can coexist with different no-disclosure choices and different causal pathways. The search enumerated every deterministic model in a small, explicit class. There are two independent fair latent features, H and V, with four equally likely combinations. Every Boolean function from those features to a forecast F is included: 16 possible forecast rules. The agent's response is any Boolean function of H, V, and the received message, where the message is either “none,” 0, or 1. There are 4,096 such response rules, giving 65,536 forecast–response models in all.

In each model the exposed choice is the response to the generated forecast, and the no-disclosure choice is the response to receiving no message. The search keeps the exact population distribution rather than drawing a finite sample. Among the models with the same balanced, perfectly accurate disclosed table—two cases of (forecast 0, choice 0), and two of (forecast 1, choice 1)—there are 1,536 compatible models. Across them, the joint forecast/no-disclosure-choice table takes nine forms. The forecast's accuracy against the no-disclosure choice ranges from zero to one, and the message can be causally inert or change the response in every latent state.

Two models make the result concrete. In the first, the forecast equals H and the agent always chooses H. The forecast and choice share a cause; the agent ignores the message. The forecast is perfectly accurate whether it is shown or withheld, and changing the message has no effect. In the second, the forecast still equals H, but the agent's no-message choice is V and she follows whichever message she receives. Under disclosure, accuracy is again perfect and the same balanced forecast–choice table appears. Without disclosure, however, H and V are independent, so the forecast matches the choice only half of the time. Changing the communicated value changes the choice in every state; disclosure changes the actual choice in half of them.

The observations available under disclosure are identical in these two cases. The causal model and the counterfactual differ. A third model checks the opposite response: the agent chooses the opposite of the forecast when she sees it. The forecast then has zero accuracy under disclosure and perfect accuracy when withheld. Thus the model class does not assume compliance.

This is an exact result about the stated finite class, not a statistical claim about how frequently such cases occur. It shows that even a perfect population record in one regime does not identify the other regime or the message's causal role. It does not show that the forecaster in the follower model could not know the exposed choice. If she knew the agent's response rule, she could have strong grounds for predicting it. The model isolates the limit of exposed accuracy as evidence about a different target.

## 4. What causal epistemology contributes

Alvin Goldman’s early causal theory of knowing is a useful starting point because it already treats future knowledge as more subtle than backward causation. In his example, one person’s present intention causes both a later action and another person’s belief about that action. The future action need not cause the earlier belief; a common cause can connect them. Goldman also requires more than a causal path: important links must be correctly reconstructed, and inferences must be warranted (Goldman 1967, pp. 365–366, 370). These points caution against the simple inference that any causal influence makes a forecast unknowledgeable.

Kenneth Stalkfleet presses a different difficulty for Goldman's theory. In his Gene example, an analyst predicts a stock-price rise, the prediction spreads, and traders' purchases bring about the rise. Stalkfleet asks whether Gene knew the price would rise, then considers a repair requiring the relevant belief to remain justified, true, and causally connected if the prophecy had never been made (Stalkfleet 2011, pp. 75–78). The repair usefully draws attention to counterfactual dependence, but it also makes assessment harder. More basically, a no-prophecy test must keep the proposition being tested fixed. Whether the stock will rise under the circulated report and whether it would rise with no report are different claims. Evidence for the first cannot simply be transferred to the second.

This qualification does not resolve Gene's case. If Gene's words purport to predict an independently rising price, and the only basis for success is that investors follow him, an intuition of epistemic defect remains. But the defect cannot be read off from accuracy alone. If he instead predicts the price under a known disclosure regime, and has adequate evidence about how investors respond, the self-referential route may support rather than undermine his belief. The question is not whether the report caused the rise, in isolation; it is what proposition Gene believed and whether his evidence supported it.

King and Mertens make the causal distinctions especially clear. They distinguish transformative self-fulfillment, where the outcome would not have occurred without employing the prediction, from operative self-fulfillment, where employment contributes to an outcome that would have occurred anyway. They also distinguish both from a self-securing prediction that is fulfilled without helping to produce the outcome (King and Mertens 2023, pp. 10–11). Their account shows why “caused by the forecast” and “would not otherwise have happened” are not interchangeable. It also explains how accurate outcomes can conceal process mistakes and impede accountability. My target distinction is an additional axis, not a replacement taxonomy: regardless of whether a case is transformative, operative, or self-securing, ask whether the claimed knowledge concerns the outcome under disclosure or the outcome under non-disclosure.

Kopec argues that reflexive influence can be probabilistic rather than a deterministic switch, and that observing the predicted outcome after dissemination may carry no evidence, negative evidence, or stronger positive evidence depending on the case (Kopec 2011, pp. 1250–1252). My finite search is deterministic and cannot settle that Bayesian question. Its role is more limited: to display a pair of possible mechanisms that make the same exposed record compatible with different counterfactuals.

The performative-prediction literature already evaluates a model under the distribution induced by acting on it (Perdomo et al. 2020). Kim and Perdomo further distinguish objectives that reward accurate forecasting from objectives that steer outcomes in preferred directions (2023). Khosrowi emphasizes that performativity arises in a concrete context of model use, and that model appraisal must consider actual and counterfactual forecasts (2023). These works block any suggestion that deployed accuracy is inherently suspect. A predictor can be useful precisely because it forecasts a response-sensitive environment. The present claim is narrower: the performance measure for that environment does not by itself establish performance for a different one.

Mertens and co-authors discuss a related problem in neuroprognosis: when a poor prognosis is followed by withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, the resulting outcome may hide a false positive and hinder learning from the data (Mertens et al. 2022). That is an important system-level epistemic concern. It is not identical to the proposition-level question here. A clinician might know something about the outcome under treatment withdrawal while the research program still lacks reliable feedback about what would have happened under continued treatment. Separating those questions makes the practical epistemic risk easier to state without claiming that every individual prediction is unknowable.

## 5. The strongest objection: is the point trivial?

The strongest objection is that the essay has only restated proposition-relativity. Of course a forecast about what happens after a message is shown does not prove what would happen if it were withheld. King and Mertens already discuss counterfactual dependence; performative-prediction theory already distinguishes deployed distributions. If that is all the argument establishes, its contribution is too slight.

The objection has force. The enumeration is a simple illustration of causal non-identification, and the literature search was targeted rather than exhaustive. I cannot establish that no earlier author has articulated this exact target-indexing rule. The claim should therefore be judged as a narrow diagnostic proposal, not as a discovery about self-fulfilling prophecy.

There is still a useful philosophical discipline here. Discussions often move among at least four assessments without saying so: whether the prediction is true under actual use; whether the predictor had good evidence; whether the forecast caused or helped cause the outcome; and whether the data teach us how the target would have acted without the forecast. A perfect record settles the first for the observed regime. It does not, on its own, settle the others. Treating them as separate does not add a new theory of knowledge, but it prevents the success of one claim from lending unearned support to another.

A second objection says that once the target actually chooses, the only proposition that matters is the actual choice; counterfactuals are irrelevant to knowledge. That is right for some questions. If a forecaster knows, with adequate warrant, that the person will choose blue after seeing this forecast, she may know that fact even if the forecast helps produce it. But many real claims are broader: that someone is intrinsically risky, likely to fail without intervention, or destined for an outcome independent of the policy built around the prediction. Those are not merely claims about a post-deployment result. If the evidence is only accuracy after deployment, the broader claim remains unsupported.

A third objection is that the forecast's public content can make an ordinary prediction self-defeating: an agent who dislikes being predicted may resist. The resistance model shows that possibility, but it does not defeat the rule. It strengthens the case for specifying the response policy. A forecast calibrated in a low-publicity setting may fail when publicized; a forecast trained on publicized cases may learn resistance. The right question is which conditions the evidence actually covers.

## 6. A practical epistemic ledger

When a forecast concerns an agent who can respond, an evaluator can ask four questions.

1. **What is the target?** State the action, time, population, and whether the forecast is disclosed, to whom, and under what policy.
2. **What does the accuracy record measure?** Separate the observed outcome after deployment from the no-disclosure outcome and from other interventions.
3. **What supports the forecast?** Is it supported by evidence about a prior intention, by a model of the target's response to disclosure, or by both? Accuracy alone does not disclose this causal structure.
4. **What further claim is being made?** If the conclusion concerns no-disclosure behavior, identify the intervention evidence, causal assumptions, or randomized comparison that bridges from deployed outcomes to that claim.

This ledger does not demand that every study randomize public disclosure. Ethical, legal, or practical constraints may forbid that experiment. It does demand that claims track the available evidence. When a counterfactual is not identified, the responsible conclusion may be uncertainty, not an assertion that the target would have acted the same way without the forecast.

## Conclusion

Perfect public accuracy can reflect tracking, compliance, or other response patterns. An exhaustive search of a small model class exhibits identical disclosed records with different no-disclosure choices and causal roles. The philosophical conclusion is modest: accuracy is indexed to the conditions under which the outcome is measured. It is evidence about the proposition supported by that regime; it cannot be silently carried over to a different proposition.

A forecast that helps bring about its own truth is not thereby knowledge, but neither is it thereby excluded from knowledge. What matters is whether the proposition was correctly specified and whether the forecaster's evidence supports it. The boundary between those questions is the point this essay asks readers to keep visible.
