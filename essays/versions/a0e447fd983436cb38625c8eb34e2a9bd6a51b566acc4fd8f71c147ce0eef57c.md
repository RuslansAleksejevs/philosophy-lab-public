# Why Innocence Does Not Close the Question

An institution may act fairly and still owe someone the means to find out whether it acted fairly. That claim is easy to accept when we suspect concealed wrongdoing. It becomes more difficult when the institution really did everything it was supposed to do. If its decision was correct, its explanation truthful, and its beneficiaries satisfied, what could be wrong with destroying the records that would support an independent examination?

I argue that an important class of such destruction is wrongful. The wrong consists in taking authority to close a question that the affected person retains standing to raise. An institution's successful discharge of a first-order duty does not, by itself, discharge its duty to remain answerable for that discharge. This is not a general obligation to prove innocence, nor a demand to preserve everything. It is a restriction on unilateral closure. It explains why evidence preservation and the presumption of compliance can properly coexist, and why adequate substitutes for records must meet two different requirements: they must support the relevant inquiry, and they must be accepted through an arrangement entitled to settle its terms.

The need for evidence in accountability is well established. The narrower task here is to explain why actual innocence is not a sufficient exception to that need, without turning accountability into endless inspection.

## 1. The compliant allocator

Imagine a town distributing a finite fund for essential home repairs. Residents are entitled to a fair assessment of their applications under publicly defensible criteria. A committee has discretion to assess need; every applicant cannot supervise its deliberations. A small body of records contains the applications, the criteria actually used, and the reasons for departures from an initial ranking. Those records would enable a competent reviewer to distinguish proper allocation from several relevant abuses, including favoritism.

The committee allocates the fund impeccably. It then destroys the only copies of the records, although secure retention for a reasonable period would have been easy and inexpensive. It offers a truthful summary of its decision and accurately explains that the originals no longer exist. It has made no separate promise to preserve them; assume no specific record-retention rule settles the question. Its members reason that there is nothing wrong for an inquiry to uncover.

Suppose all the applicants continue correctly to believe that the allocation was fair. Nobody loses money because of the destruction, nobody is deceived, and nobody subsequently brings an unsuccessful complaint. The committee is dissolved after completing this single allocation, so we need not rely on its becoming less careful next time. Has it nevertheless wronged the applicants?

The answer should be yes, provided they retain a reasonable opportunity to contest the treatment of their claims. The committee has changed their position from being able to obtain a potentially corrective assessment to being dependent on its own account. That change remains real when their present confidence is justified and no challenge is ever made. An unused capacity is a capacity, and its removal need not first produce an erroneous belief to matter.

The proviso about retained standing is essential. Consider a variation. Before the records are destroyed, an appropriately authorized reviewer examines them under a fair procedure, applicants have a reasonable opportunity to raise relevant issues, and the process concludes. The committee may then be entitled to dispose of the records, subject to whatever further obligations apply. Innocence is present in both cases. What differs is whether closure results from a settlement of the accountability relation or from the actor's own assessment that further scrutiny would be pointless.

This comparison identifies the subject of the essay. The duty at issue protects neither every piece of evidence nor permanent uncertainty about every decision. It protects the affected person's position in determining when an account is closed.

## 2. Why standing survives satisfaction

We must distinguish three things. A person can have a claim to fair treatment, standing to demand an account of that treatment, and adequate grounds to accuse the decision-maker of unfairness. The third requires something that the second need not require. If one could demand an account only after establishing a violation, and the account provided the only feasible route to establishing it, the entitlement would be unusable in precisely the cases for which it matters.

This does not mean that every unsupported accusation deserves a hearing. It means that requesting access to a fair means of checking one's treatment is not itself an accusation. We can continue to presume that a decision-maker complied while refusing to give it unilateral control over whether compliance remains assessable.

Why grant the second kind of standing at all? Not simply because a person is affected. A passer-by may dislike the appearance of my coat without having a claim to inspect how I chose it. Nor does every directed duty produce a duty to document its performance. If I undertake to water a friend's plant, the ordinary relationship may make an exhaustive account both unnecessary and intrusive.

The stronger case arises when someone exercises consequential discretion over another's claim, in circumstances that make direct supervision unreasonably difficult. The applicants have not merely benefited from a committee's generosity. The committee determines how claims owed consideration are to be treated. Its authority is conferred for that purpose. This gives it room to decide; it does not automatically give it exclusive authority to decide whether it decided within its remit.

A grant of the latter authority would be a further allocation of power. It would make the committee's interpretation of its obligations, as well as its assessment of its own conduct, effectively final. That arrangement can sometimes be justified: final adjudication is possible, and people can authorize others to settle questions. But the justification must concern that further power. It cannot consist simply in pointing out that the committee was authorized to allocate funds.

The underlying normative premise is that people whose claims are subjected to serious discretionary judgment are ordinarily entitled to some effective means of questioning whether that judgment respected their claims. They should not have to treat the decision-maker's self-assessment as authoritative merely because the decision-maker was needed to do the work. This is a defeasible premise about the acceptable distribution of authority, not a consequence of the dictionary meaning of “accountability.” Someone who thinks benevolent, unreviewable administration fully respects the applicants will reject it. But that view owes an explanation of why the power needed to assess another's claim also carries the power to make one's assessment unanswerable to them.

A procedural understanding of respect helps explain the premise. Waldron connects legal procedure to opportunities to present evidence, confront it, and receive a response from an impartial tribunal. The participant is treated as someone with a perspective on the application of a norm. The present argument extends that concern to the indispensable conditions of such participation: offering a hearing after disabling the means of inquiry can leave the relevant standing empty. It does not extend every feature of a court to every institution. [Waldron 2011, §5, pp. 14–15](https://philarchive.org/archive/WALTRO-67v1).

Standing of this kind is not extinguished by the mere fact that a first-order claim has been satisfied. Payment can discharge a debt, but it does not follow that the payer alone may determine what resources remain available for settling a reasonable dispute about payment. The two issues concern different normative powers. Whether I performed correctly concerns the merits of my conduct. Whether your opportunity to question it has ended concerns the terms on which you must accept a resolution.

There is also a temporal point. When consequential discretion is exercised behind an unavoidable informational barrier, the initial authorization creates a relationship that cannot ordinarily be completed by the exercise alone. Its completion includes a reasonable opportunity for the subject, or a suitable representative, to establish how the power was used. That opportunity may be brief, delegated, or waived. It need not be exercised. But an actor cannot count it as supplied merely because, had it been exercised, the answer would have favored the actor.

This resembles Pettit's account of contestatory control, which need not be actively exercised to exist. Pettit already makes transparency, including recorded reasons, a condition of such control. The contribution here is not that dormant contestation matters. It is the implication for closing a completed transaction: a power to act and the authority to extinguish contestation of that act are separate, even when the action was unobjectionable. [Pettit 2006, pp. 302–304, 308–310](https://ppettit.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf7426/files/documents/Democracy%20National%20and%20International.pdf).

## 3. The duty follows control of the means

The normative premise so far concerns standing, not documents. A further argument is needed to show who must preserve what.

Suppose a person is entitled to a practical opportunity to perform some activity, and another deliberately removes its indispensable means. The latter cannot adequately defend the removal by observing that the formal entitlement remains. Leaving an applicant free to complain is insufficient if the committee has destroyed the only materials through which a complaint could be investigated. An account must be open to a response that could bear on its assessment. Otherwise the invitation to respond has little substance.

Three restrictions prevent this point from generating a limitless preservation duty. First, the evidence must bear on matters within the scope of an accountability relation that is still open. Second, the agent must control a necessary or substantially irreplaceable means of making that relation effective. Third, maintaining an adequate route to inquiry must be reasonably possible without disproportionate costs or competing wrongs. These are conditions of the duty, not excuses appended to an unlimited initial demand.

The committee satisfies all three. The records bear on whether applicants received the consideration they were owed. Other people cannot reconstruct the decisive judgments, because discretion was exercised within the committee. The committee can retain the records securely at modest cost. It has acquired both the relevant authority and the associated informational advantage through performing the role. The duty therefore falls on it for a reason more specific than a general obligation to help others acquire knowledge.

What must it preserve? The answer is best stated in terms of an inquiry's capabilities. The committee should preserve, or arrange a suitable substitute for, enough evidence to permit competent examination of relevant possible departures from its obligations. The standard concerns the distinctions an inquiry can make, rather than the volume of material available.

A thousand pages praising the committee's conscientiousness might be less useful than one record showing why an applicant's assessment changed. Conversely, a validated summary may retain everything needed to settle a narrow question even if it omits most of the original data. We should ask which reasonable challenges the preserved materials allow an examiner to investigate, and whether the process would be capable of responding differently if relevant facts differed.

This idea also has clear antecedents. Kroll's account of traceability addresses context-sensitive retention and evidence adequate for external inquiry, including arrangements involving trusted intermediaries. Cobbe, Lee, and Singh explain reviewability through appropriate records of technical and organizational processes, while warning that indiscriminate recording can obstruct understanding. Neither preserving originals in every case nor disclosing everything to everyone follows from those approaches or from mine. [Kroll 2021, §3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.09385v1); [Cobbe, Lee, and Singh 2021, §§3.2, 4.4, 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.04201v1).

There are, however, two questions about a replacement that should not be collapsed. **Functional adequacy** asks whether the replacement preserves the capacity to investigate the relevant kinds of failure. **Authority to close** asks who may decide that the proposed scope, procedure, or substitute settles the entitlement to further inquiry.

A committee's accurate assertion that it applied the criteria correctly does not automatically satisfy either requirement. Unless the assertion can be tested through an adequate independent route, it may reproduce the very dependence that review was supposed to qualify. Even a technically excellent audit of one criterion may leave an authority problem if the committee itself selected that criterion and excluded other matters that the applicants were entitled to raise.

The converse failure is possible too. A reviewer can be properly appointed yet receive records incapable of supporting the assigned investigation. Legitimate appointment does not make evidentially empty scrutiny adequate. Someone must both be entitled to settle the matter and possess the means of doing so responsibly.

The two requirements permit substitutions that a simple command to preserve records would forbid. A competent representative may inspect sensitive originals, retain enough material to support the conclusions and any permitted challenge, and authorize the destruction of unnecessary details. Applicants need not receive personal information about one another. Whether that arrangement suffices depends both on its evidential properties and on the representative's mandate. Calling the representative “independent” answers neither question by itself.

## 4. Why the innocence exception fails

Consider the strongest defense of the committee. A person who has done no wrong need not preserve evidence of wrongdoing, because there is none. If the only purpose of the records is to expose a failure, preserving them when there was no failure is pointless. The committee knows it acted correctly. Why should it bear a cost for an investigation with a foreordained outcome?

The answer begins by identifying the object of the duty. The committee does not owe anyone evidence of a nonexistent violation. It owes the conditions of a permissible inquiry into whether a violation occurred. The inquiry's purpose is not exhausted by producing incriminating findings. Its proper outcome can be confirmation of compliance, rejection of a mistaken complaint, or recognition that the available evidence leaves an issue unsettled. What matters is that these outcomes can depend on inquiry rather than merely on the committee's decision to declare the matter closed.

This distinguishes the duty to maintain evidence from a burden to establish guilt or innocence. Cen and Alur make a related distinction in their treatment of audits as hypothesis tests: the presumption adopted affects who must establish noncompliance or compliance, while an informative test still requires appropriate evidence. Presuming compliance need not license making its contrary untestable. My claim adds a directed moral reason for resisting that move when another party retains standing to call for examination. [Cen and Alur 2024, §§5.2–5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04772v1).

The committee might reply that the difference between actual and possible failure remains decisive. Perhaps an innocent committee can permissibly destroy its records, although a guilty one cannot. Many permissions depend on facts that observers do not know. Why must this permission be externally checkable?

It need not. The argument is not that every permission must announce itself through public evidence. The relevant fact is the applicants' continuing standing. Their claim to a usable opportunity for review applies before they establish whether their treatment was correct. If correct treatment itself extinguished the claim, the committee's first-order conclusion would settle the very second-order authority it had not been given. That would replace an opportunity belonging to the applicants with one available only at the committee's discretion.

Compare a public meeting at which those whose claims are being considered may speak. A chair who already knows the correct decision cannot justify locking the door simply by proving afterwards that no contribution would have improved the result. Whether the decision was right is one question; whether the participants' opportunity had been properly concluded is another. Our case moves the interference earlier in the causal chain. The applicants retain the right to enter the room, but the materials necessary to make their participation effective have been destroyed.

This argument is conditional on a procedural entitlement, and it should be. We do not need to conjure such an entitlement out of the physical existence of paper or data. Section 2 offered a reason for recognizing it in a limited class of relationships. Once recognized, it should not be silently narrowed to a right available only to people who were substantively wronged. Applicants treated fairly can still be denied their proper part in settling whether they were treated fairly.

A related objection appeals to the absence of harm. In the example, everyone's beliefs are true and every allocation is correct. If nobody complains, nobody even experiences frustration. What interest has been set back?

Their capacity to participate in the assessment of how their claims were treated has been removed. This is an actual change in their position, not a possible future false belief. The case excludes further material losses and mistakes; it does not stipulate away the very procedural loss under investigation. Whether that loss is morally significant depends on the argument for retained standing. It cannot be refuted merely by repeating the stipulation that another kind of harm never occurs.

Sturino argues that erasing or revising published material can undermine accountability and encourage evasion. Those concerns are close to the present topic. They do not depend entirely on whether public beliefs happen to be false today. My proposed refinement isolates an especially demanding case: no wrongful allocation is hidden, no useful deterrence is lost, and the wrong is identified through the authority taken away from particular people. This is a development within the ethics of accountability, not the discovery that erasure can matter. [Sturino 2025, §5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/papa.12284).

## 5. Closure, trust, and limits

If an opportunity for inquiry is owed, how can it ever end? Any reviewer may be mistaken; any account may omit something; any preserved record might support another question. Does respecting standing require an endless sequence of reviews?

No. A right to an adequate opportunity is not a right to eliminate every possibility of error. Its extent is determined partly by the importance of the underlying claim, the available means of inquiry, the cost of delay, the rights of other people, and the need for settled decisions. A procedure can therefore have a justified endpoint. What the committee may not do is infer that endpoint solely from its own favorable verdict on the merits.

The distinction is between justified finality and finality secured through control of the evidence. A reasonable review period, an authorized final assessment, or a valid waiver can settle the relation even though further inquiry might conceivably reveal something. A committee that retains useful records through such a period and then disposes of them is not treating its own innocence as a self-issued release. The conditions for closure are justified by considerations about the relation as a whole.

Not every closure rule must be individually negotiated. Public bodies often need general procedures. Applicants can be represented; an institution may adopt a fair retention schedule before individual cases arise. The moral requirement is that the rule be defensible to those whose standing it limits, and that some appropriate means exist for addressing serious challenges to its application. The argument leaves room for reasonable disagreement about what counts as enough. That is preferable to disguising a contestable administrative choice as a deduction from successful performance.

Nor does the principle make trust irrational or morally suspect. Trust often involves refraining from available checks. A person may decide that an inquiry would be unnecessary or damaging to a valued relationship. But declining to exercise an opportunity differs from having the other party extinguish it. In the institutional case, preserved means of inquiry can allow ordinary trust to continue without making it compulsory. It is possible to trust an allocator while refusing to accept that only the allocator may say when scrutiny is finished.

Still, accountability can become oppressive. O’Neill's criticism of elaborate indicators and record requirements is a serious warning: monitoring can displace the work it is meant to assess. The preservation duty defended here is correspondingly limited by proportionality and by its purpose. An additional record is not justified merely because someone can imagine a use for it. [O’Neill 2002, §§3–4](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rmhttp/radio4/transcripts/20020417_reith.pdf).

Privacy can favor destruction. Suppose retaining applicants' intimate circumstances exposes them to a substantial risk of misuse, while a less detailed record would allow all reasonable questions about their treatment to be assessed. Insisting on the originals would misunderstand the duty. If no adequate substitute can be retained safely, some inquiry may have to be sacrificed. The committee should justify that sacrifice by the competing claims and the available alternatives, not by asserting that it has nothing to hide. A good reason to close inquiry must address the interest in inquiry.

There is a danger on the other side. A committee might label a narrow summary sufficient by defining the only permissible challenge as whether its arithmetic was correct. Favoritism, inappropriate criteria, and irregular exceptions would disappear from the stated task. That is why functional adequacy alone is insufficient. A mechanism can perform perfectly against a question selected to insulate its controller. The scope of review must itself reflect the claims being exercised over, rather than simply what the actor finds convenient to demonstrate.

This makes the account compatible with a broad understanding of epistemic rights. Watson and colleagues explicitly resist restricting those rights to narrowly understood information or knowledge. The interest defended here can belong within that broader framework. The distinction is between supplying a favorable epistemic result and preserving a person's authority over how a relevant inquiry is settled, not between epistemic rights and some wholly unrelated moral category. [Watson et al. 2024, “Epistemic Rights as Human Rights”](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-45976-4_14).

Finally, destruction does not prove the original decision was unfair. In our example, that inference is false. The proper complaint concerns a second failure: the committee has disabled an opportunity it owed. If reconstruction is possible, the appropriate response may be to restore an adequate inquiry. If it is impossible, acknowledgement of the loss and revision of future procedures may be warranted. Automatically awarding every applicant the outcome they would have received under their preferred substantive judgment would confuse the two duties once again.

## 6. What a successful account must settle

The compliant allocator shows why success is not always enough to finish an institutional relationship. We can distinguish the merits of a decision from the distribution of authority over its assessment. A person may receive exactly the treatment they were owed and still be deprived of their proper position in determining whether that treatment has been accounted for.

The preservation obligation is consequently selective and relational. It arises where consequential discretion, retained standing, control of indispensable evidence, and reasonable feasibility coincide. It can be discharged through suitable substitutes, limited review, or legitimate closure. Its justification does not require suspicion, an eventual complaint, or a false belief. And it does not authorize intrusive demands wherever evidence happens to exist.

The practical question before destroying records is therefore not just whether their holder has reached the correct conclusion. It is whether the conditions for ending the relevant inquiry have been met. That requires attending both to what the remaining evidence makes assessable and to who is entitled to accept the settlement. An actor's innocence can answer a complaint on its merits. It cannot, by itself, authorize the actor to withdraw another person's opportunity to have the merits examined.
