# What the Will Cannot Undo

## Retrospective consent and the two layers of wronging

### 1. Three cases

*Extension.* A surgeon has my consent to remove a cyst. Once I am under, she finds a second growth: benign, but likely to need removing within the year. Taking it out now will spare me a second anaesthetic. She takes it out. I wake, am told, and say: “Of course. You had my consent. I give it now. Count it as authorized.”

*Ballot.* A returning officer quietly destroys my ballot and substitutes one he has filled in himself — filled in, as it happens, exactly as I filled in mine. The count is unaffected. Afterwards he confesses, and I shrug: “No harm done. I would have voted that way anyway. Consider it mine.”

*The Obliging Agent.* For ten years a literary agent has signed his client’s name to contracts without asking. Every one was a contract the client would have signed; every time, on being told, the client has been delighted and has said, “You had my blessing.”

Each case poses the same question. Can consent given afterwards make the earlier act permissible — not merely excusable, not merely forgiven, but permitted, as though authorization had been in place all along?

Most of us hesitate over *Extension*, balk at *Ballot*, and find something badly wrong with the Obliging Agent without being able to say which of his signatures was the wrong one, if the first was retrospectively fine. Yet the endorsement plainly does something. The patient who says “I am glad” is not merely reporting a mood; the moral weather really has changed. Any account on which retrospective endorsement is simply inert will fail to explain why we seek it, why we are relieved to receive it, and why its withholding is a serious matter.

My aim is to say precisely what retrospective endorsement does and precisely what it cannot do, and to derive both results from a single thesis about the temporal direction of normative powers. The thesis is more general than the case of consent: it applies to promising, release, forgiveness, waiver and authorization alike. If it is right, a familiar move in the ethics of paternalism is not merely mistaken but mislabelled, and a familiar puzzle about forgiveness turns out to be an instance of something structural.

### 2. The thesis

Distinguish two layers of normative relation.

The **primary layer** consists of the directed duties in force over an action and the claims that correlate with them: the surgeon’s duty, owed to me, not to cut me open beyond what I have authorized, and my correlative claim that she not do so. This layer determines whether an act is permissible, and, if it is not, whom it wrongs.

The **remedial layer** consists of the normative relations that come into existence *because* a primary duty has been breached: my claim against the surgeon that she acknowledge what she did and make it good; my standing to protest, resent and reproach; her duty to disclose and to submit herself to my judgement; the impairment of whatever relationship we had; and the standing of third parties to hold the episode against her.

My thesis has one clause and two corollaries.

> **The Forward Reach Thesis (FRT).** An exercise of a normative power at a time *t* can alter only normative relations obtaining at times later than *t*.

> **Corollary 1 (Non-retroactivity of authorization).** No exercise of a power at *t₂* can make an act performed at an earlier *t₁* permissible, or make it the case that its agent was authorized to perform it.

> **Corollary 2 (Reach over the residue).** The remedial relations created by a wrong at *t₁* persist after *t₁*, and are therefore squarely within the reach of a power exercised at *t₂*. The person wronged can waive her claim to repair, forswear protest, and release the wrongdoer.

Put together, these yield a redescription. So-called retrospective consent is not consent. It is *release*: the very operation performed in forgiveness, differing mainly in tone. The patient who says “you had my consent” is doing something real and often admirable, but what she is doing is closing her account, not opening a permission. Calling it consent is a category error, and §7 argues that it is an error with practical costs.

### 3. What a power is

The machinery is Hohfeld’s. Among the elements out of which rights are built, he distinguishes claims (correlative to duties), privileges (correlative to no-claims), powers (correlative to liabilities) and immunities (correlative to disabilities); complex rights are molecules assembled from these atoms, so that my property right over a computer includes first-order privileges and claims and second-order powers to waive, annul or transfer those claims (*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, “Rights”, §§2.1.5–2.1.6).

Hohfeld’s own definition of a power is worth quoting, because its shape already matters:

> A change in a given legal relation may result (1) from some superadded fact or group of facts not under the volitional control of a human being (or human beings); or (2) from some superadded fact or group of facts which are under the volitional control of one or more human beings. As regards the second class of cases, the person (or persons) whose volitional control is paramount may be said to have the (legal) power to effect the particular change of legal relations that is involved in the problem. (Hohfeld 1919: 51)

A power is thus individuated by a *change* it effects through *superadded* facts. That does not by itself rule out backward reach; one could posit a power whose exercise alters what obtained earlier. But it fixes the terms of the question. A power is a way of making something the case. Is it also a way of making something *to have been* the case?

In the moral case the corresponding question is sharper, because moral powers lack the convenience of stipulation. There is no legislature to declare that consent shall have retrospective effect. Hume’s complaint about promising — that an obligation arising from a mere act of will is “one of the most mysterious and incomprehensible operations that can possibly be imagined, and may even be compared to transubstantiation or holy orders” (*Treatise* 3.2.5, as quoted in SEP “Promises”, §1) — is a demand that the power be *grounded* in something. The main contemporary answers ground it in an interest: Raz in an interest in enhanced control over one’s life, Owens in an “authority interest” in holding practical authority over others, Shiffrin in an interest in standing in good relationships (SEP “Promises”, §1.3). Whatever the details, the grounding is by an interest in *authority*: in being the one whose say-so settles a practical question about oneself.

This matters, because an interest-based grounding constrains the shape of the power it grounds. A power exists because having it serves an interest; it has the reach that serving that interest requires, and no more. The question of backward reach is therefore not a free-floating metaphysical question but a question about what authority is for.

### 4. Three arguments for forward reach

#### 4.1 The argument from grounding

What interest would a backward-reaching power of authorization serve?

Not the authority interest. Authority over how one is treated is the standing to determine, by one’s own say-so, what others may do to one. A say-so delivered after the event determines nothing about the event. My retrospective “yes” to the surgeon does not put me in charge of the second excision; the excision was over before my will engaged with it. Whatever I get by speaking afterwards, I do not get control.

What a practice of acting-on-predicted-endorsement might serve is a welfare interest: perhaps things go better, on the whole, if competent agents may act on well-grounded predictions about what we would want. That may be true. But it is a reason for a permission grounded in *beneficence*, not for a power of authorization. The retroactivist who leans on it has changed the subject: he has offered a defence of benevolent interference and labelled it consent. The label matters, because permissions grounded in beneficence face the whole familiar battery of anti-paternalist objections, whereas permissions grounded in consent are supposed to bypass them.

This argument is defeasible: it shows only that the usual rationale for normative powers gives no motivation for backward reach. The next argument is meant to be stronger.

#### 4.2 The bypass argument

To act within another person’s protected domain without her authorization is not merely to bring about a state of affairs she has not approved. It is to stand in a particular relation to her will: to treat her authority as something that can be worked around. Call this *bypassing*.

Now, how an agent stands to another’s will in acting is settled by facts about the action and its practical basis — above all, by what did, and what could, serve as the agent’s warrant for proceeding. A consent given at *t₂* was not the surgeon’s warrant at *t₁*. Nor could it have been: it did not exist, and she proceeded on something else — her own estimate of my good. So the bypassing was complete when she cut, and nothing later can make it not have occurred. The most that later events can do is bear on how much it cost, how it should now be handled, and what it reveals about her.

*Ballot* isolates the point. The returning officer changes no outcome, imposes no welfare loss, and frustrates no preference of mine. What he takes from me is the exercise of my authority: my vote’s counting as *mine*. The perfect match between his substitute and my original is exactly what makes the case instructive, because the wrong survives the match. This shows that authority is not a device for producing the outcomes the right-holder happens to favour. If it were, a sufficiently reliable predictor could discharge it on my behalf, and elections could be replaced by polling. Authority is the standing to determine the outcome oneself.

Someone may object that *Extension* differs because the surgeon was not free to ask. Sometimes that is so, and the difference is real: where authorization was genuinely unavailable, no available authority was bypassed. But notice what follows. In such a case there is no bypassing *and no authorization either*. There is at most a justification of some other kind — necessity, emergency, the prevention of serious and irreversible harm. And where the surgeon could have asked in advance and did not — which is the usual case, since the second growth was not an emergency — she bypassed an authority that was there to be consulted.

One might press further: is not anticipating someone’s will a way of *respecting* it? Sometimes, yes. Knowing what someone wants and acting on it is respectful when one acts on a permission already outstanding — a standing authorization, a delegation, a shared understanding. What is not respectful is treating a prediction of a permission as though it were the permission. The difference between “she told me to use my judgement about the garden” and “I knew she would have agreed” is not epistemic. It is the difference between holding a grant and forecasting one.

#### 4.3 The argument from parity

Two surgeons perform the same unauthorized excision on the same reasoning, with the same care. The first patient endorses; the second, no less reasonably, does not — she minds having been operated on further than she agreed, and says so. The retroactivist must say that the first surgeon acted permissibly and the second did not, though nothing distinguished them at the time, and the difference lies wholly outside the control of either.

This is resultant moral luck lodged in the deontic status of an act, and of a peculiarly unattractive kind, since the “result” is another person’s later decision. The Control Principle — that we are morally assessable only to the extent that what we are assessed for depends on factors within our control — is intuitively compelling, and its corollary is that two people should not be assessed differently when the only differences between them are beyond their control (SEP “Moral Luck”, §1). One may of course accept some moral luck. But an account that avoids gratuitous instances of it, other things equal, is to that extent better.

FRT avoids this one. Both surgeons wronged their patients; they differ only downstream, in what each now owes and what each patient may now demand. That is a difference we are independently content to make hostage to later facts. What repair you owe depends on what damage was actually done and on what the person you wronged now wants; the remedial layer is where outcome-sensitivity is at home. FRT does not abolish the moral luck in these cases. It relocates it to the layer that can bear it.

The Obliging Agent makes the same point diachronically. On the retroactivist view he has an unblemished record: ten acts, ten authorizations, no wrongs. But then his conduct is not merely permissible episode by episode; there is no pattern of anything except good judgement. That is the wrong verdict. What he has is a settled disposition to treat his client’s authority as an obstacle to be managed, and a disposition to do what is permissible is not a vice. FRT gives the natural description: ten wrongs, each of them released.

### 5. What endorsement actually does

If retrospective endorsement is not authorization, it is not nothing. Set out the remedial layer as a list of Hohfeldian incidents and it becomes clear which of them the will can reach.

1. The victim’s claim that the wrongdoer acknowledge the wrong and repair it.
2. The victim’s standing to protest, resent, reproach.
3. The wrongdoer’s duty to disclose and to account for himself.
4. The impairment of the relationship between them.
5. Third parties’ standing to hold the episode against the wrongdoer.

Endorsement is a power over (1) and (2). It waives the claim to repair and forswears protest. That is exactly the structure attributed to forgiveness by those who treat forgiveness as a normative power: on Nelkin’s account the forgiver releases the wrongdoer from the obligation to go on apologizing and making up for it, and on Warmke’s the forgiver places herself under new obligations to withhold further blame (SEP “Forgiveness”, §4.5). Item (3) is discharged rather than waived: the wrongdoer’s disclosure is typically what makes the endorsement possible. Item (4) is not in anyone’s gift; relationships mend or they do not.

Item (5) is the crucial test, and here FRT makes a prediction that practice confirms. A hospital review committee does not close its file because the patient is pleased. If retrospective consent conferred authorization, this would be baffling: the act would simply have been within the surgeon’s remit, and there would be nothing to review. On the release account it is exactly what one should expect. What the patient waived was *her* claim. She has no power over what the surgeon’s colleagues may hold against her, because that standing was never hers to give.

The convergence with the literature on forgiveness runs deeper than coincidence. Hieronymi’s “uncompromising” forgiveness insists that the forgiver keep the judgement that she was wronged, revising away resentment only because the wrong no longer makes a threatening claim (SEP “Forgiveness”, §4.3). Philosophers who think forgiveness incompatible with retracting that judgement standardly classify the retraction as condonation. FRT explains why the constraint is not an optional stipulation. Forgiveness cannot touch the primary layer, because no power can. A “forgiveness” that purported to unmake the wrong would not be an especially generous forgiveness; it would be a confusion.

So retrospective consent is forgiveness with better manners. It is quick, it is warm, and it spares everyone the vocabulary of wrongdoing. That is its charm, and its danger.

### 6. Four apparent counterexamples

#### 6.1 Objective oughts and future facts

Objective oughts routinely depend on later facts. You ought to bet on the horse that will win. Why, then, can “she may operate” not depend on the patient’s later consent?

Distinguish two ways a future fact can matter. It may be a *condition* whose obtaining, whenever established, fixes the status of the act. Or it may be an *exercise of authority*. Consent is the second, and exercises of authority have a feature that mere conditions lack: they transfer practical standing from one agent to another. The permission a consenter creates is not one she discovers; it is one she *gives*. And a gift given at *t₂* is in the recipient’s hands from *t₂*. The retroactivist therefore needs more than the general truth that oughts can depend on later facts. He needs the claim that a giving at *t₂* can put something into another’s hands at *t₁*. That is not a thesis about the direction of grounding; it is a thesis about the direction of giving, and it fails for the same reason that paying a debt tomorrow does not make it true that it was paid today.

The retroactivist can, of course, retreat to a conditional that involves no authority at all: *it is permissible to operate if the patient will afterwards be glad*. That principle is perfectly intelligible and may even be true. But it is a principle of beneficent interference, not of consent, and it inherits every objection that paternalism attracts. Here as in §4.1, the retroactivist wins the word and loses the argument.

#### 6.2 Backward reach in promising

A genuine claim of backward reach is reported in the literature on promising. Liberto is said to argue, on the basis of cases involving promisees who are delayed, absent or not yet specified when the obligation attaches, that uptake has a “backward reach”: it must be capable of being satisfied *after* the promissory obligation has attached (SEP “Promises”, §5.1). I have not read Liberto’s paper, and what follows addresses the structure as reported rather than her argument.

Two replies. First, that structure is consistent with FRT if the relation obtaining at *t₁* is read as already conditional. At *t₁* the promisor bound himself, subject to uptake; at *t₂* uptake settles which branch of an already determinate conditional obtains. Nothing about *t₁* changes. The appearance of backward reach is the appearance of any conditional whose antecedent is settled late — a bet’s terms are fixed when it is placed even though its payoff is fixed when the race ends.

Second, and more importantly, uptake is not an exercise of authority over the promisor. It is a receptive condition on the promisor’s own exercise of *his* power over himself. What late uptake settles is who holds the claim, not from when the binding runs. FRT concerns the direction in which authority is conferred, and a theory may perfectly well allow late completion of a self-binding while denying late conferral of a permission to another. Indeed FRT predicts that we should find the first much easier to swallow than the second, which seems to be the pattern: the suggestion that a promise can await its addressee provokes technical discussion, whereas the suggestion that yesterday’s trespass can be authorized today provokes resistance.

#### 6.3 Relation back in law

Legal systems do operate devices that look retroactive. Hohfeld observes that courts handle the escrow not in terms of powers but “in terms of ‘delivery,’ ‘relation back,’ ‘performance of conditions,’ etc.” (1919: 54 n.74).

Two things should be said. The first is that institutional statuses are constituted by rules, and rules can stipulate what they like; from the fact that a legal system assigns entitlements by reference to a counterfactual past, nothing follows about what anyone morally had. A relation-back rule is a technique for distributing present rights and liabilities, using an earlier date as an index.

The second is more interesting, and it is Hohfeld’s own. His point about the escrow is that the courts’ language obscures a better analysis: once the escrow is formed the grantee holds “an irrevocable power to divest that title by performance of certain conditions,” and “while such power is outstanding, the grantor is, of course, subject to a correlative liability to have his title divested” (1919: 54). Nothing reaches backwards. A power was *outstanding* from the earlier time and was exercised at the later one.

That suggests a general diagnostic, which I recommend as the practical upshot of FRT: **wherever an act looks retrospectively authorized, look for a permission or power that was already outstanding when the act was done.** Standing authorizations — “use your judgement about the garden,” a broad delegation to an agent, an advance directive — are outstanding consent, and are satisfied forward in the ordinary way. Where no such grant can be found, the appearance of retroactive authorization is the appearance of release wearing borrowed clothes.

FRT also yields a testable conjecture about legal practice, which I offer as a conjecture rather than a doctrinal claim: relation-back devices should be hedged in favour of third parties who relied during the interval. If the past had genuinely changed, there would be no interval during which anything was different, and such hedges would be unmotivated.

#### 6.4 Ordinary language

We do say “you had my blessing.” We also say the sun rises. The idiom is the idiom of amnesty, and its generosity is the tell: to say “think nothing of it” is to give something away, and one cannot give away what one never had. If the act had been authorized all along, the client’s magnanimity would be unintelligible — there would be nothing for him to be magnanimous about.

### 7. Two payoffs

#### 7.1 Paternalism

The ethics of paternalism has a recurring move: the interference is defended by the prospect of later endorsement. “You will thank me.” The literature has tended to treat this as one justificatory strategy among others, alongside hypothetical consent and contractualist agreement under idealized conditions (SEP “Paternalism”, §3). If FRT is right, it is not a justificatory strategy at all.

Two consequences follow. The first is that the case for paternalistic interference must be made entirely in terms of considerations that are of the right kind to license acting on someone without her say-so. Future endorsement is not such a consideration; it is at most evidence that the interference will be welcome, which bears on beneficence. That is why “you will thank me later” is experienced as an insult rather than an argument. It offers, as a reason of authority, something that is not a reason of authority — and it does so in the very act of setting the person’s authority aside.

The second consequence is more constructive. Even successful paternalism leaves a residue: a duty to disclose, to account, and to place oneself before the other’s judgement. Standard views mislocate this datum. If the successful paternalist acted permissibly, his subsequent explanation is a courtesy; on FRT it is *owed*, being the wrongdoer’s remedial duty of acknowledgement. And there is a further twist that ought to be decisive for practice. A release given in ignorance releases nothing, since a power cannot be validly exercised over relations one does not know one has. So disclosure is not merely decent: it is the precondition of the very endorsement the paternalist wants to lean on. The interferer who quietly hopes to be vindicated by his subject’s later gratitude, without telling her what he did, cannot get even the release that FRT allows him.

#### 7.2 Prior consent, withdrawal, and re-description

FRT is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is clarifying.

Prior authorization is structurally unproblematic: it runs forward, which is the direction powers run. So the difficulties about advance directives are not difficulties about whether authority can reach across time. They are difficulties about *whose* authority is in force — whether a person’s earlier autonomous directives continue to govern once she has crossed some threshold of capacity, or whether decision-making is inherently present- and future-oriented, so that bygone interests have no purchase on decisions that can only affect the present (SEP “Advance Directives and Substitute Decision-Making”, §§3.1–3.2, discussing Dworkin 1993 and Dresser 1986). FRT does not settle that dispute, but it clears the ground: nothing about temporal reach as such is the problem.

Withdrawal is forward-acting too. To withdraw consent at *t₂* is to end a permission from *t₂*. It does not make the act done at *t₁* unauthorized, any more than revoking an offer unmakes a contract already formed.

This leaves a case that deserves care: the person who comes to believe that what she earlier took herself to have consented to was not really consented to at all. FRT says this is not an exercise of a power. It is a *finding* — a claim that the conditions for valid consent were absent at the time, because of misinformation, pressure, or incapacity. That distinction does real work in both directions. It means, on the one hand, that later reflection cannot manufacture a wrong where the conditions for valid authorization were in fact met. It means, on the other, that later reflection is often the best evidence we have about whether those conditions were met, since a person is frequently not in a position to recognize at the time that she was being deceived or pressed. The impossibility of retroactive consent does not make later testimony idle. It relocates its role from constitution to evidence — which is, as it happens, exactly where the evidential weight belongs.

### 8. Limits

Four concessions.

First, FRT is a thesis about powers, not about normative change generally. Later facts can change how an act is described, what it turns out to have been the beginning of, what it is evidence of, and what significance it carries in a life. FRT denies only that later acts of will can confer authority for it.

Second, I have not argued that the remedial layer is *entirely* within the will’s reach. Some hold that certain claims to repair cannot be waived at will, or that a victim cannot release a wrongdoer from the duty to acknowledge what he did. FRT is neutral here: it says that the remedial layer is the only place where a power could operate, not that a power operates everywhere within it. Critics of normative-power accounts of forgiveness press exactly this — that forgiving someone who has already apologized, or someone dead, waives nothing (SEP “Forgiveness”, §4.5). If they are right, retrospective endorsement does even less than I have allowed, which strengthens rather than weakens Corollary 1.

Third, the bypass argument assumes an authority-based conception of consent. A thoroughgoing instrumentalist — someone who thinks consent’s force consists wholly in its tendency to produce outcomes people like — will not feel its force. I have argued that *Ballot* is trouble for that view, because the substituted ballot produces precisely the outcome the voter wanted. I have not refuted the view.

Fourth, the hardest case for FRT is ratification by a principal who had delegated nothing. Where a broad delegation was already in place, the diagnostic of §6.3 applies and the power was outstanding. Where it was not, I am committed to saying that ratification changes who bears the losses and who may complain, but not who had authority. I believe that is right, and that the practice of ratification is intelligible as a way of settling the first question while leaving the second alone. But it is the point at which the thesis does the most work, and correspondingly the point at which it is most worth resisting.

### 9. Conclusion

The will can give; it cannot have given. That is the whole of the thesis, and most of what follows from it is a matter of keeping two layers apart. On the primary layer, the question is whether an act was within the agent’s authority, and it is settled once and for all by how the agent stood to the other’s will when he acted. On the remedial layer, the question is what is now between them, and that is the will’s proper territory, because it is a question about a relation that is still going on.

Retrospective consent lives entirely on the second layer. It is release, and release is a real and often a fine thing: it ends a grievance, restores a relationship, and lets people stop apologizing. What it does not do is reach back and turn a trespass into a visit. When the patient says “you had my consent,” the truthful translation is: *you did not, and I am not going to hold it against you.* That is a generous thing to say. It is worth hearing what it says.
