# Teaching Students to Question the Lesson

A student can reason for herself and still depend on her teacher in a hidden way. The teacher may welcome disagreement and insist on evidence, yet decide which evidence the student will ever think to seek.

This is a problem for education that claims to develop intellectual independence. We often test that independence by watching what students do with a problem. Can they explain their answer? Can they test it? Can they defend it when the teacher disagrees? These are real achievements. But they do not show that students can investigate how the problem, and the evidence offered for solving it, came to be put before them.

I will call this further ability **selection-assessment**: the ability to identify an important limit on an inquiry, check whether it matters, and adjust one's conclusion when it does. My central claim is about assessment. Good reasoning inside a selected lesson is not, by itself, evidence of selection-assessment. I then defend two further claims. Guided practice can help students develop this ability. And a program that claims to have developed independent judgment in a domain must give students suitable practice and assessment in it.

This duty is conditional. It does not require every lesson to reveal every choice. It applies when a hidden choice is reasonably likely to change the conclusion that an inquiry licenses, or the proper scope of that conclusion. It also depends on the learner's stage, the stakes, the cost of checking, and the division of work between teacher and institution. Yet before a program declares success, students must sometimes face checks whose outcomes were not fixed by the process that selected their starting materials.

## A successful lesson with something missing

Consider a course about how a town should use its public spaces. Students care about the town. They interview residents, compare costs, and revise proposals after criticism. Their teacher never announces a preferred answer. Students disagree with him, and he gives excellent marks to well-supported disagreements.

But the course quietly restricts their starting materials to spaces already approved for commercial development. The documents describe several genuine choices about those spaces. No document suggests asking why other spaces were excluded. The research portal contains only the selected files. Students can request more documents, but only by naming a project or issue. They do not know that a large public garden has been left out, and nothing in the course gives them a reason to suspect it exists.

The students reason well. Some proposals may be right. Their thinking is not fake. The missing ability is different: they cannot discover a major limit on their inquiry unless the selection process happens to reveal it. That process can change the range of plausible conclusions without meeting the students' critical skills. It changes what those skills ever encounter.

Now alter the course. The teacher explains that the first packet covers only proposed commercial developments. Students can inspect a short list of excluded sites and the reasons given for exclusion. They can ask a librarian or planning officer for records through a route that the course's original selection process does not control. They need not study every site. Most may reasonably keep working on the original assignment. Their position has still changed. They can recognize the boundary and investigate whether it matters.

The two courses might produce equally polished reports. They might even produce the same reports. Yet the second supplies evidence of an ability that the first never tests. Its students can discover whether agreement rests on a choice worth checking.

This example concerns an inquiry that invites a town-wide judgment. A worksheet asking students to calculate the areas of three given lots makes no promise to survey the town. The hidden garden is consequential because it could change either the answer to the stated public question or how widely the answer is justified. Selection alone is unavoidable. Trouble begins when a course claims more for the student's conclusion than its hidden limits support.

## Two kinds of achievement

The tension has a long history. In Book II of *Emile*, Rousseau distinguishes dependence on things from dependence on people. He recommends arranging a child's encounters so that experience, rather than direct commands, guides conduct. He also describes the tutor as retaining control while the child experiences himself as choosing freely. The passage shows how personal power can work through an arranged environment. It does not show that Rousseau's whole project fails. In Book III, the pupil can put difficult questions to the tutor. The narrower lesson is that activity inside an arranged setting does not settle how independent the learner is from the arrangement. [Rousseau, *Emile*, Books II–III](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5427/pg5427-images.html).

Dewey explains why activity inside a lesson can still be valuable. In chapter XII of *Democracy and Education*, students need a problem that engages them, material with which to address it, their own suggested solutions, and chances to test ideas in action. The teacher may actively provide the conditions and materials. Dewey also warns against classrooms where the student's real problem is merely to discover what will satisfy the teacher. The first town course avoids that crude failure: its students address a real issue and test their own proposals. Yet its materials can still hide a boundary. Dewey's approach also suggests the remedy. The arrangement of the inquiry can itself become a problem for investigation. [Dewey, chapter XII](https://www.johndeweyphilosophy.com/books/democracy_and_education/Thinking_in_Education.html).

Recent work also treats autonomy, dependence, educational authority, curriculum, and indoctrination as connected problems. Atkinson's Rousseau-inspired account of mentoring new teachers, for example, defends a personalized and autonomous approach while retaining a guiding mentor. I rely only on the publicly accessible abstract for that characterization. The broad worry about hidden guidance is therefore established territory. The narrower proposal here is a test for one gap in claims of educational success: performance within selected evidence does not show that a student can assess its selection. [Atkinson, “Not just Émile”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopedu/qhaf095).

The distinction is simple. A person may be able to judge the evidence she has without being able to judge how that evidence was selected. Both abilities come in degrees. Neither requires complete knowledge. A student who can explain why one sample is biased has begun to assess selection. A student who can compare two arguments but cannot discover that the strongest rival was omitted has mainly exercised reasoning within a selection.

Selection-assessment belongs to intellectual independence because selection can guide belief while leaving first-order reasoning untouched. A student may control each step from her premises to her conclusion while having no effective part in determining which premises become visible. If independence means having a meaningful role in directing and correcting one's beliefs, then an ability to inspect important limits on inquiry is part of it.

This does not make reliance a failure. Every inquiry starts from inherited questions, testimony, tools, and classifications. We often rely reasonably on people who know more than we do. Selection-assessment changes the terms of reliance. It lets a person discover which claim a source supports, where its boundary lies, and when another route is needed. Intellectual independence can consist in better organized dependence rather than solitary knowledge.

## Three claims that should not be confused

The argument now divides into three claims.

First is the **assessment claim**. Performance on a problem with supplied evidence shows how well a student reasons from that evidence. It does not by itself show whether the student can find an important omission in the supply. This claim follows from the town case. Two students can display the same first-order skill while only one has a usable route to find the garden.

Second is the **teaching claim**. Selection-assessment is not produced merely by giving students outside access. An unused library card proves little. Students need guided practice: noticing the stated reach of an inquiry, comparing that reach with the sources supplied, finding a possible omission, judging its relevance and reliability, and revising either the conclusion or its scope. Early examples may be designed by a teacher. Later examples must allow an answer the original selection process did not arrange in advance. That gradual change gives practice a point. It moves from rehearsing an approved objection to handling a possible surprise.

Third is the **duty claim**. A course cannot fairly claim to have developed independent judgment in a domain while it hides avoidable choices that may change what students are justified in concluding there. If the program makes that claim, and students are ready at an acceptable cost, it must give them practice in finding and testing such choices.

Why accept this principle? A claim of domain-level independence says more than “this student can reason from whatever we supply.” It says the student can direct and correct inquiry in that domain. Undisclosed control over a conclusion-changing selection removes one important route of direction and correction. If that control is avoidable, the program cannot use its own restriction as evidence that the missing capacity does not matter. The duty therefore follows from the content of the program's claim, rather than from a general rule that every teacher must expose every curricular choice.

Each condition limits the duty. The selection must bear on a conclusion the course licenses, not merely on any experience the course could have offered. It must be reasonably likely to change that conclusion or its warranted scope. The students must be able to use a check at their stage of learning. And the educational cost and practical stakes must be acceptable.

These limits explain harder cases. An introductory physics course may teach calculations without presenting rival interpretations of quantum mechanics. If it claims only that students can perform and understand those calculations, the omitted interpretations do not change the licensed conclusion. A literature teacher's choice of three novels may strongly shape students' experience. If the course makes no claim that they represent all good literature, disclosing every budget and availability constraint is not required for every discussion. But if the course concludes that the three books show what literature of a whole period was like, hidden selection becomes relevant to the reach of that conclusion.

## What a suitable check requires

A proportionate response has three parts. Students first need to know where an important choice has been made. In the town course, saying that the files cover only commercial proposals may be enough to start. In history, a teacher might say that all of the week's records come from officials. In science, students might be told that an exercise removes measurement noise to isolate one relation. Such statements reveal a boundary without burying beginners under every editorial decision.

Students then need a manageable way to ask whether the boundary matters. A catalogue of a million documents is not usable for a novice. A short index, a worked omission, help from a librarian, time to request another source, or a later research task may be. The test is practical: can students with the skills they have actually learned use the route? A formal permission to object is weak when they have no way to find grounds for an objection.

Finally, the result must be allowed to affect the inquiry. A relevant discovery may widen the assignment, narrow the conclusion, or support the original boundary with better reasons. The teacher need not accept the student's preferred answer. The institution must treat a relevant challenge as evidence about the inquiry, rather than as a failure to obey its design.

Some checks must be able to uncover evidence that the original selection process did not control. The relevant unit is often larger than one teacher. A website and a textbook may repeat the same curriculum. Two teachers may inherit the same exam rules, search ranking, library budget, or disciplinary blind spot. Adding another person does not necessarily create a check.

A check is independent in the limited, functional sense that the original pathway cannot determine whether relevant counterevidence appears. A public archive, a student's field observation, a librarian using a different catalogue, or an appeal process may serve. None is free from influence or error. Its purpose is to make one route of control incomplete.

Responsibility therefore falls across roles. A classroom teacher can state the boundary of an assignment and teach students how to test it. A school can provide library help and protect serious challenges from punishment. A curriculum body can publish exclusions and review procedures. The nearest teacher should not be blamed for every systemic limit, but a program should not claim independent judgment while every route of correction passes through the same hidden filter.

## When delay is justified

The strongest objection concerns cases where openness now may damage learning or safety. Imagine a student learning to identify medical emergencies. The instructor begins with canonical symptoms and omits rare conditions that resemble them. Searching broadly during the first timed exercise could create fatal hesitation in real practice. The instructor may be justified in simplifying the task and even in leaving some complexity opaque at first.

This case shows that the duty unfolds over time. It does not show that selection-assessment is dispensable. A training program can contribute to eventual independence while an early lesson builds fast recognition under guidance. But before it certifies independent clinical judgment, it should include a later stage in which students learn that the first patterns were simplified, study important exceptions, and face cases not arranged to fit those patterns. It should assess whether they can tell when a standard response is warranted and when the case calls for consultation or wider search.

Delay counts as a genuine path only when it is tied to a condition that can be checked. The program might name a later module, a level of competence, or an assessment in which prior simplifications must be recognized. “You are not ready yet” cannot renew itself without limit. A program that never reaches the promised check may still teach useful skills. It cannot yet claim to have produced independent judgment in the restricted domain.

The same answer meets the worry about misinformation. Selection-assessment does not give every rejected view equal time or let a class vote on proof standards. A biology teacher may explain why a claim lacks evidence and teach how that failure is tested. A mathematics teacher may retain proof as a requirement while inviting students to examine why an apparent argument fails. A challenge can end by supporting the original selection. Independence is shown by the quality of the assessment, not by the frequency of rebellion.

There is also a cost objection. Time spent examining a curriculum is time not spent on its subject. That is why the response should track the educational claim and the risk of error. A small simplification may need one sentence. A course that claims to prepare citizens to judge public policy needs more. It cannot count polished arguments on approved questions as complete evidence that graduates can direct public inquiry.

## What success would show

An examination can establish that a student reasons well from supplied evidence. To test selection-assessment, it must ask for something else. Students might have to state the reach of an assignment, identify a hidden limit, find a relevant source beyond the starting packet, and explain whether the new material changes the answer or only narrows its scope. They may defend the original assignment. Disagreement is neither necessary nor sufficient.

Even this performance is evidence rather than a guarantee. A rehearsed exercise can teach the approved objection. Before a program claims that students can judge independently in a domain, they should face at least one suitable case whose result the original selection process has not fixed. The case must fit their level and the stakes. Surprise alone is not education. But without any chance of surprise, the program tests compliance with a script that includes the command to be critical.

This proposal does not settle how to measure the ability across ages and subjects. It also does not establish that no earlier writer has combined the same diagnostic point and practical test. Its narrower value is to identify a common gap. Open discussion, active projects, and strong arguments can all occur within an inquiry whose decisive limits remain hidden. Those achievements matter, but they do not warrant the whole claim of intellectual independence.

Teachers and institutions must choose what learners first encounter. Education begins with a world arranged for the learner. It succeeds in teaching independence only when the learner can eventually identify, test, and challenge that arrangement.
