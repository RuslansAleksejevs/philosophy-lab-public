# One Apology Is Not Everyone’s Settlement

A driver carelessly damages two parked bicycles. He takes responsibility, pays for the repairs and changes how he drives. He apologizes separately to the two owners. One accepts. The other is not ready to speak to him.

The first owner cannot settle the second owner’s complaint. Nor can she announce that the neighborhood has reconciled with the driver. But these limits do not make her own response defective. “I accept your apology to me” need not mean “No one else has anything left to say.”

This elementary distinction becomes less easy to maintain when an apology includes a promise of general reform. A satisfactory apology cannot normally mean: I regret endangering you, but have no objection to endangering everyone else. Its commitment reaches beyond its immediate recipient. Does that make the recipient responsible for judging an apology on everyone’s behalf? And if she cannot do that, does the failure of comprehensive repair weaken whatever obligation she might otherwise have to acknowledge the apology?

I argue that neither conclusion follows without a further premise. The reach of a commitment is not the reach of the recipient’s authority. Moreover, the incompleteness of public repair does not, by itself, defeat reasons for a particular acknowledgment. This identifies a limit on the reach of one argument against duties to accept apologies. It does not establish that victims generally have such duties, much less that they must forgive, resume relationships or provide public endorsements.

## 1. Where the social objection reaches

Cécile Fabre defends a qualified duty to accept a genuine apology, grounded in respect for the wrongdoer as a rational and moral agent. Acceptance includes belief, communication and changed conduct. Crucially, she distinguishes recognizing a changed normative relation from exercising a power to forgive; she also rejects describing acceptance as readmission to the moral community. Her proposed duty can therefore have interpersonal grounds without depending on comprehensive social reconciliation. These claims remain controversial, and I will not assume their truth. [Fabre, §§3–4.3; author-hosted version, pp.7–17.](https://www.cecilefabre.com/uploads/1/3/6/4/13640562/fabre_the_duty_to_accept_apologies.pdf)

Colin Hay objects both to burdens imposed on victims and to an excessively bilateral picture of repair. His social objection has two pertinent moves. Universal forbearance exceeds the victim’s concerns, limiting waiver; uncompleted societal repair also weakens the case for obligatory acceptance. Yet he expressly permits local settlement and later proposes separate bilateral and societal procedures. The charitable reading is that unrealized public benefits undermine a justification resting on those benefits. My objection concerns the stronger inference from incomplete public repair to weakened interpersonal grounds, not his entire position. [Hay, “The waiving of the duty to apologize” and “From apology to reparation.”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41296-024-00743-8)

The distinction between local and wider obligations is already established. Christopher Bennett distinguishes directed obligations that victims can waive from other obligations their waiver leaves intact. He also separates acknowledgment of discharged obligations from forgiveness. His later work on historical injustice allows apology to accomplish its work without perfectly determinate bilateral relations. I borrow this architecture rather than claim to discover it. My contribution is to distinguish the grounds that the social objection can undermine, using separate tests for additional claimants and unfinished public repair. The result qualifies its reach rather than refuting the anti-duty conclusion. [Bennett 2018, accepted manuscript, pp.11–15](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/133937/1/Forgiveness%20as%20Normative%20Power%20PAPA%20final.pdf); [Bennett, “The Relevance of Apology to Reparations for Historical Injustice,” §§4–6](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/japp.70098).

The precise target is the passage beginning “But, crucially,” in Hay’s “From apology to reparation,” immediately before the paragraph beginning “In short.” It connects unattained societal recalibration with diminished obligation. The relevant waiver passage begins “Such objections notwithstanding” in the preceding section and appeals to the apology’s concern for others. Neither passage licenses an unrestricted conclusion about independent interpersonal grounds. This is a limitation of their force against Fabre, whose §§4.1–4.2 offer reasons concerning the apologizer’s agency and the recipient’s response. Those reasons can be disputed on their own terms; they are not conditional on everyone’s reconciliation. The charitable reading of Hay survives, but then does less work against that proposed basis.

We should first separate three matters that the language of “settlement” can blur. A person may release someone from a duty owed to her. A person may acknowledge that a duty has been fulfilled. A person may forgive. None automatically amounts to either of the others. In particular, recognizing your adequate apology need not be an exercise of my power to make an inadequate performance adequate. The waiver argument and the acknowledgment argument consequently require different treatments.

## 2. A commitment can concern people who are not its recipients

Suppose you promise me that, while caring for my garden, you will also keep the shared gate closed so nobody’s dog escapes. Our neighbors benefit. They may already possess rights concerning the gate, and your undertaking might generate further responsibilities. Still, the fact that your promise concerns their dogs does not make me their representative. If I release you from your promise to me, their independent rights need not disappear. Whether I may permissibly release you when doing so would endanger those dogs is a further question. A limit on the consequences of a release is not the same as absence of the power to release a particular claim.

An apology involves a similar distinction, without needing to be analyzed as an ordinary promise. We must ask separately who benefits from future reform, who was wronged, and whom the particular address is owed to. These groups often overlap. They are not interchangeable.

Return to the driver. His commitment to careful driving benefits future cyclists he has never met. Some other people may also have been wronged by the original incident: a passerby exposed to an unjustified risk, for example. A community might have a further claim arising from the violation of a shared norm. We can grant all these claims. None entails that an apology owed to one bicycle owner is owed to her as everyone’s representative.

Now suppose that owner declines the encounter: “You do not need to give me an apology. I want no further contact.” Assume she can validly waive the apologetic address owed to her. Three things remain distinct. The driver may still owe an apology to the other owner. He may still owe public acknowledgment or other repair to the community. He certainly still owes everyone appropriate care. Her waiver does not cancel any of these obligations.

The broad content that her apology would have expressed does not change this. If she had heard it, she would have heard a commitment extending to others. Declining to hear that commitment does not authorize its opposite. Nor does the duty to drive carefully first come into existence when someone receives an apology. Eliminating a particular occasion for affirming a duty does not eliminate the duty affirmed.

There may be reasons why exercising an effective power of waiver would be wrong, and other reasons why a purported waiver would not take effect at all. A spokesperson might lack the authority to waive a collectively held claim. By contrast, someone might possess a power over her own claim yet betray a separate undertaking by exercising it. Perhaps silencing the only available account would expose others to danger. But these are additional relationships and consequences. They do not follow simply from the universal reach of the wrongdoer’s prospective reform.

The strongest assurance objection remains open: the driver may owe others a publicly answerable account of his recognition and reform. That obligation is additional to both ordinary careful driving and the private address to this owner. Waiving the private address does not fulfill the public assurance duty. It leaves that duty outstanding. Nor does waiver automatically cancel every responsibility concerning the owner’s subsequent attitudes. I am not defending a package in which declining an encounter requires her to treat the driver as fully restored.

The corrected conclusion is thus narrow: inability to waive everyone’s claims constrains what a waiver accomplishes. It does not, without more, show inability to waive one’s own. Conversely, identifying a valid waiver does not show that exercising it is wise, fair or obligatory. Keeping these questions separate avoids both inflating and denying the victim’s control.

## 3. Two tests, and what each shows

Acknowledgment presents a different problem. My power to waive an apology gives no answer to why I must accept one. Three questions need separating: whether a consideration favors a response; whether it grounds a duty; and whether competing considerations defeat that duty overall. The tests below concern a specified consideration. Any conclusion about a duty is conditional on a further account of when that consideration generates one.

Take this response by the first owner: “I believe that you understand what you did to me and that your remorse is sincere.” Its proposed interpersonal ground is the importance of acknowledging, rather than withholding acknowledgment of, a person’s adequately evidenced recognition of a particular wrong. That ground is contestable. Someone might hold that belief is owed but communication remains optional. For present purposes the point is that the object of the proposed reason is identifiable: this person’s recognition of this wrong, addressed to its victim.

The response does not promise friendship, erase the original wrong or undertake to give the driver’s interests the same weight they had before it. Nor does it certify that every aspect of his character has changed. A person could communicate it while refusing contact afterward. The argument therefore concerns the proposed communicative component of acceptance, not Fabre’s entire package of belief, communication and changed treatment. A duty to stop falsely describing someone as unrepentant would be thinner still; establishing that duty alone would not establish even the communicative component.

First consider a test of additional claimants. There is one damaged bicycle, an adequate apology and evidence of sincere reform. Grant an unsettled public claim as well: the incident requires an acknowledgment to the neighborhood. Now add a second bicycle owner who receives an equally sincere offer but postpones the encounter. The first owner’s evidence, the cost of responding and the meaning of her words remain the same. No shared remedy is diverted; neither response is a condition of the other.

The new claimant does not, merely by existing, change whether the driver has adequately addressed his recognition to the first owner. That is why the specified interpersonal consideration can remain unchanged. The point is not that we have stipulated a duty immune to criticism. It is that the newly unsettled claim has no identified bearing on the object of this consideration. A duty of solidarity, a changed assessment of sincerity or a new social consequence could supply that bearing. Each would be an additional argument.

This first comparison tests only multiplication. It does not show that public incompleteness, already present in both cases, had no prior effect on the overall justification. To address that different issue, consider a second comparison.

Hold fixed the original incident, private apology and evidence of reform. A credible public assurance is also owed. In one version, the driver delivers that assurance at the neighborhood’s scheduled meeting. In the other, the neighborhood postpones its meeting. He remains willing to attend; his sincerity and the available evidence are unchanged. The first owner receives no demand to speak for the neighborhood in either version. There is no emergency, scarce remedy or predicted misuse of her acknowledgment. Public repair is complete in the first version and unfinished in the second.

The postponement does not alter what the driver has already acknowledged to this owner, or whether her proposed response accurately recognizes it. Nor does it alter the interpersonal interest invoked to support communicating that recognition. If that interest supplies a reason in the first version, its specified basis is present in the second. This is an explanation of invariance, not merely an instruction to imagine identical duties: scheduling a separate acknowledgment does not change the completed communicative exchange to which the reason responds.

A defender of comprehensive settlement might nevertheless insist that no recipient has reason to acknowledge a local apology until public repair is complete. That claim needs a principle making the local response depend on the public process. It cannot be inferred from the existence of a still-owed assurance. The second version leaves that assurance outstanding rather than concealing it or declaring it unnecessary. Conversely, if the postponed meeting delays a benefit that was itself an additional reason for accepting, the total case may indeed be weaker. That is fully compatible with the survival of the specified interpersonal consideration.

The conclusion is deliberately restricted. Bare multiplication does not refute this proposed basis, and unfinished public repair need not alter its object or grounds. Whether the surviving reason generates a duty remains disputed; whether any such duty survives other burdens is a further question. The tests expose what a social objection must additionally establish, rather than prove that victims owe the response described.

## 4. What would connect the claims?

The social objection becomes stronger when it identifies a relationship between settlements. Four variations show why the qualification matters.

First, change the meaning of the response. The driver requests a letter saying that all affected residents consider the incident resolved. One owner cannot responsibly provide that assurance merely because her own apology was adequate. Her lack of representative authority now directly bears on the act requested. She can accept her own apology while refusing the letter. This is a limit on the proposed scope of acknowledgment, not a reason to pretend no apology to her occurred.

Second, change the resources. The driver offers to repair the first bicycle only if its owner accepts a settlement consuming a fund that should also remedy the second owner’s damage. The first owner’s action now affects the second owner’s remedy. An objection can appeal to fair distribution, complicity or the terms imposed. It need not claim that multiple victims always make local repair incomplete in a disabling way. Indeed, once the unfair condition is removed, that particular objection loses its footing.

Third, change the evidence. The driver apologizes solicitously to the influential owner while openly dismissing the poorer owner’s identical complaint. This casts doubt on whether he recognizes the wrong rather than merely regretting a costly consequence. The first owner may no longer have reason to believe in the general reform that her apology requires. The other claimant matters because the contrast undermines sincerity. This differs from a case in which a sincere driver awaits an encounter the second owner freely postpones.

Fourth, change the predictable social effect. A private acceptance will be advertised as proof that residents should stop asking about an unresolved danger. If that misuse cannot reasonably be prevented, the first owner may have a reason to postpone public endorsement, qualify her words or withhold a response. Whether she must do so depends on the stakes and available alternatives. The crucial point is that the objection identifies a mechanism by which her response affects others.

These variations are not a complete account of collective repair. They show what is missing from the bare appeal to unsettled public claims. The needed connection may be semantic, distributive, evidential or practical. It may also be constitutive: some claims really belong to a group and cannot be divided into individual shares. In such a case, an individual cannot simply declare her supposed portion settled. But a claim’s collective ownership requires an argument about that claim. It cannot be inferred from the fact that a private apology includes a commitment to treat everyone properly.

The framework is therefore compatible with profound social criticism. It asks the critic to explain how the social facts bear on this action, rather than assume that private acknowledgment must either settle society’s account or lack sufficient normative significance.

## 5. The charitable reading and the remaining burden

An important qualification preserves the best version of the objection. Suppose someone supports obligatory acceptance exclusively because it would secure comprehensive public reconciliation. Show that one victim’s acceptance cannot secure that result, and an essential premise fails. More modestly, if the justification assigns substantial weight to an unrealized public benefit, recognizing its absence can weaken the justification. Nothing in my argument restores that missing benefit.

My complaint is about transferring this result to a different justification. A duty grounded in a particular person’s claim to an accurate acknowledgment does not automatically rise and fall with the production of public reconciliation. The public-benefit objection may show that one proposed ground is unavailable. It does not show that every independently proposed interpersonal ground is reduced by the same amount. Thus Hay can be correct about the loss of a societal justification while that point does not answer the different interpersonal justification. This is a limit on the objection’s dialectical reach, not evidence that he denies every possible local reason.

There is a second qualification. Even an unchanged interpersonal reason can be overridden. If replying exposes a victim to renewed trauma, manipulation or danger, showing that other people’s claims are separable provides no answer to that burden. A complete defense of obligatory acceptance would need to justify the demand placed on this victim. The counterexample here does not supply such a defense. It only prevents a missing public benefit from doing argumentative work that properly belongs to an account of burdens or competing claims.

Nor need this correction settle whether acceptance amounts to forgiveness. Oliver Hallich argues that apologies seek forgiveness and can burden recipients through that request. If that account is correct, we have further reasons to scrutinize demands for acceptance. But the scope distinction persists: even forgiving a wrong to oneself need not forgive another person’s wrong on that person’s behalf. [Hallich, §§1–4.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10892-025-09519-7)

The comparison also need not treat apology as completed in a moment. Rebecca Harrison argues that apologizing unfolds through uptake and subsequent action. We can place the two owners’ responses after the relevant evidence of follow-through, or understand them as acknowledgments at corresponding stages of an ongoing process. The argument requires separable reasons for responses, not a theory on which uttering the right words finishes repair. [Harrison, §§1,3–4.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05478-0)

## 6. A limited conclusion with practical weight

The result can be stated without settling the larger dispute about what victims owe. Universal reform does not make the recipient of an apology everyone’s representative. A waiver of one apologetic address need not cancel duties to other people. And unfinished collective repair need not weaken the specified interpersonal reason for communicative acknowledgment, even when it reduces the overall public benefit of acceptance. To reach that last conclusion, an argument must show dependence or interference between the local response and the unsettled claims.

This matters for victims as well as wrongdoers. Requiring every local response to stand for a collective verdict can burden the person who wants to accept as well as the person who does not. The first may be accused of abandoning others; the second may be treated as the final obstacle to everyone’s reconciliation. Sometimes those social responsibilities are real. They should be established through the relationships involved, not assigned automatically by the reach of someone else’s promise to improve.

One owner’s acceptance is not evidence that the other owner is ready. The other owner’s unresolved complaint is not evidence that the first owner’s acknowledgment is empty. Repair can remain unfinished without every completed part losing its significance.
