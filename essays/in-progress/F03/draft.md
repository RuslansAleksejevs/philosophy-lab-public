# An Error Is Not Yet a Metaphysical Verdict

An error about what a sentence says is not automatically an error about how the world is. But removing the first kind of error does not establish that the second is absent. These two points impose a joint constraint on arguments about essence. An example of mistaken judgment must be traced to a failure that also operates in the disputed case. A reformulation that removes an ambiguity must be shown to remove that failure, rather than merely change its expression.

I defend this constraint through the recent dispute between Timothy Williamson and Kit Fine. My conclusion is neither that essence reduces to necessity nor that it exceeds necessity. It concerns what could decide between those views. A known error and a stable judgment after correction can each fail to distinguish the rival explanations available. When they do, the debate must compare accounts of reality together with accounts of how judgments about it are formed. Charging one metaphysical theory for an error that its rival predicts equally well mislocates the evidence.

The issue matters because judgments about essence help determine what metaphysics is allowed to explain. Is being human part of what a person is, while membership in a certain set is merely something the person cannot avoid? Or does that contrast reflect our explanatory interests rather than a further division in reality? A verdict about this question should not emerge from a mistake about which part of an example has actually been tested.

Fine's familiar challenge concerns a person and the set containing only that person. On the relevant modal assumptions, neither can exist without the other. Yet Fine takes the person's membership to belong to the nature of the set, not to the nature of the person. Necessity then fails to capture the direction of essence. This is a claim about what constitutes a thing, not merely about how we happen to identify it. [Fine 1994](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366643157_ESSENCE_AND_MODALITY_Kit_Fine_Philosophy_NYU)

Call a view *modal* if necessarily equivalent claims cannot differ in essentiality, once the relevant existence conditions are held fixed. Call its rival *finer* if some such claims can differ. These labels mark the present disagreement, not complete theories. They leave open the metaphysics of sets, the correct conditions on existence, and which properties are essential.

Williamson argues that some compelling essentialist judgments conflict with ordinary identity. Complex functional terms can directly designate their values. Distinguishing the essentiality of identities involving different terms for the same number then produces error. A relevance heuristic may explain both these errors and the disputed judgments about sets: apparently extraneous material triggers rejection. The argument is an evidential challenge, not a claim that every fallible method is worthless. [Williamson 2025, §§3–5](https://metaphysicsjournal.com/articles/10.5334/met.195)

Fine's reply already separates an object's occurrence in a sentence from its presence in the content expressed. He offers a diagnosis involving the misreading of functional terms, distinguishes an ontological principle from a linguistic one, and proposes reformulating the singleton case with variables to prevent irrelevant linguistic detail from intruding. The distinction between reference and aboutness is therefore not my proposed addition. [Fine 2025, §3](https://metaphysicsjournal.com/articles/10.5334/met.209)

My addition is a paired argument. The error controls need not discriminate between the rival metaphysics; the proposed linguistic controls need not discriminate between rival accounts of our access to metaphysical truth. Recognizing only the first limitation gives essence an unwarranted immunity. Recognizing only the second gives a diagnosis of error an unwarranted reach.

Consider first how to establish an error without settling the metaphysics. Let a name an object. Define a function d_a that takes any object as input and returns a. Whatever b is, d_a(b) is a. For this example, stipulate that the whole term d_a(b) contributes just that object to the sentence in which it occurs. This is a deliberately fixed semantic setting. It makes no claim that all ordinary functional expressions work this way.

Now compare:

(1) It is essential to a that a = a.

(2) It is essential to a that a = d_a(b).

Someone may find (2) objectionable because b seems extraneous to what a is. But under the stipulated interpretation, the two embedded claims have the same content. Any difference in verdict is an error. We do not even need to decide whether self-identity counts as essential. If it does, both claims are true; if it does not, both are false. The point is their equal status.

The example allows arbitrary detours. The input b could be a mountain, a coin, or a remote star. Its appearance need not make the claim about that object. Nor does evaluating the term erase a relation that the sentence originally asserted. Under the stipulated semantics, the detour supplied a way of referring to a from the outset. There was no additional relation to b in the embedded identity claim.

This gives a useful control. It can reveal that a person is treating a route to a referent as part of the asserted content. Yet its diagnostic usefulness should not be confused with a result about essence. The function could equally be inserted into a claim about location or weight. The semantic mistake would remain a mistake even if there were no distinctive relation of essence.

Compare a third sentence:

(3) It is essential to a that a belongs to b.

Here a and b are simple names, and b names the singleton of a. Replacing either name with another name for the same object leaves a membership predication. It does not turn the predication into self-identity. That further transition requires an account of the content of whole claims, not merely an account of what names contribute.

A small semantic illustration makes the boundary precise. Suppose referring terms are evaluated to objects, while an atomic sentence has content consisting of its predicate and those objects. The content of both embedded identity claims is then represented by Identity(a, a). The content of the embedded claim in (3) is represented by Membership(a, b). One can accept substitution of co-referring terms while distinguishing these contents. One can also instead identify the contents when their truth conditions necessarily agree. The term semantics alone settles neither choice.

This is a compatibility illustration, not a complete semantics of essence. It does not establish that structured contents are metaphysically real, or that any difference between them is essential. Its purpose is to locate the open issue. The known error concerns two presentations of one content under an agreed interpretation. The contested case concerns whether two predications present one content, and whether their relation to a's nature can differ.

Merely pointing to this difference would be an inadequate response to a debunking argument. An error can undermine judgments beyond the exact class in which it was first detected. If a measuring instrument is affected by heat, discovering an error in one hot room can rationally reduce confidence in its readings in another. The rooms need not be identical. What matters is whether the feature responsible for the error is also present.

The metaphysical case needs an equivalent connection. Did the subject misread a referring term, or use explanatory usefulness as a substitute for essential truth? The first process might fail in (2) but not in (3). The second might affect both. Establishing that (2) has received a wrong verdict does not by itself identify which process produced it.

This point can be sharpened by separating three components of an account: a metaphysical theory, a semantics for the examples, and an account of judgment. A metaphysical theory says which claims are essential. A semantics says what the sentences express. An account of judgment says how a thinker arrives at a verdict. Agreement on two components does not force agreement on the third.

Let M be a modal theory and F a finer theory. Let S be the stipulated semantics for the detour example. Let A be an account on which subjects sometimes mistake a complex referring term for a relational description. Both M and F can accept S and A. Both can agree that assigning different verdicts to (1) and (2) is erroneous. Both can predict that the error becomes more likely when the detour is harder to evaluate.

Consequently, conditional on that common account, the probability of the control error is the same under the two metaphysics:

P(error | M, S, A) = P(error | F, S, A).

This is not an estimate of anyone's psychology. It states the consequence of comparing theories while holding fixed a judgment process that predicts the same error. An observation with the same likelihood on both sides supplies no relative support between those two packages.

The qualification about packages is essential. It does not follow that the evidence is unconditionally neutral between every modal theory and every finer theory. One side might have independent reason to favor a different account of judgment. One might entail constraints inconsistent with A. Or A might become implausible in light of further cases. Those are possible sources of discrimination. They need to be supplied rather than inferred from the bare occurrence of the error.

The same qualification prevents an easy essentialist victory. A finer theory cannot simply announce that the control error concerns reference while every surviving judgment tracks essence. That is one hypothesis about access to truth, and a favorable one. It requires support. In particular, a plausible alternative may explain the surviving verdict without appealing to a distinctive essentialist fact.

Consider a thinker who has corrected the detour error. The thinker now accepts that (1) and (2) must share a verdict but continues to reject (3). There are at least two ways this could happen.

On a first account, the thinker understands the membership claim and correctly judges that the set plays no part in constituting the person. The earlier error concerned a mistaken interpretation. Once that interpretation is corrected, a sound metaphysical judgment remains.

On a second account, the thinker understands the membership claim but judges it by asking whether mentioning the set helps answer an ordinary request to explain what the person is. The answer is no. The thinker converts that unhelpfulness into a verdict about essence. Correcting the interpretation of (2) does not correct this different transition.

Both accounts predict the correction of the detour error and the persistence of the target judgment. The observation therefore does not choose between them. This is the second limitation, the counterpart of the first. An error may be common to rival metaphysics; a successful correction may be common to rival diagnoses.

Using variables does not automatically change that result. Suppose a speaker points out the person and the singleton, assigns them the labels a and b, and then asks about (3). Understanding the sentence requires keeping track of which objects the labels designate. A thinker can retrieve that information and assess the claim through a familiar paraphrase. The written sentence contains no elaborate referring term, but the thinking involved in understanding it can still recruit one.

More generally, an explanatory heuristic need not inspect the letters currently displayed. It may operate on a representation produced after reference has been resolved. For example: this is a claim involving the person's membership in a set; mentioning that membership would be unhelpful in explaining the person; so the claim is not essential. This process is invariant under a change from ordinary names to variables. Its invariance does not tell us whether the final transition is legitimate.

One should distinguish a literal word filter from a broader explanatory heuristic. A filter that rejects sentences whenever a particular string occurs can be disabled by removing the string. A process that recovers a familiar description and assesses its explanatory role may survive that intervention. Evidence against the first is not automatically evidence against the second.

There is no need to insist that this broader process actually explains the judgments. The present point is conditional and diagnostic: a change of notation does not establish its absence. The process is also not an arbitrary invention designed only to defeat the control. Retrieving what a variable stands for is ordinarily necessary for understanding a claim about particular things. Moving from that recovered information to a familiar explanatory task requires no mysterious additional faculty.

This does not invalidate the variables control. It blocks some misunderstandings and helps display which objects are at issue. It can also be a useful step in an investigation of judgment. What it cannot do on its own is convert linguistic stability into evidence that the surviving judgment tracks the world's constitutive structure.

The two limitations support a definite rule for assessing the dispute: a proposed diagnostic observation should be credited only with the comparisons it actually discriminates. A control error can discriminate between an infallible and a fallible account of judgment. It may discriminate between competing interpretations of a term. But if rival metaphysics predict it equally under the same account of access, it does not discriminate between them. Likewise, a stable verdict under renaming may discriminate against a literal word filter while leaving competing content-sensitive explanations untouched.

This rule concerns the allocation of evidence, not an unattainable demand for certainty. It does not require identifying a mental mechanism beyond possible doubt. It requires some reason to think that the competing accounts predict different outcomes, or fit the total evidence differently. Without such a difference, calling an observation a control does not make it one.

A critic may object that the comparison has been artificially balanced. If the finer view was introduced to preserve the very intuitions now shown to be unreliable, does it not suffer a loss that the modal view escapes?

There can indeed be an asymmetric loss of support. Suppose a finer theory had been supported by the assumption that the relevant judgments never confuse reference with content. The detour error defeats that assumption. A modal theory that never relied on it loses no corresponding support. Equal conditional prediction does not erase the history or structure of a theory's justification.

But this establishes a loss for a particular route to the finer theory, not a verdict on every route. The next comparison must concern the repaired accounts. Can the finer theory acknowledge the reference error while retaining independent reasons for its claims about essence? Can the modal theory explain the remaining judgments without additional unsupported assumptions? The strength of the initial challenge depends on how much of the finer theory's support actually relied on the defeated assumption.

It would therefore be a mistake to compare a modal theory equipped with a sophisticated theory of fallible judgment against a finer theory required to endorse every initial reaction. Both sides are entitled to distinguish successful from unsuccessful exercises of thought. Neither is entitled to do so solely according to whether the verdict favors its metaphysics.

Conversely, a defender of the finer view might say that the metaphysics already supplies the relevant distinction. If essentiality implies a certain dependence, and the person does not depend on the set, then the denial of essentiality follows. Why insist on a further account of judgment?

The inference may be valid. Its epistemic use depends on how the lack of dependence is known. If that premise is supported only by the very sense of explanatory irrelevance under examination, the inference gives that sense another name without independently validating it. If the premise has independent support, however, the objection succeeds. The target judgment then rests on more than its initial appearance or its stability under reformulation.

What counts as independent support here need not be evidence from a laboratory. It might be a general account of objects that explains several otherwise unconnected structural features and entails the relevant direction of dependence. The important requirement is that the reason for accepting the account should not consist wholly in its ability to reproduce the target verdict. A theory can organize judgments as well as explain them; organization becomes evidentially stronger when it imposes constraints that were not chosen just to preserve those judgments.

A further objection is that theoretical simplicity already breaks the tie. If modal theories require less structure, should we not prefer them as soon as the alleged counterexamples lose their authority?

Perhaps. Nothing in the argument rules out an eventual preference based on simplicity, strength, or broader explanatory success. What it rules out is treating the mere presence of a reference error as if it had measured all those advantages. Simplicity is a property of an articulated account. One must specify what each account includes and what work each additional commitment performs.

In particular, the cost of a mistake belongs initially to the component that generates it. If a shared model of interpreting complex terms predicts the error, the error does not by itself show that one ontology is needlessly complicated. If explaining the remaining judgments forces one side to add a special exception for every example, that is a genuine comparative cost. The difference must be demonstrated in the accounts, not assumed from which side calls the judgments mistakes.

Nor does this mean that every additional hypothesis can be excused as a repair to our access rather than to reality. An access model can itself be contrived. Its flexibility can insulate any favored metaphysics from evidence. A proposal that lets the theorist label every troublesome judgment a new kind of illusion has paid for explanatory fit with an uncontrolled ability to accommodate outcomes.

The relevant discipline is predictive constraint. State the interpretation, the proposed source of error, and the expected effects of a correction before inspecting all the cases to be explained. Then ask which changes the account predicts without further adjustment. This does not guarantee a true metaphysics. It makes visible where its explanatory success is purchased.

A concrete investigation could begin with the detour family. Vary the complexity of a referring term while holding its referent and the asserted relation fixed. Check whether the thinker understands the stipulated reference and whether apparent essentiality changes with the complexity. Such variation can test an account of reference failure without building the desired answer to the singleton question into the test.

A second step could compare conditions in which the assignment of simple labels is fresh or familiar, and in which the associated description is salient or unobtrusive. If a proposed heuristic depends on retrieving a familiar explanatory description, those changes give it something to predict. If it predicts no difference under any change, its alleged psychological explanation needs more specification. No particular outcome is assumed here.

Even a successful investigation of that kind would not supply a direct truth test for the singleton judgment. The verdict about essence remains disputed. What the investigation could provide is evidence about whether the mechanism independently detected in the controls also helps explain the target judgment. That is the missing bridge. Once supported, it could rationally diminish the evidential value of the judgment, even without proving it false.

The defender has a corresponding task. Passing a reference check and preserving a verdict under several presentations establish at most that certain sources of error have been controlled. To turn the remaining verdict into substantial support, the defender should connect it to constraints or explanations whose credibility does not wholly depend on the verdict itself. Stability is useful because instability can expose a problem. The absence of that problem is not the presence of every virtue required for knowledge.

These tasks are demanding, but neither side must complete an entire science of philosophical cognition before reasoning about essence. Evidence can be partial. A debunking hypothesis can be plausible before experimentally confirmed, and a metaphysical theory can be reasonable before independently established. The constraint is comparative: a theory should not claim a victory from observations that its live rival can predict on equally supported assumptions.

This also explains why the proposed argument is not merely the familiar reminder that people can make mistakes. General fallibility leaves unanswered which observations matter. The paired analysis identifies two specific failures of discrimination. The first concerns a change from a term's contribution to the content of a sentence. The second concerns a change from a sentence's visible form to the process that produces a verdict about it. Keeping those changes separate tells us which further evidence would be relevant.

The metaphysics of essence concerns whether reality contains distinctions that necessary truth alone cannot express. Its resolution may favor a modal theory, a finer theory, or a reformulation of the question. But the route through errors has a structure of its own. A reference mistake is decisive against a judgment that violates the stipulated reference. It travels further only with support for a diagnosis that travels. A transparent reformulation removes an identified semantic danger. It protects a metaphysical judgment only to the extent that the relevant cause of error has also been removed.

The proper lesson is a rule for inquiry: compare what the rival accounts predict about both the world and our access to it, and assign each observation to the difference it actually tests. An error is evidence. So is a successful correction. Neither is yet a metaphysical verdict.
