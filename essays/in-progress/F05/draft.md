# Knowing the Experience You Changed

Consider two ways of trying to inspect an inner image. In the first, bringing attention to a faint image makes it clearer and sometimes brings an image into existence. In the second, trying to hold an image still sometimes makes it disappear. These are descriptions of possible methods, not claims that either describes introspection generally. Suppose, moreover, that the observer accurately judges what is present when the inspection ends. Can that judgment establish what was present before inspection began?

The usual warning is that introspection may change its object. That warning is both too blunt and too weak. It is too blunt because a method can change experience and still support a sound judgment about its earlier condition. It is too weak because even perfect knowledge of the inspected experience may leave a claim about the earlier experience unsupported.

I defend a more discriminating position. A method which can create a feature, but cannot remove it, can sometimes establish that the feature was previously absent. A method which can remove a feature, but cannot create it, can sometimes establish that the feature was previously present. The qualifications concern the particular feature and interval under investigation. They do not require the whole experience to remain unchanged.

These asymmetries matter to the authority of first-person reports. We can accept an observer's account of the experience she now has without accepting her inference about the experience she had before looking. Conversely, acknowledging that reflection interferes with experience need not force us to distrust everything it reveals. The relevant question is what the interference can do to the particular claim being carried across time.

I will use “experience” for conscious experience: seeing a colour, feeling pressure, entertaining an auditory image, and so on. “Inspection” means the attempt to attend to and judge some feature of that experience. Nothing in the argument requires every kind of self-knowledge to work by such inspection. Nor does it assume that consciousness requires attention or that it can occur without attention.

The background problem is old. James argues that trying to arrest a transition in thought can destroy the very character one hoped to inspect. He also describes present experience as retaining something of its immediate past. Both observations matter: disturbance threatens observation, while temporal continuity offers a possible route around the threat. Neither should be omitted from an account of his position. (James 1890, chapter IX, pp. 240–245, [text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin9.htm).)

Schwitzgebel's discussion of unattended experience makes the complementary danger explicit. Attending to a bodily sensation might produce the experience whose earlier presence is disputed. His proposed use of immediate retrospection targets the moment before a signal, and he distinguishes several possible sources of overreporting and underreporting. Creation, temporal misattribution, and memory loss are therefore already central to the problem. (Schwitzgebel 2007, author manuscript, §§5–6 and 10, [text](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/ExpWOAttn060301.htm).)

Schooler and Schreiber likewise distinguish having experience from explicitly characterising it, and identify detection, transformation, and substitution among the sources of introspective failure. The possibility of interference is not the contribution of this essay. (Schooler and Schreiber 2004, abstract, [author-uploaded text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233527104_Experience_Meta-consciousness_and_the_Paradox_of_Introspection).)

The contribution I propose is a limited reconstruction of what interference permits. It separates accurate judgment of an inspected state from transmission of that judgment to an earlier state; identifies two different conditions for that transmission; and explains why sequences of accurate local judgments can support a false picture of the mind as a whole. These are applications of familiar logical distinctions. Their philosophical use is to locate a failure without automatically placing it in the observer's knowledge of her current experience.

Take a determinate question: was there any visual imagery during a short interval just before inspection? Let F₀ mean that the feature was present in that target interval, and F₁ mean that it is present during the later interval being judged. The feature must remain fixed. “Some visual imagery” must not silently become “a vivid image” or “an image receiving attention.” We may choose a coarse feature or a fine one, but we must use the same one on both sides.

For now, grant the observer accurate knowledge of F₁ or its absence. This is a substantial concession. Failing to find an image is not automatically knowing that no image is present. But granting local accuracy allows us to isolate a different problem. Even an observer who makes no such detection error must still get from the later interval to the earlier one.

There are four possible transitions: absence to absence, absence to presence, presence to absence, and presence to presence. To infer earlier presence from later presence, we must exclude the transition from absence to presence. To infer earlier absence from later absence, we must exclude the transition from presence to absence. The two requirements are different.

Call the first condition **no gain**: across the relevant transition, a previously absent F does not become present. Given this condition, knowledge of F₁ supports the inference to F₀. The condition permits loss. Some earlier images might disappear during inspection. An image that is nevertheless found cannot have arrived from an initial absence, if the condition holds.

Call the second condition **no loss**: across the relevant transition, a previously present F remains present. Given this condition, knowledge that F₁ is absent supports the inference that F₀ was absent. The condition permits gain. Inspection might generate images on some trials. But if an earlier image would have survived, its later absence rules out earlier presence.

In symbols, the positive inference uses F₁ → F₀. The negative inference uses F₀ → F₁, together with its contrapositive. Requiring both would amount to requiring preservation of whether F is present. Each inference needs only one direction.

These are sufficient conditions for the corresponding deductive moves, not a complete analysis of knowledge. They do not solve problems about luck, misleading evidence, or what justifies belief in a conditional. Nor are they necessary for every way of knowing the past. Someone might remember an earlier image directly even though that image has since disappeared. The conditions concern an inference whose stated experiential premise is the inspected feature's later presence or absence.

There is also a mundane qualification with philosophical importance. “No gain” must exclude relevant onset during the whole interval, not merely onset caused by introspection. An image could begin for another reason while inspection is taking place. “No loss” must likewise cover spontaneous disappearance. An investigator cannot justify an inference about the earlier interval merely by showing that the act of inspection itself was harmless.

Consider a possible observer whose quiet checking routine sometimes suppresses visual imagery but never initiates it under a specified set of circumstances. A surviving image supports earlier presence. The negative result does not support earlier absence in the same way: the routine might have removed what it sought. Now consider a different routine which sometimes starts imagery but never stops imagery already present. Its negative results support earlier absence; its positive results leave open whether the routine started the image.

These cases are deliberately conditional. I am not asserting that a familiar human technique satisfies either description. Their purpose is to test the inference that interference as such discredits a method. They refute that inference. They also show why describing a method simply as “reactive” leaves its evidential value unsettled.

The conditions are relative to a feature. Reflection might change an image's vividness while preserving its existence. It might preserve the existence of some imagery while replacing a particular imagined shape. We could therefore have good grounds for “I was already imagining something” but poor grounds for “I was already imagining a square.” As the historical claim becomes more specific, the relevant prohibition on gain becomes more demanding.

This point blocks an attractive shortcut. Suppose inspection reveals a vivid, articulated image, and the observer confidently takes herself to have discovered it. We cannot automatically project the inspected image's detail backwards. A process might preserve a faint image while supplying its detail. Earlier presence of an image and earlier presence of all its now apparent properties are different conclusions.

There is an equally important restriction on negative results. If the target feature is “imagery outside focal attention,” attending to it may remove that feature simply by changing its attentional status. A truthful judgment that no unattended image is now being inspected could then be useless about the earlier case. The problem cannot be repaired by being more careful with the same inference. The target property includes precisely what the procedure changes.

The wording of these conditions may seem to make the entire argument trivial. No gain just excludes one kind of historical mistake; no loss excludes the other. Why give ordinary logic a philosophical title?

Because discussions of introspection often bundle three matters together: whether an experience occurs, whether it is accurately recognised, and whether recognition provides evidence about the experience outside the recognising episode. The logical distinction shows that neither answering the second question favourably nor unfavourably settles the third. It also identifies a possibility easily obscured by talk of distortion: the observer may be entirely right about the experience she inspected, and entirely wrong about the history she constructs from it.

There are two independent places for a failure. The judgment might misdescribe the later state. Or it might accurately describe that state while being carried backwards through an unsuitable transition. Fixing the first failure need not fix the second. Training someone to notice smaller differences, use words more carefully, or express uncertainty more precisely may improve local accuracy without establishing either no gain or no loss.

This distinction also limits an argument for general introspective pessimism. Schwitzgebel's broader challenge concerns errors about current experience itself, including the relation between individually inspected visual regions and overall visual clarity. My argument does not refute that challenge. It shows why some failures involving reports over time would survive even if the challenge were answered in the optimist's favour. The optimist's best local case still needs a temporal argument. (Schwitzgebel 2008, author manuscript, §§vi and xiii, [text](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/Naive070907.htm).)

Suppose two accounts agree about everything the observer correctly identifies during inspection. On one, a faint auditory image existed earlier and attention made it salient. On the other, there was no auditory experience until attention intervened. Both accounts predict the same clear auditory image at the time of judgment. Greater confidence that the image is now present does not favour the first account over the second.

This is not because the report is false or worthless. It excludes accounts on which there is no later image. Its force is relative to the alternatives being compared. If two hypotheses assign the same probability to the available report, learning the report does not change their probability ratio. It may strongly support something both hypotheses share.

The point has a close antecedent in Phillips's treatment of consciousness research. He argues that an account of information available after a cue requires further assumptions before it establishes the consciousness of the earlier representation. He also stresses that competing interpretations can challenge arguments in both directions. I do not offer the symmetry of this predicament as a new discovery. My narrower proposal concerns which changes defeat which temporal inferences even after a later experiential judgment is granted. (Phillips 2018, §§1, 3–4, [text](https://www.ianbphillips.com/files/papers/the-methodological-puzzle.pdf).)

A reasonable objection is that almost any inference can be defeated by inventing a sufficiently ingenious alternative. Perhaps the image began during inspection; perhaps a hidden machine created an apparent memory; perhaps the whole world began a moment ago. Merely mentioning alternatives should not destroy knowledge.

Agreed. The argument establishes no universal requirement to eliminate every possible history. To challenge an actual inference, the alternative transition must deserve consideration in the context. A bare logical possibility shows only that present accuracy does not entail historical accuracy. An epistemic objection needs more: evidence that the procedure can generate the feature, a serious theory on which it does, or some other reason to doubt the relevant bridge.

This matters in debates about whether attention is necessary for a particular kind of experience. There the possibility that attending brings the experience into existence is not an unrelated sceptical fantasy. It is part of the dispute. One cannot settle that dispute by presuming, without further grounds, that an experience found through attention could not have been generated by it. Yet outside such a dispute, a stable, familiar course of experience may provide perfectly adequate grounds for a modest retrospective judgment.

The observer may now reply that the report leaves something out. She does not merely experience a tone. She experiences it as continuing. Surely the feeling of continuation supplies what the bare current tone lacked.

It supplies additional evidence, which must be assessed. An account predicting only an abrupt new image may now fare worse. But two claims must still be kept apart: that the current episode has a character of continuation, and that this character accurately reveals an earlier episode. Confidence about the first is not simply reusable as confidence about the second.

This is no reason to treat the feeling of continuation as generally deceptive. It is a reason to recognise what an argument now depends on. If such a feeling reliably depends on an earlier experience, it can carry information across the transition even when the earlier feature has vanished. The pertinent condition then concerns the relation between the earlier feature and a later retained presentation, not the continued existence of the original feature.

Nor must temporal knowledge consist of a conscious inference from a time-slice of experience. Perhaps the observer is directly aware of a temporally extended episode, including its earlier phase. Phillips develops an account on which extended periods of experience are fundamental relative to their instantaneous parts. If an accurate judgment already concerns such an extended episode, there may be no inference from a later slice to an earlier one to scrutinise. (Phillips 2011, §4.3, pp. 397–399, [text](https://www.ianbphillips.com/files/papers/perception-and-iconic-memory.pdf).)

My notation does not decide between competing accounts of temporal experience. The intervals can be long enough to avoid assuming that experience consists of instantaneous atoms. What matters is whether the warrant granted in the argument covers the disputed earlier phase. If it does, the historical claim has already entered the premise. If it does not, invoking temporal unity cannot substitute for explaining the warrant's reach.

Retrospection therefore offers a possible solution, but changing the grammatical tense is not itself a solution. “I was imagining a flute” can be a reliable memory, a reconstruction from the current image, or a mixture. The conditions for using a memory as evidence need not be available to the observer as an explicit theory. Nevertheless, a philosophical argument that relies on the report must distinguish those routes when they make different predictions about the question at issue.

This also shows why literal preservation of experience is not the general ideal. A useful process may replace an unstable experience with a stable record. The original can be destroyed while information about it survives. Conversely, the sensory feature can continue unchanged while information about its earlier occurrence is lost. Preservation of an object and preservation of evidence about that object are different achievements.

The problem becomes sharper when we try to describe the whole field of experience by inspecting its parts. Imagine an observer successively checking for an inner voice, visual imagery, and bodily sensations. Suppose every local judgment is correct. That does not establish that everything found was present together before the search.

A search could introduce a feature whenever that feature becomes its target. It could also remove an earlier feature as attention moves on. The observer would then assemble a description of a rich simultaneous experience out of accurately reported episodes which were never simultaneous. There need be no mistake in any individual report of what was then occurring.

A negative inventory can fail in a matching but less obvious way. Consider two possible features, an imagined tone and an imagined shape. Both are initially present. Inspection of the tone removes it while the shape remains. By the time the observer inspects the shape, the tone has returned and the shape disappears. She truthfully reports the absence of the tone on the first inspection and of the shape on the second.

Neither result was false. Yet neither feature was absent before the search, and there was no point during these two inspections when both were absent. A supposed discovery of mental emptiness could therefore be composed entirely of true local absence judgments. This is a countermodel, not evidence that actual reports of emptiness are generally produced that way.

The lesson is not that negative introspection always fails. It is that successful local inspection and a successful inventory have different requirements. For a positive inventory of an earlier interval, no gain must hold for each attributed feature across its respective interval of inspection. For a negative inventory, no loss must hold for each excluded feature. Alternatively, a reliable record could support both kinds of attribution. Merely accumulating confident local reports does not provide these conditions.

A description of experience over a period can legitimately include changing features. The error arises only when results about different times are combined into a claim about one time, or about a fixed earlier interval. Keeping the time index visible is not pedantry. It determines whether there is a disagreement at all.

For example, two observers might use different reflective routines and accurately describe different experiences. One finds articulate imagery; the other finds none. If their claims concern the states produced during their respective routines, both can be right. A conflict arises when both descriptions are projected onto the same kind of uninspected episode. Before diagnosing an error of introspection, we should identify which temporal projection each person is making.

This is especially important for a proposed hierarchy between ordinary and trained introspectors. A trained observer may reliably discriminate the fine structure of the experience available under a trained routine. That is an achievement. It does not by itself establish what the novice experienced before adopting that routine, or what the trained observer experiences outside it. Training may improve discrimination, alter experience, or do both.

The distinction does not vindicate an untrained observer against an expert. It determines what the expert has demonstrated. Evidence of improved discrimination supports confidence in judgments within the tested setting. Generalisation to a different setting requires grounds for believing that the relevant experiential features, or informative traces of them, survive the change. An expert can supply such grounds; expertise cannot replace them merely by being expertise.

How could the needed grounds be obtained without circularity? We often learn what checking does by checking. If all access to the earlier experience changes it, how can we discover whether a method introduces or removes the feature?

There is no guarantee of an easy answer. A transition condition cannot be established solely by repeating the very inference for which it is needed. Finding imagery after inspection a hundred times does not show that inspection never initiated imagery. If the challenge concerns creation, greater consistency in the later observations may simply show more consistent creation.

Still, calibration need not start from a complete, independently certified inventory of consciousness. An observer might compare routines in circumstances where an earlier episode is independently well supported by an ongoing task, a retained experience, and previous reports. Varying the routine could expose losses of detail or the addition of features suggested by instructions. Such evidence can support restricted conclusions about specified methods and features.

These supports are themselves defeasible. A previous report may already have changed the experience. Task performance may be possible without the disputed conscious feature. A retained presentation may be reconstructed. Accordingly, agreement among measures is useful only when their errors are not all explained by the same disputed assumption. The point of using several routes is to obtain different constraints, not simply more copies of one report.

Most importantly, calibration in cases of attended imagery does not automatically establish preservation in cases of wholly unattended imagery. That would generalise across precisely the difference whose significance is in question. A successful test in the first setting can provide some reason to investigate the second; it cannot, without an argument about the transfer, settle the second.

This limitation is a result, not a reason to abandon the conditions. Where the question is whether experience exists outside attention, it may remain difficult to establish the relevant transition independently of a substantive theory. We should then say that the inspected experience does not settle the dispute by itself. That is more precise than saying that the observer knows nothing about her mind or that introspective evidence has no value.

There is no requirement here that ordinary people formulate transition principles before remembering breakfast or reporting a lingering headache. Knowledge need not await a theory of the method producing it. The requirement concerns the scope claimed for evidence, especially when that scope is challenged. One can possess grounds implicitly and normally rely on them. When a philosophical dispute turns on whether inspection creates or removes its object, those grounds become part of what needs defence.

The resulting position leaves room for considerable first-person authority. An observer may be best placed to say what the inspected experience is like. Her report may deserve trust, including when its content is unfamiliar or difficult to describe. What does not follow is that her authority expands automatically to the experience before inspection, to all unattended experience, or to a simultaneous totality assembled from successive observations.

It also leaves room for informative interference. A destructive inspection can preserve evidence of prior presence. A productive inspection can preserve evidence of prior absence. Neither finding makes the method universally trustworthy. Each shows that trust belongs to a specified inference about a specified feature across a specified transition.

The familiar question, “Does introspection change experience?”, should therefore be followed by a more exact one: “Which historical inference would this change invalidate?” Answering that second question allows us to respect truthful reports while resisting unsupported histories. We may know the experience we have changed without thereby knowing how it was before the change. Sometimes we can know that too—but the direction of the inference matters.
