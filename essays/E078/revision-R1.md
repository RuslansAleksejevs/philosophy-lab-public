# Revocable Consent and Responsibility for Preserving Choice

Rowan lends Ellis a spare pump to supply a small settlement with water. The loan is expressly terminable on a day's notice. Rowan makes no promise to continue it. Ellis has a reserve pump that could take over within that day; maintaining it costs little. Instead, Ellis sells the reserve to fund another useful but nonurgent project. A replacement would take two weeks to obtain. If Rowan requests the loaned pump's return, the residents will face a serious shortage unless the loan continues.

Suppose an extension costs Rowan little, while a shortage would gravely harm innocent residents. Rowan may now have a duty to extend the loan. Yet Ellis may have wronged Rowan by creating this predicament. Ellis need not have intended to constrain Rowan, denied the effectiveness of revocation, or caused an actual shortage. The question concerns an earlier organizational decision: whether Ellis could fairly obtain the benefit of selling the reserve by making Rowan responsible for continuity.

I defend a limited duty to preserve significant opportunities for withdrawal. Its basis is neither the bare word “revocable” nor an entitlement to remain free of new obligations. It rests on two considerations: people have reason to retain authority to revise their contributions, and an organizer should ordinarily bear modest costs of discretionary changes that would otherwise enlarge someone else's responsibility. Both considerations admit exceptions. Their conjunction explains why a precaution can be owed before anyone wants to leave, why reimbursement sometimes leaves something unrepaired, and why preserving exit can nevertheless demand too much.

The difficult case is continued willing help with ordinary costs reimbursed. There, it is insufficient simply to repeat that Rowan retained a choice. We need to explain what that choice protects, establish a baseline without assuming the disputed duty, and identify a practical consequence beyond condemning dependence in general.

## 1. Revocation, permissibility, and available exit

Three forms of control must be distinguished. A person may retain the normative power to withdraw permission, the moral liberty to secure cessation, and the practical means to do so. These can come apart.

Healey distinguishes the directed permission supplied by consent from overall permissibility: removing one duty can leave independent duties intact (2019, pp. 503–504). Dougherty distinguishes the priority of present dissent over earlier consent from independent ethical reasons, and allows an effective revocation to breach a promise not to revoke (2014, pp. 27, 33–34, 38–39). These distinctions make room for Rowan's predicament. Withdrawal can end the original permission even if an emergency requires Rowan to permit continued use, or independently justifies Ellis's temporary retention. The argument here does not decide between those analyses.

Call an exit *morally available* when securing cessation would not violate an undefeated duty. This describes an option; it does not establish an entitlement to have it. Nor does a morally available exit guarantee practical access to the equipment.

The pump case concerns a change from a one-day transition to two weeks of morally necessary assistance. Three questions remain separate: how stringent the duty to assist is, how burdensome compliance is, and how significant the blocked decision is. A stringent rescue duty may be cheap to discharge. A cheap duty may still restrict an important decision. Conversely, a momentary restriction on an insignificant option need not warrant costly prevention.

The residents' claims survive Ellis's misconduct. Any account that treats the prior wrong as permission to expose them to a grave shortage has misunderstood the problem. The issue is how to protect them while fairly assigning responsibility for the dependence.

## 2. Why retain a choice one willingly leaves unused?

Suppose Rowan wants the loan to continue and Ellis reimburses maintenance, wear, and every ordinary inconvenience of the extension. Why spend anything on restoring an option Rowan does not intend to exercise?

One answer concerns revision under uncertainty. Rowan's plans, needs, and judgments can change. Retaining a usable exit allows Rowan to respond without having to choose between abandoning a new purpose and failing the residents. Reimbursement for continued lending does not necessarily provide access to the pump for another purpose. Even when foreseeable expenses are covered, a payment cannot specify in advance every reason Rowan may later have for wanting the contribution to end.

This is a reason for preserving flexibility, but it has limits. A very unlikely change in a trivial preference cannot justify maintaining an expensive duplicate system. The value of the option depends partly on what decisions it enables, for how long, and under what uncertainty. It cannot be inferred from the strength of the rescue duty alone.

A second answer concerns authority over the scope of a contribution. Consider two arrangements with the same present use and the same payment. In one, continued use depends on Rowan's continuing decision, subject to an agreed transition. In the other, Ellis has arranged matters so that refusing continued use would wrong the residents. Rowan currently welcomes both outcomes. Nevertheless, Ellis has changed who determines the extent of Rowan's responsibility. Rowan's present willingness is now doing less work: even a revised judgment would not suffice to release Rowan.

Why should this difference count? People have reason to distinguish the assistance they undertake from the assistance others can foreseeably make it their duty to provide. Without some protection for that distinction, accepting a limited role exposes a person to expansions chosen by whoever controls the surrounding project. The person's capacity to arrange several commitments is then partly governed by another's decisions about which safeguards to retain. A communicated limit provides a way to cooperate while allocating this authority. Defeating it for an additional discretionary benefit can deny the contributor an appropriate role in determining what they undertake.

This is a claim about fair terms of cooperation, not a conclusion extracted from the definition of consent. It gives Rowan a reason to object even while welcoming the actual assistance. Approval of an outcome does not necessarily authorize another person to decide that the contribution must continue. Equally, a person's authority over a contribution is not unlimited: the needs of beneficiaries and legitimate reliance can outweigh it.

The argument should not be stretched to make every unused option inviolable. Imagine that Rowan's preference cannot change during a brief extension, every relevant practical interest is secured, and Rowan attaches no significance to continuing personal control. The planning argument then disappears, and the remaining complaint concerns the unilateral alteration of the arrangement. That complaint may support disclosure or acknowledgment without supporting expenditure on redundant equipment. A standing interest in determining one's responsibilities need not give every particular decision substantial weight.

Thus continued willingness and reimbursement do not automatically eliminate the reason to preserve exit. They can, however, greatly reduce what preservation reasonably requires. The stronger claim—that an unchosen restriction always warrants physical restoration regardless of its significance—is indefensible.

## 3. A baseline that does not assume the conclusion

It would be circular to compare Ellis's conduct with “responsible reliance” if responsible reliance just means reliance that preserves Rowan's exit. Instead, the assessment should begin with independently describable facts about the arrangement and its feasible alternatives.

First, identify the contribution actually offered. Relevant evidence includes express terms, assurances, the disclosed purpose, and preparations Rowan invited or encouraged. A one-day notice term is strong evidence against a commitment to indefinite continuity. It is not a waiver of the residents' claims or a guarantee that withdrawal will always be permissible.

Second, identify the dependence required by the disclosed activity under the circumstances in which it was undertaken. Starting a water supply can permissibly involve installation, ordinary use, and a safe transition. These consequences cannot be excluded merely because Rowan later dislikes them. In the stated case, however, supplying water does not require selling the reserve: the existing equipment permits both the authorized use and a one-day transition.

Third, compare feasible arrangements at the point when Ellis chooses whether to sell. Keeping the reserve, securing a dependable substitute, negotiating a longer commitment, and arranging a safe end to the loan are possible kinds of alternative. Their availability must be established in the particular case; they cannot simply be stipulated into every dispute. Comparison must include their burdens on Ellis and the residents as well as their effects on Rowan.

These steps yield a factual starting point: continuing the existing loan with the existing reserve would preserve water service and the stated transition period at a small maintenance cost. Selling it finances another project while introducing a longer dependence. This comparison does not yet prove the sale wrongful. It identifies the benefit obtained and the responsibility shifted, so that their relative importance can be assessed.

The normative judgment follows from the competing interests. Rowan has a claim against having a limited contribution enlarged through someone else's discretionary project decision. Ellis has claims concerning the use of Ellis's property, the burden of maintaining backup capacity, and the benefits of the proposed project. The residents have strong interests in reliable water. Where a modest precaution protects both water provision and Rowan's significant opportunity to revise the contribution, a merely discretionary improvement normally gives Ellis insufficient reason to dispense with it.

“Nonurgent” does not mean unimportant. A nonurgent project might prevent substantial future harm or serve people with serious unmet needs. The description therefore supports a presumption against the sale, rather than an unconditional verdict. Its strength depends on the foregone project's value and on the real cost of alternatives. The fact that Ellis controls the reserve identifies who can prevent the dependence; it does not by itself determine who should pay.

This baseline avoids two distortions. It does not compare the loan with a world in which Rowan never helped and nobody relied. Nor does it permit Ellis's successful exploitation of generosity to establish what reliance is reasonable. Rowan's predictable kindness is evidence about likely behavior, not authorization to increase Rowan's responsibilities.

Some cases remain indeterminate. If the parties knew from the outset that switching pumps took two weeks, a one-day term would not settle a fair transition by itself. If Rowan encouraged the sale, Ellis's reliance would have a stronger basis. If initial expectations were ambiguous, the parties might owe clarification and a fair allocation of transition costs rather than strict enforcement of either party's preferred baseline. The principle offers no mechanical resolution of such conflicts.

## 4. The recipient's interests and the limits of precaution

The proposed duty can now be stated:

**When organizing an activity under limited permission, a recipient presumptively owes feasible, proportionate precautions against discretionary changes that foreseeably obstruct a significant opportunity for the giver to end the contribution. The assessment must include invited reliance, the recipient's important interests, and the claims of affected third parties.**

The presumption is strongest when the recipient controls a cheap safeguard, the proposed change serves an additional optional benefit, and the giver has neither authorized nor encouraged the added dependence. It weakens when any of these conditions fails.

A crucial limit concerns the recipient's own security and independence. Suppose Ellis must sell the reserve to obtain necessary medical care, preserve basic housing, or escape a seriously harmful situation. Even if Rowan's notice term was clear, Ellis's important interests can outweigh Rowan's interest in a short period of flexibility. The permission relationship does not give Rowan authority to require that Ellis maintain it through severe personal sacrifice. A narrowly bounded extension with reimbursement might then be the fairest available arrangement.

This limit is not confined to emergencies. A supposed opportunity to “end the project instead” may carry serious losses of livelihood or of investments Rowan encouraged. Such alternatives remain relevant, but their being physically possible does not make them reasonable demands. Nor is an explicit exit term automatically fair if its operation would place substantial, foreseeable burdens on the recipient that the giver invited the recipient to incur.

There is also a limit concerning the source of dependence. If the reserve fails despite appropriate maintenance, Ellis has not committed the discretionary act described in the opening case. Ellis may still owe transition efforts because Ellis manages the supply, but liability for a wrongful sale does not transfer automatically to unavoidable failure. Similarly, a general duty Rowan would have faced regardless of this arrangement cannot be charged wholly to Ellis. The relevant question is what Ellis's choice added to the predicament.

Finally, precaution is not synonymous with permanent duplication. A reliable substitute available within the notice period may preserve the relevant option as effectively as a reserve owned by Ellis. Transition funds suffice only if they can actually secure timely replacement. A promise to reimburse Rowan after an extension does something different: it covers burdens without necessarily preserving the decision.

The duty therefore concerns adequate means at reasonable cost, not a particular piece of equipment. Where several arrangements protect the relevant interests, Rowan cannot demand the most expensive one merely because it offers greater independence.

## 5. What the account adds to the assessment

The coexistence of a genuine obligation and a complaint about its creation is familiar. Keller's discussion of moral blackmail concerns wrongful manipulation that can produce real obligations, including through vulnerable beneficiaries (2018, “Moral Blackmail” and “Blackmailing a Fiduciary”). Spafford's moral tyranny constraint addresses foreseeable, discretionary disadvantage under full moral compliance, includes negligent conduct, and allows a rescue duty to coexist with a compensation claim against whoever created the need (2023, §§2.1–2.2, 2.5 and n. 25). Nothing here establishes a new general category of wrong.

Nor does the argument vindicate an unrestricted prohibition on shifting costs. O'Brien's distinction between wanton cost shifting and costly self-protection gives a reason to reject that extension (2026, §§II–III). The recipient's important interests must enter the assessment before a precaution is declared owed.

The more specific contribution is a set of judgments about the organization of a revocable arrangement.

First, the object of assessment is the change in dependence. If Ellis sells the reserve without an adequate alternative, a complaint can arise then, before revocation and even if Rowan never seeks return. At that point the terms on which Rowan could revise the contribution have changed. Whether the complaint is substantial depends on the option's significance; an unrealized possibility of withdrawal does not establish substantial damages by itself.

Second, reasonable care can suffice to ground the duty. Ellis's optimism about Rowan's generosity does not answer whether a modest precaution was owed. This is not a novel discovery about negligence. Its application matters because a recipient who honors every actual request for renewed consent might nevertheless organize the interaction unfairly.

Third, the appropriate response need not be compensation alone. Liberto's account permits exploitative promissory release to be effective, while requiring that the sought release actually be obtained (2016, pp. 149–150, 160–162). Here there need be no release or renewal at all. The relevant repair can concern the conditions under which a future decision will occur.

Finally, consent supplies specificity without supplying the whole moral foundation. If Ellis owned the operating pump, sold it, and made Rowan the only available rescuer, Rowan could have a similar complaint. The loan need not add a separate wrong. It identifies a contribution, a transition period, and invited reliance against which responsibility can be assessed. These details can determine what precaution was due and what repair would restore.

## 6. Reimbursement, restoration, and timing

Suppose Ellis has sold the reserve, Rowan remains willing to lend, and Ellis covers all ordinary costs. Three responses must be distinguished: reimbursing the extension, restoring a feasible exit, and obtaining informed agreement to a revised allocation.

Reimbursement addresses expenses and inconvenience. Restoration makes the relevant decision available again. Agreement can authorize a longer commitment or a different transition. Depending on the case, one response may make another unnecessary. None automatically performs all three functions.

Why prefer restoration when help continues willingly? Where Rowan has a significant interest in being able to revise the contribution, and a modest precaution would protect it, Ellis's reimbursement leaves that interest unaddressed. The complaint does not require Rowan to invent a present desire to stop. Requiring such a declaration would make protection depend on initiating a conflict, perhaps only after the option had become costly to recover.

But this supports only proportionate restoration. If a dependable replacement service can guarantee transfer within a day, Ellis need not purchase a second pump. If Rowan knowingly accepts two weeks of commitment in exchange for compensation, the prospective duty to preserve a one-day exit can cease. And if the remaining restriction is brief, insignificant, and expensive to remove, reimbursement and a scheduled transition may be sufficient even without Rowan's preferred form of repair.

Consent to new terms should ordinarily be sought before the recipient removes the safeguard, while refusal can still leave the existing arrangement intact. After the sale, Rowan's renewed “yes” may genuinely authorize continued use without approving the earlier decision or waiving a claim concerning it. The emergency does not automatically invalidate later consent. It does mean that agreement to help cannot simply be read as retrospective authorization of the dependence.

Now suppose Rowan requests return. Under the opening assumptions, Rowan should permit temporary use to prevent the shortage. Ellis should pursue a replacement and bear the reasonable costs attributable to the wrongful change. These may include replacement and transition expenses as well as Rowan's ordinary costs. Their precise allocation remains sensitive to any reliance Rowan invited and any contribution Rowan made to the predicament.

The repair timetable follows the preventable restriction, not simply the date of Rowan's complaint. If the sale wrongfully obstructed a significant option, Ellis has reason to begin restoring it when that fact becomes apparent, even while Rowan welcomes continued lending. Waiting for withdrawal can needlessly extend the period in which Rowan must continue. Yet this does not imply obtaining a substitute at any price: haste must be weighed against the remaining importance of the option, the costs of acceleration, and residents' safety.

Goodin's chapter abstract recognizes reasonable detrimental reliance as a pro tanto reason against revocation and for compensation, and uncompensatable reliance as a pro tanto reason for irrevocability (2024). Those possibilities remain open here. Rowan may owe notice, compensation for invited expenses, or a safe transition. As Rouméas distinguishes, substantive opportunities to exit and fair allocation of exit costs are separate questions (2023, “Exit ought to be fair”). Preserving Rowan's option does not settle every question of who pays.

The resulting verdict is deliberately conditional but consequential. For a significant retained decision, cheap backup, and no sufficiently weighty competing benefit, Ellis should secure a workable transition before selling. Following a wrongful sale, repair can be due before Rowan objects; payment for ongoing help may be insufficient; and restoring the option need not require ending the loan. Where the option has little value or preservation threatens the recipient's important interests, the required response can instead be a bounded transition or revised arrangement.

Revocable permission cannot insulate people from every duty arising through cooperation. It can nevertheless help define a limited contribution whose expansion calls for justification. Respect for that contribution requires attention to who controls the surrounding dependence, what a feasible alternative costs, and whose important interests it serves. The duty to preserve exit is strongest where a modest organizational precaution permits valuable cooperation without letting one participant unilaterally enlarge another's responsibility.
