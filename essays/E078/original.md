# Revocable Consent and the Duty to Preserve an Exit

Rowan lends Ellis a spare pump to supply a small settlement with water. The loan is expressly terminable on a day's notice. Rowan makes no promise to continue it. Ellis has a reserve pump that could take over within that day; maintaining it costs little. Instead, Ellis sells the reserve to fund another useful but nonurgent project. A replacement would take two weeks to obtain. If Rowan asks for the loaned pump back, the residents will face a serious shortage unless the loan continues.

Suppose a short extension would cost Rowan little, while an immediate shortage would gravely harm innocent residents. Rowan may now have a duty to extend the loan. Ellis need not deny that revocation ends the original permission. Nor need Ellis have sold the reserve in order to constrain Rowan. Ellis may simply have expected continued generosity. Nevertheless, Ellis has foreseeably changed the terms on which Rowan can obtain the pump's return: a decision that was to remain Rowan's now requires either two weeks' delay or a serious wrong to others.

I argue that Ellis presumptively owed Rowan a reasonable precaution against this change. The wrong is an avoidable transfer of responsibility for continuity, produced through an undertaking based on explicitly limited permission. It is not the creation of just any new duty, and its explanation need not be unique to consent. The permission relationship specifies what was undertaken, which reliance needs justification, and what an appropriate repair would restore.

The contribution is therefore a restricted account of the care owed to another person's retained option. It connects consent and fair exit with familiar objections to making others bear the costs of one's decisions. Its practical consequence is easy to miss: valid revocation, an absence of manipulative intent, and genuine duties to innocent beneficiaries can all coexist with a prior wrong to the consent giver. Recognizing consent's validity is only part of respecting it.

## 1. Three different things a person can retain

Rowan can retain a power to end permission without retaining a liberty to make the activity stop immediately. A third possibility is that Rowan retains both, but has no effective means to secure cessation. These are different failures or limitations of control.

Healey distinguishes the directed permission that consent supplies from overall permissibility. Consent may remove one duty while leaving independent duties, including duties to the same person, untouched (2019, pp. 503–504). Dougherty similarly distinguishes the priority of present dissent over earlier consent from the weight of independent ethical reasons. He also allows consent to be combined with a promise not to revoke: breaking that promise need not make the subsequent dissent ineffective (2014, pp. 27, 33–34, 38–39).

Applied to the pump, the original permission may end while an emergency gives Rowan a new duty to permit temporary use. Alternatively, the emergency may justify Ellis's temporary retention despite the withdrawal. The choice between these analyses does not decide my argument. In either, Rowan's revocation need not be invalid, yet Rowan cannot permissibly obtain immediate cessation. Call an exit *morally available* when securing cessation would not violate an undefeated duty. This describes an option; it does not itself establish anyone's entitlement to possess it.

Three further distinctions matter. The **strength of the duty** concerns the reasons against refusing to continue. The **cost of complying** concerns what continuation demands of Rowan. The **significance of the blocked option** concerns the range and duration of decisions Rowan can no longer permissibly make. A rescue duty can be stringent but cheap to fulfill. Neither fact alone tells us how significant a loss of control has occurred.

The pump case combines a weighty reason to prevent serious harm, a low immediate cost of helping, and the replacement of a one-day exit by a two-week dependence. The last change matters because control over the ongoing use of one's resource is a meaningful planning option, even when one currently wishes to keep lending. A two-minute safe shutdown would normally be different. So would a barely foreseeable inconvenience in an option of negligible importance. The account protects significant control against unreasonable loss; it does not make every new obligation an injury.

Nothing here gives Rowan sovereignty over the residents' needs. Once they depend on the pump, their claims must be assessed. The question is whether Ellis should have preserved an affordable way to meet those claims without making Rowan responsible for continued support.

## 2. Whose undertaking, whose precaution?

The argument begins with responsibility for a project, rather than with the bare meaning of revocation. Ellis organizes the water supply, knows the loan's limit, controls the reserve, and chooses where the proceeds of its sale will go. Maintaining the reserve would permit both the authorized project and Rowan's timely exit. Selling it obtains an additional benefit for a different project by leaving Rowan to supply continuity.

Why should the precaution fall on Ellis? Consider the competing claims. Ellis asks to redirect a resource while continuing to use Rowan's equipment for an undertaking whose needs Ellis manages. Rowan asks that an explicitly limited contribution not be treated as a commitment to cover the consequences of that redirection. A modest precaution would honor the limit without stopping the authorized undertaking. In these circumstances, the claim to discretionary improvement does not presumptively outweigh the claim against having responsibility enlarged. Ellis should seek different terms, arrange a substitute, or end the loan safely before selling the reserve.

This is a substantive allocation judgment. It is not entailed by the absence of a promise from Rowan. Nor does a one-day notice clause expressly promise Rowan freedom from every independent duty. The justification lies in the conjunction of a communicated limit, discretionary project control, a reasonably preventable loss of a meaningful option, and no sufficiently strong reason for shifting that loss. The recipient's control over the precaution explains whom to address; the giver's retained option explains what must be protected; the relative claims explain why the precaution is presumptively due.

Ellis can object that the reserve belongs to Ellis. That objection has force: ownership is one reason not to demand unlimited backup capacity. But ownership does not by itself settle how one may organize an undertaking around somebody else's limited contribution. The proposed duty does not arise from merely possessing a useful pump. Ellis remains free to redirect it after a safe transition, to negotiate a different loan, or to discontinue the undertaking within the constraints owed to its beneficiaries. What is disputed is the combination of taking the new benefit and making another person cover a foreseeable continuity problem that a modest precaution would avoid.

Now remove the original loan. Suppose Ellis supplies the settlement entirely with Ellis's equipment, sells it for the same nonurgent benefit, and knows that Rowan is the only nearby owner who can avert the resulting shortage at little cost. Rowan may have a complaint here too. The foreseeable emergency has been imposed without any prior permission. This comparison shows that the complaint in the loan case cannot rest entirely on having been a consent giver. I do not claim that adding the loan necessarily creates a second wrong or makes an otherwise identical wrong more serious.

What, then, does consent contribute? It makes a particular allocation available for assessment. Rowan allowed this continuing use while declining a commitment to its continuation; Ellis chose to organize a project on that basis. The relationship identifies both the reliance that was invited and the decision Ellis must take care not to obstruct. In the nearby-owner case, the appropriate precaution prevents an uninvited rescue predicament. In the loan case, it also preserves the operating terms of an existing interaction. A general duty can acquire a determinate object through a particular transaction without gaining an entirely new foundation.

This matters because ordinary descriptions of consent can conceal that object. “Ellis always stops relying on the old permission when Rowan withdraws it” sounds sufficient until one asks how Ellis has arranged the costs of actually stopping. The transaction supplies a reason to scrutinize that arrangement before revocation. It does not follow that every consent recipient must maintain a substitute for every eventuality.

The residents' welfare does not exhaust the issue. Suppose Rowan will certainly keep the pump available, so selling the reserve creates no increased risk of an actual shortage. Ellis still chooses who must secure that favorable outcome. Keeping the reserve makes it the project's responsibility; selling it makes it depend on Rowan's inability permissibly to end the use. Rowan might even welcome the second project. An improvement Rowan likes is not automatically authorization to turn a retained option into a resource at Ellis's disposal.

There is, however, no duty to preserve an option simply because its holder describes it as retained. If the only feasible way to conduct the disclosed activity includes a longer transition, or if Rowan encouraged the relevant dependence, the allocation changes. The case for Ellis's duty requires assessing the actual terms and alternatives, not awarding decisive weight to the word “revocable.”

## 3. The connection with created obligations

The combination of a present obligation and a justified complaint about its creation is familiar. Keller's discussion of moral blackmail explains how another person's wrongful manipulation can produce real obligations, including obligations arising through relationships with vulnerable beneficiaries (2018, “Moral Blackmail” and “Blackmailing a Fiduciary”). In the malicious pump case, Ellis sells the reserve precisely to constrain Rowan. The case of heedless optimism adds a different point: preventing such a predicament can be a matter of reasonable care even without that intention.

That point should not be advertised as a new general discovery. Spafford's moral tyranny constraint already addresses foreseeable, discretionary, unilateral disadvantage under full moral compliance, with an alternative-choice baseline. His discussion includes negligence and allows a rescue duty to coexist with a claim to compensation against the person who created the need. The currency of advantage is left open, rather than restricted to money or welfare (2023, §§2.1–2.2, 2.5 and n. 25). The pump argument shares this concern. It does not derive a general constraint on acceptable moral theories, or assume that compensation must make every innocent rescuer no worse off. Instead, it asks what precaution and remedy are warranted in a specified revocable arrangement.

This restriction is deliberate. O'Brien distinguishes plausible objections to wanton cost shifting from a wider principle that would also constrain costly self-protection. His boulder example challenges the latter extension (2026, §§II–III). One need not settle that dispute to judge the reserve's inexpensive preservation reasonable. A permissible alternative that demands extreme sacrifice cannot establish the same duty merely because it is available. The seriousness of the organizer's reasons and the cost of the precaution belong within the assessment.

Liberto supplies another close comparison. Her account allows exploitative promissory release to be effective: the residual wrong need not be promise-breaking. Her discussion also requires that the sought release actually be obtained (2016, pp. 149–150, 160–162). Here, by contrast, Rowan need not have been asked to renew permission or release a claim. The loss of a meaningful exit can already occur while the loan continues willingly. The prospective duty concerns the arrangement of dependence, not only a later exercise of a normative power.

These connections limit and clarify the contribution. The argument identifies neither a wholly new category of wrong nor a universal principle governing all created duties. It specifies a protection for revocable arrangements: reasonable care for the giver's practical opportunity to make the decision that was left open. Its importance appears in the baseline, timing, and remedy, not in labeling all new obligations illegitimate.

## 4. A principle with a baseline

The resulting principle is this:

**A recipient organizing a continuing activity under revocable permission presumptively owes its giver reasonable precautions against discretionary changes that foreseeably and significantly reduce the giver's morally available opportunity to end that activity, beyond the dependence properly included in the authorized arrangement. Further authorization or sufficient independent reasons can defeat that presumption.**

Four features prevent this from becoming a general entitlement to costless withdrawal.

First, the relevant option must have been left open. A gift, fixed-term commitment, promise not to revoke, or fairly agreed notice period can allocate responsibility differently. Dougherty's discussion of contractual reliance is a reason to take such differences seriously, not to treat every permission as a freely terminable loan (2014, pp. 38–39). There can also be reasons, independent of the recipient's conduct, why the original arrangement ought not to permit unrestricted withdrawal. The principle does not settle those questions by definition.

Second, the comparison is with the authorized activity conducted responsibly, not with a world in which no one ever depended on Rowan. Loaning equipment ordinarily permits preparations, use, and a safe shutdown. What was disclosed, what was assured, and what was reasonably necessary to perform the activity are all relevant. In the stated case, the existing reserve lets Ellis do exactly what Rowan allowed and still return the pump within a day. Its sale introduces an additional obstacle. If switching pumps actually takes two weeks, that is a different case: the difficulty belongs to the initial transition, not to the later sale.

The baseline cannot simply incorporate whatever dependence Ellis could predict Rowan would tolerate. Otherwise, a habit of exploiting generosity would justify itself. Nor can it exclude every consequence Rowan dislikes. Where an ordinarily necessary form of reliance was invited, Rowan cannot retroactively redescribe the permission as authorizing a frictionless exit. The baseline must be justified by both parties' reasonable claims.

Third, the reduction must be significant and the precaution reasonable. Relevant considerations include the option's importance, how long it will be blocked, the probability that the recipient's choice will obstruct it, the cost of prevention, and the recipient's reasons for acting. Rowan's present desire to continue does not reduce the option's value to zero: retaining a decision includes being able to change one's mind. But a remote chance of a negligible interruption does not justify maintaining an expensive duplicate system. The strength of the duty to help residents after an emergency is therefore not a sufficient measure of what Ellis must spend beforehand.

Fourth, authorization and independent justification remain possible. Rowan may knowingly agree to sell the reserve and accept the resulting exposure. Alternatively, selling it may be the only way to prevent an imminent catastrophe, while Rowan faces only a slight, temporary restriction. The presumption can then be defeated. Lesser benefits can also count, although the mere existence of a useful alternative project is not decisive. The argument assigns weight to retained control; it does not make it absolute.

Authorization must be sought while rejecting the proposal remains a meaningful option. Informing Rowan after the reserve has been sold does not by itself authorize the sale. Nor is a renewed “yes” decisive evidence about the earlier choice if the avoidable emergency already dictates Rowan's response. This does not make all later consent invalid. It distinguishes permission for future conduct from approval of how the predicament arose.

Reasonable precautions need not take the form of repeated requests for consent. They can include preserving the substitute, reserving transition funds, obtaining a bounded commitment before expanding the activity, or limiting the project to a scale compatible with the giver's exit. Which precaution is appropriate depends on the relevant allocation, rather than on the ritual of asking again.

## 5. Reliance and compensation

A serious objection is that useful permission normally invites reliance. If Rowan can insist that Ellis retain a costly escape route at every moment, lending might become pointless. Why should the recipient finance the giver's flexibility?

The answer cannot be that all reliance was unauthorized. Goodin's chapter abstract identifies reasonable detrimental reliance as a pro tanto reason against revocation and for compensation; it also treats uncompensatable reliance as a pro tanto reason for irrevocability (2024). I do not reject those possibilities. A giver's assurances, encouragement, and the normal requirements of the activity can support claims against withdrawal. The pump case stipulates an expressly limited loan, an existing cheap reserve, and no encouragement to discard it. It concerns an additional organizational decision, not the bare fact that permission proved useful.

Reliance must therefore be distinguished from an expectation of kindness. Ellis may correctly predict that Rowan will always help the residents. That prediction does not establish an entitlement to make continued help obligatory. The issue is who should bear the costs if Rowan wants the loan to end, not simply what Rowan is likely to want.

The principle can nevertheless place duties on both parties. Ellis may owe an affordable precaution, while Rowan owes warning, payment for a legitimately invited expense, or a safe transition. Rouméas distinguishes substantive opportunities to exit from fair allocation of exit costs: making exit affordable does not by itself make its terms fair (2023, “Exit ought to be fair”). The proposed precaution is one consideration within such an allocation, not a rule that the departing party always pays nothing.

A further objection says that Ellis can simply compensate Rowan for the extension. Suppose Ellis covers every ordinary expense and inconvenience of the extra two weeks, while retaining the pump throughout. Why insist on a substitute as well?

Sometimes a compensatory arrangement will be acceptable, particularly if Rowan chooses it. But reimbursement alone is not always sufficient. It can remove the costs of lending while leaving the decision whether to lend unavailable. The original arrangement allocated that decision to Rowan. Unless there is a sufficient contrary reason, Ellis cannot convert it into an entitlement to continued use at a price Ellis sets. There is a difference between offering Rowan an attractive reason to extend the loan and making extension obligatory, then paying for its ordinary costs.

This difference does not assume that every loss of control is impossible to compensate. It also does not refute a theory whose account of advantage already includes control. Rather, it identifies an interest that an adequate account must consider. Payment may address losses already sustained; obtaining a substitute restores the decision for the remaining period. When that restoration is readily possible and Rowan has not accepted an alternative, the recipient needs a reason for withholding it. The mere fact that the payment leaves Rowan happier overall is not enough.

The same reasoning limits the remedy. If restoring the option instantly would impose disproportionate costs or endanger residents, a slower restoration may be appropriate. If Rowan knowingly chooses compensation and continuation, the forward-looking allocation can change. What Ellis lacks is a general authority to decide unilaterally that the original choice is worth less than the offered benefit.

## 6. Repair without abandoning the residents

Suppose the sale has occurred and Rowan asks for the pump back. Neither the later duty nor the earlier wrong cancels the other. The residents did not lose their claims to protection when Ellis planned badly. Under the assumptions of the case, Rowan should allow temporary continued use. Ellis, meanwhile, owes reasonable efforts to obtain a substitute and restore the exit, as well as an appropriate allocation of transition costs.

This repair targets the arrangement that made renewal morally necessary. Merely soliciting a new expression of consent can leave that arrangement intact. Conversely, Rowan can genuinely accept a new arrangement without thereby declaring the earlier sale permissible. Cooperating with an emergency response is not the same as endorsing the decision that produced the emergency.

Repair need not wait for a refusal. Rowan may still want the loan to continue. Nevertheless, the possibility of revising that intention was part of the control left open. If Ellis can restore it at modest cost, the absence of a present intention to leave is not a complete answer. We ordinarily need some decisions to remain ours without having to announce plans to exercise them. Otherwise the opportunity can disappear before we have reason to want it.

This is not a general defense of independence from relationships. Friendship, caregiving and shared projects can create legitimate unchosen duties, sometimes duties that cannot be ended by withdrawing permission. Their existence does not show that every additional dependence a participant can arrange is fair. The relevant question concerns the particular responsibility that was undertaken and the particular option that someone else foreseeably obstructed.

Respecting revocable consent therefore has an organizational dimension. A recipient can acknowledge the giver's normative power while carelessly making its practical exercise morally unavailable. The fitting response is not to deny independent duties or make all relationships easy to leave. It is to assign reasonable responsibility for preserving a significant choice where the arrangement left that choice open, and to restore it when avoidable dependence has taken it away.
