# Unfinished Wrongs: Apology and the Settlement of the Past

## 1. A parenthesis in Strawson

In the third section of "Freedom and Resentment," P. F. Strawson pauses over what he calls "a rather unfashionable subject in moral philosophy at present" and offers a compressed analysis of it:

> To ask to be forgiven is in part to acknowledge that the attitude displayed in our actions was such as might properly be resented and in part to repudiate that attitude for the future (or at least for the immediate future); and to forgive is to accept the repudiation and to forswear the resentment. (§III)

Notice the division of labour. Asking forgiveness has a backward-looking part, *acknowledgement*, and a forward-looking part, *repudiation*. The parenthesis then narrows the forward-looking part almost to a shrug: repudiation for the immediate future will do.

I think the parenthesis is a mistake, and an instructive one. It is forced on Strawson by a background assumption that he shares with nearly everyone who has written on apology since. Call it

> **Fixity.** The expressive content of a deed — what attitude toward the victim the deed manifested — is fully determined by facts obtaining no later than the time of the deed.

Fixity looks like a truism. What I did to you, and what my doing it said about how I regard you, is over. Nothing I do now can reach back and alter it. If that is right, then later repudiation can only do one of two things. It can be *evidence* about the fixed fact: my remorse shows that the sneer was uncharacteristic, that the contempt you feared was not really there. Or it can be a *promise*: whatever I was then, I shall not be that henceforth. Strawson's parenthesis takes the second option and then, sensibly, admits that a promise about the far future is not something an apologiser is in a position to make.

But neither option captures what we ask for when we ask for an apology, and neither captures what we withhold when we refuse one. Consider four features of the practice.

First, *escalation by endorsement*. If, a week after insulting you, I say "and I meant every word; I'd say it again," you do not merely acquire a second grievance alongside the first. The first gets worse. The insult, which might have been a flash of temper, has become a statement of my standing view of you. It is not that I have committed two wrongs of comparable size; it is that the original wrong has been regraded.

Second, *continuation by silence*. Victims of unrepented wrongs characteristically report not that a fresh injury is being added each day, but that the original one is still going on. The metaphor is not accumulation but incompletion.

Third, *directedness*. An apology is owed, and owed to someone in particular. It is not a good deed that happens to benefit the victim; it is something the victim has standing to demand and standing to waive. Third parties may want it; only the victim can release you from it.

Fourth, *non-substitutability*. Money can replace money. Nothing replaces an apology. You cannot delegate it, buy it, or have your lawyer perform it, and a sincere one from a stranger is worthless.

Evidence does not behave like this. Evidence is not owed to anyone, it can be obtained from third parties, and a victim who reads the wrongdoer's private diary and finds genuine remorse has acquired the evidence without receiving the apology — and, tellingly, is not thereby satisfied. Promises do not behave like this either. A promise about my future conduct leaves the past exactly where it was; but it is precisely the past that the victim is asking about.

This essay argues that these features are not anomalies to be explained away but symptoms of the falsity of Fixity. I shall argue that for a wide class of wrongs the expressive content of the deed is genuinely unsettled at the time of acting, and that it is settled afterwards, when it is settled at all, by the agent's own subsequent practical self-relation. Apology, on this account, is neither a report nor a promise but the exercise of a power: the power to settle what one did. And because what is settled is a matter concerning the victim, the exercise of that power is something the victim is owed.

## 2. Two deflations

Before developing the positive view, it is worth seeing how the two most natural deflationary treatments fall short.

**Evidentialism.** On this view, later repudiation or endorsement is evidence about the agent's quality of will at the time of the deed. Standing attitudes are dispositional; dispositions manifest over time; later behaviour is simply more of the manifestation. The wrongdoer who reverts to cruelty shows that the earlier cruelty was no accident.

Much of this is true, and any adequate view must accommodate it. But evidentialism cannot be the whole story, for three reasons. It cannot explain directedness: if the wrongdoer's later stance is merely diagnostic, a sufficiently good diagnostician would do as well, and the demand that *you* tell *me* what you meant would be an irrational preference for an inferior instrument. It cannot explain non-substitutability, for the same reason. And, most importantly, it presupposes what is at issue: that there is always an antecedently determinate fact for the later stance to be evidence *of*. Section 3 argues that there often is not.

**The debt model.** A more sophisticated deflation grants that apology changes the normative situation, and explains the change by appeal to normative powers. Brandon Warmke has given this line its clearest recent statement. Forgiving, he argues, "affects the operative standards governing how the victim and wrongdoer are morally obliged or permitted to regard and treat one another" (p. 2), and he formulates what he calls the Post-Forgiveness Fact: "Paradigmatic cases of forgiving alter the norms of interaction for both the victim and the wrongdoer in certain characteristic ways" (p. 4). Wrongdoers, on this picture, incur personal obligations to their victims — "sincere apology, repentance and remorse, restitution ... and penance" (p. 4) — and forgiveness releases them from some of these.

This is, I think, correct as far as it goes, and my own account will make use of the idea of a normative power. But as an account of why apology matters it leaves the central question untouched. A debt model tells us that the wrongdoer owes something and that apology is among the things owed. It does not tell us *why apology is the currency*. Why should the discharge of a debt created by an insult take the form of a first-personal utterance about the insult, rather than any other costly gift? The debt model has no principled answer, and its natural extension — that apology is owed because victims happen to want it — makes the demand look like a taste.

The model also cannot explain a striking asymmetry. Payments are indifferent to motive: a debt discharged grudgingly is discharged. Apologies are not like that. An insincere apology is not a poorly motivated performance of the required act; it is a failure to perform it at all. Something about apology makes the agent's inner stance constitutive rather than decorative. The account below explains why.

## 3. The unsettled will

Start with a question about what resentment is aimed at. Strawson says that the personal reactive attitudes "are essentially reactions to the quality of others' wills towards us, as manifested in their behaviour: to their good or ill will or indifference or lack of concern" (§V). And his account of ordinary excuses confirms the target. Pleas of the first group — "He didn't mean to," "He hadn't realized," "He was pushed" — work by showing "that the fact of injury was quite consistent with the agent's attitude and intentions being just what we demand they should be" (§IV). They do not deny that something bad happened to me; they deny that it manifested a will that was against me.

Now: what sort of item is a *will towards someone*? It is not an occurrent episode. It is not the flash of irritation, the spike of pleasure at another's discomfort, the momentary absence of the thought that she matters. Those are events in a psychological history. A will towards someone is a standing orientation: where the agent *stands* with respect to her. That is what we care about, and Strawson is right that we care about it enormously — his "central commonplace" is "the very great importance that we attach to the attitudes and intentions towards us of other human beings" (§III).

The crucial claim of this essay is that the occurrent psychological facts at the time of acting frequently underdetermine the standing orientation, and that they underdetermine it not merely epistemically but constitutively.

Here the work of Richard Moran on self-knowledge is directly to the point, though he does not apply it to this question. Moran argues that first-person access to one's own attitudes is not, at bottom, an epistemic achievement based on inner or outer evidence, but an expression of commitment. Imagining an agent with perfect theoretical self-knowledge — immediate, infallible, extending to dreams and passing thoughts — he observes that such an agent would still lack something: "Beyond simply being aware of the attitude in question (by whatever mode of awareness) there would remain outstanding the question of her endorsement of it" (p. 156). Discussing Sartre's gambler, he notes that the gambler "must recognize that the resolution exists as a fact for anyone to base a prediction on only insofar as I endorse it" (p. 150). And he concludes that "nothing but some commitment on his part could make it the case that his world-directed reflection on the reasons available to him for believing something or doing something does indeed settle the question of what he does believe or what he will (in fact) do" (p. 157).

Moran's topic is the present tense. But the same structure holds for the retrospective question *what did I think of her, in doing that?* Suppose Carl makes a cutting remark to a colleague in a meeting. Give us complete access to his psychology at the moment: irritation at being interrupted, a flicker of satisfaction at the sting, no explicit thought about her standing. Does this settle whether he holds her in contempt? It does not. "Holding her in contempt" is a matter of where Carl stands, and Carl may genuinely not yet stand anywhere. The record is mixed because he is mixed, and he is mixed because he has not yet determined himself on the point.

This is not a fanciful condition; it is the normal one. Consider how such cases go. Carl is asked, by the colleague or by himself, "did you mean that?" Sometimes he can answer at once. Often the honest answer is: *I don't know — I have to decide*. That locution is philosophically remarkable. It is not what one says about an unknown fact. One does not decide whether it rained yesterday. If Carl's stance toward his colleague were an antecedently determinate fact awaiting discovery, "I have to decide" would be a category mistake rather than, as it plainly is, a form of scrupulousness.

Notice too the authority we accord the agent's answer. When we are wronged we do not seek the best-placed observer; we ask the wrongdoer. If the expressive content of the deed were a settled psychological fact, this would be a strange preference, since the wrongdoer is often a poor witness to himself. Moran's diagnosis explains the preference. What we want from Carl is not a report but an avowal, and an avowal is not a superior sighting of a fixed object; it is the taking of a stand, which is answerable to reasons and "mine to maintain or revoke" (p. 154).

Two clarifications will forestall misunderstanding.

First, I am not claiming that the deed is unsettled. That Carl spoke, that the words were these, that they wounded, that he was irritated — all fixed, all beyond retrieval. What is unsettled is a further matter: whether the irritation was *his*, in the sense of expressing where he stands, or whether it merely passed through him.

Second, the claim is not that all deeds are unsettled in this way. Premeditated cruelty settles itself: the deliberation that precedes it is already a taking of a stand. Unsettledness is a feature of the vast middle range of ordinary wrongdoing — the careless, the hot-tempered, the cowardly, the deeds done from within a role — which is precisely the range in which apology does most of its work.

Finally, an intuition pump to isolate constitution from evidence. Aron and Boris each say the same cruel thing, in the same circumstances, with psychological records at the moment of speaking that are, so far as anything bearing on the point goes, alike. Afterwards their paths diverge: Aron comes to repudiate what he said; Boris comes to stand behind it. Was Boris's victim wronged worse than Aron's? Most of us think so. The evidentialist must say either that the divergence reveals a difference that already obtained (but by hypothesis the record at the time does not contain one) or that the two acts were exactly alike in gravity and Boris has simply added a separate offence. The second answer is available, but it incurs a debt: it must explain why the gravity of Boris's "new" offence is proportioned to the *old* deed rather than to the utterance, why it is owed to the *original* victim rather than to anyone who hears it, and why the victim experiences it as the same wrong going on. Unsettledness explains all three at once.

## 4. The Settlement Thesis

I can now state the view.

> **The Settlement Thesis (ST).** For a wide class of wrongs, the expressive content of the deed — what attitude toward the victim it manifested — is not fully determined at the time of acting. It is settled, when it is settled, by the agent's subsequent practical self-relation: by whether the agent repudiates the deed or stands behind it. Settlement makes it true, rather than merely revealing, that the deed expressed what it did.

The crucial distinction, which disposes of the immediate charge of incoherence, is between *alteration* and *completion*. To alter the past would be to make it the case that some proposition about it was true and is now false. Nothing of the kind is claimed, and nothing of the kind is possible. Completion is different: it is making determinate what was indeterminate. Before settlement, "Carl's remark expressed his contempt for her" is neither true nor false. After settlement it is one or the other. No truth-value flips; a gap is closed.

Completion of this kind is not exotic. Whether a battle was the turning point of a war is not settled when the battle ends. Whether a remark was the opening move in a campaign of harassment depends on what follows it. Whether a decision was the moment a man lost his nerve depends on what he did next. Morally weighty descriptions of past events routinely wait on later facts, and no one thinks this involves backward causation. What is distinctive about the present case is only that the later facts which do the settling are facts about the *same agent's will* — which is exactly what makes them apt to be the object of the victim's reactive attitudes rather than mere historical context.

There is a partial precedent in Bernard Williams. Discussing his Gauguin, who abandons pressing human claims for the sake of an artistic project whose success cannot be foreseen, Williams writes: "The justification, if there is to be one, will be essentially retrospective" (p. 24). Whether Gauguin was justified is not fixed at the moment of choice; it depends on what he turns out to be. Williams applies this to rational justification and leaves the moral relation largely aside. ST applies the same structural point to the moral relation itself, and locates the settling factor not in luck but in the agent's own practical self-determination.

There is a further precedent, from a very different tradition, in Max Scheler's essay on repentance. Scheler's target is the view — he attributes a version of it to Nietzsche — that repentance is self-deception, since it "leads us to dwell morbidly on a past which is done with and unalterable" (p. 36), and that in repenting we "confound our memory-image of the deed with the deed itself" (p. 37). Against this he insists that repentance has, alongside its negative function, "another which is positive, liberating and constructive" (p. 36).[^1] ST agrees with Scheler that the past is, in a restricted respect, still open, and disagrees about what is open and to whom it matters. Scheler's concern is the agent's spiritual rebirth. Mine is bipolar: what is at stake is a relation between two people, and settling it is something one of them owes the other.

Three constraints keep ST from licensing self-serving revision.

*Truthfulness.* Settlement is not a free choice among narratives. An agent cannot settle a deed as a lapse by describing it as one; he must actually repudiate it, and repudiation that misdescribes what was done is not repudiation of it. This is why evasive formulas — "mistakes were made," "I'm sorry you feel that way" — fail. They attempt to settle without acknowledging, and acknowledgement is the material on which settlement operates. Strawson was right that apology has an acknowledging part; the error was to think the other part points only forward.

*Constraint by the record.* The occurrent facts underdetermine, but they do not fail to constrain. A deed done with elaborate preparation cannot be settled as a slip. What settlement determines is which of the admissible readings is the true one, not which is the most comfortable.

*Answerability over time.* Settlement is not accomplished by an utterance. It is accomplished by a stand, and a stand, as Moran notes, exists as a fact only insofar as the agent endorses it (p. 150). A repudiation followed by reversion was not, in the end, a repudiation. Settlement is therefore defeasible — which is not a defect of the account but an explanation of a familiar phenomenon: apologies can be broken.

## 5. What the victim is owed

If ST is true, a normative consequence follows immediately, and it is here that the view does the most work.

Something about the victim's situation is unsettled — not a fact about the world at large, but a fact about how another person stands toward *her*. Michael Thompson has argued that judgements like *X wronged Y* exhibit a distinctive "practical-bipolar" form, irreducible to the monadic judgements that dominate ethical theory: "You have, as we sometimes say, a duty 'to Sylvia' not to kill her. You 'owe' it to her not to kill her" (p. 334). The just agent, on his account, deliberates in first-person judgements of this bipolar type (p. 337). The unsettled matter identified by ST is bipolar in exactly this sense. It is not the impersonal question whether a bad thing happened; it is the question what one person's will was toward another.

This yields:

> **The Duty of Settlement (DS).** Where a wrong is unsettled in the sense of ST, the victim has a claim against the wrongdoer that the wrongdoer settle it, and settle it truthfully.

Three things recommend DS. It is *directed*, because the unsettled matter is a matter about the victim, which is why third parties may deplore the silence but cannot release the wrongdoer from it. It is *non-delegable*, because only the agent whose will is in question can settle where that will stands. And it explains why leaving the matter open is not a neutral omission but itself a way of treating the victim badly: to leave unsettled whether one holds someone in contempt is to treat the question of her standing as not worth one's trouble, which is a recognisable species of the indifference Strawson identifies as an object of resentment.

We can now say what an apology is. **An apology is the exercise, addressed to the victim, of the power to settle what one did.** It is not a report, because it is an avowal, not an observation. It is not a promise, because what it settles is the deed, not the future. It is not a payment, because the currency is the agent's own stance and nothing else has that denomination.

The four features of section 1 fall out. Escalation by endorsement: "I meant it" is a settlement, and it settles the deed in the worst available way, which is why the original wrong gets worse rather than a new one being added. Continuation by silence: an unsettled wrong is literally unfinished, so the victim's sense that it is still going on is accurate. Directedness and non-substitutability: settlement is bipolar and first-personal, so it can be neither owed to the public nor performed by proxy. And the constitutive role of sincerity is explained: an insincere apology fails not because it is badly motivated but because no stand has been taken, and where no stand is taken nothing is settled.

## 6. Objections

**(i) This still changes the past.** The objection says that on ST the wrongdoer makes it the case that his deed had a property it did not have. Reply: he makes it the case that his deed had a property it was not determinate that it had. No proposition changes truth-value; an indeterminacy is resolved. If one finds the very idea of an indeterminate moral fact suspect, note that the indeterminacy here is inherited from a familiar source. The truth conditions for ascriptions of standing attitudes include, among their truthmakers, the agent's own endorsement; where endorsement is withheld and the behavioural record is mixed, there is nothing to make the ascription true or false. The moral indeterminacy is a shadow of an indeterminacy in the will.

**(ii) The victim is held hostage.** ST appears to give the wrongdoer power over how badly the victim was wronged — power he can exercise or withhold at pleasure. This seems to compound the original injury.

The objection is serious and the reply has three parts. First, the power is not discretionary but dutiful: DS says the victim has a *claim* to its exercise, so the wrongdoer is not a free agent in the matter but a debtor. Second, it is bounded by truthfulness and by the record; the wrongdoer cannot settle the deed as better than it admits of being. Third, and most importantly, the apparent cost is the price of apology's mattering at all. On any view that makes everything fixed at the moment of the deed, apology can only be evidence or promise, and we have seen that it is neither. Meanwhile, the phenomenon the objection points to is real and ST alone predicts it: there *is* a distinctive injury in being wronged by someone who will not settle what he did, and it is not the injury of being disbelieved or of facing future risk. It is the injury of being left in normative limbo by the only person who can end it.

**(iii) The deathbed.** If the wrongdoer dies immediately, the deed is permanently unsettled. Is that not absurd?

It is, I think, the right result. Compare an unfinished symphony: there is no fact about how it goes on, and no amount of scholarship will produce one. Those wronged by people who died unrepentant often describe exactly this — not ignorance of what was done, but the sense that they will never know what it *was*. That is not a confused complaint; it is an accurate report of an incompletable matter. Three mitigations keep the result from being intolerable. Much is settled by the deed itself. The agent's life up to the deed constrains the admissible readings, so death leaves ambiguity, not a void. And nothing in ST prevents the victim from grading the wrong by the range of admissible readings, which is what, in practice, survivors do.

**(iv) Cheap grace.** Cannot a wrongdoer settle a deed in his own favour by a well-timed repentance and then revert? He can attempt it, and he will fail, because the settling item is not the utterance but the stand, and a stand abandoned was not taken. The account thus builds in a temporal test that matches practice: we do not regard an apology as having succeeded until we see how the apologiser goes on. What ST adds is that this is not merely a matter of our evidence catching up. The reversion does not show that the settlement was always bogus; it unmakes it.

**(v) Does this let wrongdoers off?** No. Successful repudiation does not make the deed permissible, does not undo the harm, does not by itself discharge duties of repair, and does not entitle the wrongdoer to forgiveness. It settles the deed as an expression of a will the agent now disowns — which is a real change in the wrong's gravity, but a change purchased at a price. To repudiate sincerely is to accept that one was the person who did that, and to give up the various comforts of not deciding. Cheap repudiation is the one kind that does not work.

**(vi) Is this not just dispositionalism?** One might say: standing attitudes are dispositions; dispositions are individuated partly by their manifestations; so of course later behaviour bears on what attitude was present. But dispositionalism keeps Fixity. On that view the disposition was there all along and the later manifestation is evidence of it. ST denies that there was always a determinate disposition to be evidenced. The difference is testable against the cases in section 3: dispositionalism cannot make sense of "I have to decide," and cannot explain why the agent's own avowal has an authority that a better-informed observer's verdict lacks.

## 7. Residue, forgiveness, and the apologies of institutions

Three consequences are worth drawing out.

*Moral residue.* Philosophers have often noticed that even after apology and forgiveness something remains, and have struggled to say what. ST gives a clean answer. Settlement operates on expressive content only. The deed, the harm, and the fact that this person once did that to that person are untouched. What can be finished is the question of where the agent stands; what cannot be finished is that the question ever arose.

*Forgiveness.* Strawson says that "to forgive is to accept the repudiation and to forswear the resentment" (§III). ST explains why acceptance comes first. The wrongdoer settles; the victim accepts the settlement; the reactive attitudes, which track quality of will, then have a different object than they had. This also explains why forgiveness is not condonation: what the victim accepts is a settlement that classifies the deed as wrong and as disowned, so accepting it is the opposite of minimising it. And it explains why forgiveness in the absence of any settlement — forgiving the unrepentant — has the peculiar character it does. It is not irrational, but it is not acceptance of anything; it is a unilateral decision to stop pressing a claim that remains outstanding.

*Institutions.* Apologies by states, churches, and firms for historical wrongs are often dismissed as empty, on the grounds that the actors are dead and the past is past. ST identifies what is actually at stake. Institutions have standing orientations that persist across changes of personnel, and those orientations are partly constituted by what the institution is prepared to say about its own history. A standing refusal to repudiate is not a neutral silence; it settles the deed as the institution's own. This explains three familiar facts: that the *refusal* to apologise is itself a live grievance, quite apart from compensation; that apologies which explain the deed away as a product of its era fail, since they decline to settle; and that descendants have standing to demand settlement, because the unsettled matter is the institution's present orientation toward the community they belong to, not merely a historical curiosity.

The limits of ST should be stated plainly. It is not a theory of blame, nor of punishment, nor of every kind of wrong. Wrongs whose gravity is exhausted by harm are largely untouched by it. Deeds that settle themselves in the doing are untouched by it. And it says nothing about what a victim ought to do once a settlement is offered. It is an account of one thing: why the moral significance of ordinary wrongdoing remains, for a while, the wrongdoer's business, and why the victim may insist that he attend to it.

## 8. Conclusion

Strawson's parenthesis — repudiation "for the future (or at least for the immediate future)" — was a small concession to a large assumption. If quality of will is fixed at the moment of acting, repudiation can only be a forecast, and a forecast about the far future is not something one can honestly offer. The concession is unnecessary, because the assumption is false. The wills we care about are not episodes but orientations, and orientations are not simply found; they are, in part, taken up. What an agent did to someone is therefore not always finished when the doing stops.

That is why an apology is not a report and not a promise. It is the completion of an act. And it is why, when we are wronged and hear nothing, we are not being impatient or vindictive in continuing to wait. We are waiting for something that is genuinely outstanding, and that only one person can supply.
