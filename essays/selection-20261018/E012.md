# When a Remedy Becomes Evidence

Consider a hypothetical municipal fund which reimburses residents for damage caused by its contractors. It supplies an inspection service and undertakes to preserve its photographs. Mara files a timely claim. Before it is decided, the fund deletes the photographic archive to save money, despite knowing its preservation obligation. The deletion is indiscriminate: nobody examines the photographs or selects inconvenient claims. The fund considers Mara’s testimony, the inspection receipt, and the reasonable alternative sources, but cannot now adequately determine the contractor’s responsibility.

The fund recognizes that it owes Mara a remedy for destroying her opportunity to substantiate her claim. It awards a payment. Months later, another office receives a list of “successful claims” and treats Mara’s entry as evidence that the contractor damaged her home. Alternatively, suppose the fund refuses reimbursement but pays separate compensation for the lost opportunity. Its list of “unsuccessful claims” later becomes evidence that Mara’s accusation was unfounded. Either way, a decision about what to do under damaged evidential conditions acquires an apparent authority to answer a question the proceeding did not determine.

The problem can survive a fair payment and an honest initial hearing. I argue that wrongful evidence loss can leave a continuing duty to support the affected person’s ability to contest consequential misrepresentations of the decision, where the loss still impairs a means of defence protected by the original undertaking. Accurate communication is one way of fulfilling that duty, but sometimes the duty requires assistance after an accurate communication has been distorted by someone else. This is a specific application of a reparative obligation, not a new general theory of institutional honesty. It explains both why an institution may owe more than a corrected label and why its help must preserve the limits of its own determination without claiming authority over what everyone else can know.

## 1. Three questions hidden in one outcome

Three propositions must be distinguished in Mara’s case:

1. The contractor damaged Mara’s home.
2. The fund wrongfully destroyed evidence relevant to Mara’s claim.
3. Mara is now entitled to the payment awarded by the fund.

The proceeding may establish the second and third without establishing the first. The third need not be a polite fiction. If the fund has a justified power to award compensation for an evidential wrong, exercising that power can create a real entitlement. Mara may insist that the money is hers even though she cannot cite the award as a finding that the contractor damaged her home.

The first proposition is not therefore unknowable, or even unknown. Mara might have witnessed the damage. An outside investigator might properly credit her testimony more highly than the fund did. The correct qualification is that this payment decision did not determine contractor responsibility. It is not that nobody has adequate grounds for believing the contractor responsible. Institutions can abuse epistemic authority by treating their own non-findings as declarations of universal ignorance.

The propositions can diverge in the other direction as well. Independently recovered evidence might establish that the contractor caused no damage, while the fund still owes Mara compensation for expense or delay caused by the destruction. An evidential wrong does not guarantee that the underlying claim was true. Conversely, a true underlying claim need not involve an evidential wrong. These differences concern grounds of entitlement, not degrees of sympathy.

Institutions nevertheless need categorical outcomes. Payment cannot remain simultaneously authorized and unauthorized while a fact-finder suspends judgment. But a categorical practical answer does not require an answer to every historical question on which the dispute initially depended. A remedial entitlement can provide another ground on which deliberation ends.

The distinction between action and belief is familiar. The further issue is what the institution may let its decision communicate to people who did not observe the adjudication. A record can correctly communicate the third proposition to a cashier while misleadingly suggesting the first to someone evaluating the contractor. Its adequacy depends on its use, and that use may change after its author has stopped attending to it.

## 2. The contribution and its antecedents

The thought that evidence loss itself calls for redress is established. Porat and Stein defend liability for wrongfully worsened uncertainty, including compensation and changes to the burden of persuasion. Friedman favors remedies adapted to different circumstances. These views already recognize an injury beyond the primary wrong being litigated. (Porat and Stein 1997, abstract; Friedman 1997, introductory passage.)

Nance supplies a closer antecedent. He separates evidence management from deciding merits and criticizes concealing a remedy for an evidential problem inside an apparently ordinary merits determination. His preferred use of issue preclusion can direct facts to be treated as fixed within litigation, and he discusses consequences for later malpractice claims. These observations require us to distinguish a decision’s procedural force from its evidence about history, including when it is used elsewhere. (Nance 2010, pp. 1103–1104, 1110–1114.)

The social life of repair is likewise an established philosophical concern. Flowerree argues that successful epistemic reparation requires audience uptake and an appropriate reparative response, rather than merely offering information. Altanian argues that restorative epistemic reparation must address harmful epistemic practices and recognize victims as contributors, beyond providing them with knowledge. Their accounts make subsequent reception and testimonial standing central to repair. (Flowerree 2026, §§2–3; Altanian 2024, §4.)

My narrower contribution concerns a particular combination: an established evidential breach, a practically authoritative decision, and an underlying allegation which that decision does not adequately determine. I defend a residual duty to substitute, within reasonable limits, for the claimant’s impaired ability to challenge later misdescription. I also specify what such assistance must communicate. It should identify what the institution did and did not determine, preserve legitimate procedural consequences, and leave others’ testimony available for evaluation. This develops the existing reparative approach rather than opposing it.

The argument does not determine the correct monetary remedy. The value of Mara’s lost opportunity, the fund’s undertaking, available alternatives, and other claims on its resources all matter. Assume that a payment for the lost opportunity has been fairly assessed. That assumption isolates the further obligation which a correct award can leave outstanding. Nor does payment first create that obligation: it arises from the wrongful impairment. A refusal to offer any remedy would not erase it.

## 3. From accurate records to substitute support

The protected function needs to be specified before the later duty can be defended. Suppose the fund promises to take reasonable care to preserve dated inspection photographs and supply residents with authenticated copies for five years. The stated purposes include both presenting claims and answering later official challenges to those claims. Residents can reasonably depend on that service instead of arranging duplicate inspections. The undertaking protects their use of the evidence, not merely the fund’s internal storage. Closing a payment proceeding does not end the promised access.

This is a substantive premise. A duty to keep material only until an initial decision would not by itself establish the same later obligation. The example concerns a continuing evidential service on which the claimant was entitled to rely. Its scope must be warranted by the undertaking or another independent ground; it cannot be inferred simply from the fact that help would now be useful.

Deleting the photographs can therefore damage two interests. Mara loses an opportunity to obtain reimbursement. She can also lose a means of answering subsequent descriptions of the dispute. Payment for the first loss does not necessarily restore the second. Its importance becomes clear when other offices rely on the fund’s records instead of conducting their own inquiries.

In an easy case, the fund sends a bare denial code to an office which predictably interprets it as a finding that Mara complained falsely. The fund should qualify the record. Yet this case does not establish a specifically reparative duty. An institution which innocently lost photographs would also owe accurate, appropriately qualified communication. Mara could complain of a misleading publication in either case.

Now consider two harder variants. In both, the fund initially sends a clear record: reimbursement was not established, the fund breached its preservation duty, and compensation was awarded for the resulting disadvantage. Two years later, the receiving office migrates its database and drops the explanation. It gives Mara a false-complaint flag, understood as saying that she invented the reported damage, and uses that flag to reject a new application. The fund neither authorized the alteration nor could reasonably have prevented it. The receiving office owes correction in both variants. What differs is whether the photographs would have helped Mara obtain it.

In the first variant, the office offers two ways of removing the flag. An inexpensive factual review accepts authenticated inspection photographs showing that the reported damage existed. Otherwise, Mara must seek formal correction of the imported decision record. That process requires an authenticated account of the original decision and costly procedural assistance; merely obtaining the certificate does not complete the review. These are stipulated features of a hypothetical procedure, not claims about an actual office or a defence of its burdens.

Suppose the lost photographs would have shown the fresh crack Mara reported, while leaving open whether the contractor caused it. With access to the archive, she could have answered the accusation of invented damage through the inexpensive review. The photographs would not have authenticated what the fund decided. They would have supplied a different route to removing the harmful flag. Evidence insufficient to establish contractor responsibility can still answer the narrower accusation that no damage occurred.

Without the photographs, Mara must use the more expensive route. She cannot afford its additional cost; the fund can provide effective assistance at a cost proportionate to the threatened disadvantage. Here the evidential breach makes a concrete difference. Funding the additional reasonable work can replace protection once supplied by the photographs, although the replacement concerns the decision record rather than the underlying damage. The fund should certify only what it actually determined. It need not, and should not, invent a finding that Mara’s factual account was true.

The stipulated content of the photographs establishes a clear positive case. In an actual dispute, destruction alone would not establish that they were helpful. Their likely relevance would need support from such things as the inspection’s subject, surviving descriptions and the receiving procedure’s requirements. A genuine lost opportunity can justify assistance without certainty of victory, but a merely imaginable evidential benefit cannot justify charging every later expense to the custodian.

In the second variant, the office treats the flag strictly as a report of what the fund decided. Correction turns solely on whether the original decision made a finding of false complaint. Only the fund’s authenticated decision record answers that question; photographs of a cracked wall do not. Assume the steps, cost and prospect of correction would be the same if the photographs had survived. The fund still owes appropriate authentication, and the receiving office still owes correction. But the deletion supplies no additional reason for the fund to finance this process. Any such reason would need another basis, such as responsibility for the later error or an ordinary duty of assistance.

The comparison holds the receiving office’s mistake fixed and asks what preserving the photographs would have changed. It does not ask whether the fund created the mistake. In the first variant, the receiving office creates the threat while the fund has wrongfully removed a protected means of resisting it. In the second, the removed material has no bearing on the available defence. Both institutions may owe accurate communication, yet only the first variant establishes the further reparative ground at issue here.

The underlying principle is this: when one wrongfully impairs another’s protected capacity to resist a foreseeable kind of disadvantage, one has a presumptive duty to provide a proportionate substitute when that disadvantage materializes and the capacity remains impaired. A custodian does not restore a usable means merely by providing one accurately described object. If an honest statement ceases to serve as an adequate substitute for the lost evidence, the original reason for repair can remain. The duty concerns the claimant’s ability to answer the challenge, not the production of a document regardless of its use.

Why accept this bridge? Imagine destroying someone’s only means of accessing a service, then offering an inadequate replacement while insisting that the replacement was accurately described. Accurate description does not answer the complaint that access remains impaired. Similarly, an honest decision record and a usable ability to challenge official misdescription are different goods. The latter need not be wholly restored for an obligation to be fulfilled, but its continuing impairment must enter the assessment of what the wrongdoer still owes. The two variants show why that assessment cannot be replaced by either a demand for perpetual help or a presumption that one honest statement ends the matter.

This also explains the duty’s temporal and institutional reach in the positive case. The promised period has not expired, and answering an official challenge to this claim falls within the service’s protected use. The particular database mistake need not have been predictable or preventable by the fund. What matters is that losing a usable defence to consequential accounts of the claim is a foreseeable kind of disadvantage within the undertaking’s scope. The argument does not establish the same scope for every promise to preserve evidence.

The fund’s obligation concerns the additional difficulty its breach created. If the receiving office supplies an effective correction without burdening Mara, no funded substitute is needed. If both offices assist, the same expense should not be recovered twice. Where neither has yet done so, the receiving office’s responsibility is no reason to classify the fund’s outstanding repair obligation as already fulfilled. The exact division of costs between the offices requires further principles; the present argument establishes a claim to proportionate assistance against the fund, not its sole responsibility for every consequence.

What difference does wrongdoing make? Compare a fund which lost the same archive in an unforeseeable disaster despite meeting its undertaking to exercise reasonable care. Both funds may owe inexpensive authentication under ordinary institutional duties. In the first variant, however, only the responsible fund also owes assistance because it wrongfully deprived Mara of a protected means. This can affect the allocation of correction costs and priority among otherwise comparable demands. Where ordinary assistance is reasonably rationed, an institution should not treat an outstanding repair claim as merely one more application for discretionary help.

This is not an absolute priority over urgent needs or other debts. Nor must the two funds perform different acts in every case. The responsible fund cannot defend an ordinary assistance limit without accounting for the impairment it still owes a response to. Other things equal, its own unrepaired wrong gives Mara an additional claim on its resources. In the second variant, by contrast, the destroyed photographs do not establish that additional claim for these correction costs.

The new administrator inherits this institutional responsibility without inheriting personal blame. She receives the fund’s obligations and the means to administer them. Replacing its employees does not restore Mara’s ability to challenge an official account of her claim. The relevant continuity concerns the undertaking and the outstanding repair, rather than anyone’s culpable mental state.

## 4. What assistance should preserve

The argument yields two connected requirements. When circulating a decision, an institution must take reasonable steps against foreseeable overstatement of its factual authority. When its prior breach has impaired a person’s ability to contest a consequential misdescription, it may additionally owe proportionate support for correction, including beyond uses it controls. The second requirement supplies the specifically reparative extension.

In both cases, the correction must distinguish the grounds of this determination from a judgment about what everyone can know. An appropriate statement might say: “This award compensates for the loss of relevant evidence. The payment proceeding did not determine whether the contractor caused the damage.” That statement can coexist with Mara’s testimony, an investigator’s conclusions, or later evidence supporting either side. It cannot be used to insist that those contributions receive no credibility until the fund endorses them.

The duty therefore has a positive side. The fund should affirm its established breach and the claimant’s real entitlement, rather than describing the entire case as a haze of uncertainty. It should make surviving reasons and appropriate routes for further testimony usable. If another office misclassifies Mara’s testimony as officially refuted, the fund can authenticate the non-finding without claiming to prove her underlying account. Helping her speak in her own right can be more appropriate than speaking for her.

No universal form follows. Separate descriptions of the breach, the practical outcome, and the determination’s scope may be useful, but a short intelligible explanation sometimes suffices. An office needing only to know that payment is authorized may receive exactly that information. A person investigating contractor performance needs a different account because the payment is being used as a proxy for historical conduct.

This sensitivity to changing informational contexts is not new. Nissenbaum’s contextual-integrity framework explicitly treats context and transformations of data as significant for informational norms. Here the particular additional ground is the issuer’s debt arising from a wrongful loss of the claimant’s means of contestation. (Nissenbaum 2019, pp. 225–226, 255–256.)

An audience test helps identify a misleading historical use. Would learning the actual basis of the decision materially weaken the historical evidential inference the recipient is invited to draw from it? If so, the issuer should preserve that material limitation, absent a sufficiently strong competing reason. This test concerns evidence about what happened. It does not imply that every recipient must reassess the decision’s practical or procedural authority.

## 5. Binding a question without proving its answer

A serious objection appeals to finality. Institutions sometimes settle disputes precisely because the parties cannot agree about the facts. If a losing party can say forever that a settlement proves nothing, the authority to settle may seem empty. The entitlement example makes the answer look too easy: the money is owed while history is set aside. But some legitimate sanctions require an issue to be treated as fixed in subsequent reasoning.

Suppose a fair remedy for withholding evidence precludes the custodian from contesting a factual issue within specified proceedings. A later benefits office might be required to honor that determination. It may permissibly reason that the issue must be treated as satisfied even if it has no independent belief that the historical allegation is true. This is normative reliance on the binding act. It is neither newly acquired historical evidence nor simply recognition of a payment already owed.

We must therefore distinguish three later uses of a decision: treating it as evidence of history, recognizing an entitlement it creates, and applying an issue it legitimately fixes for procedural purposes. The duty defended here permits all three when their respective grounds support them. What it opposes is allowing the latter two to masquerade as the first.

Normative power can change what may be challenged, what premises officials must use in a defined proceeding, and what remedies may be demanded. It cannot make a contractor have damaged a house. The institution should therefore preserve both the procedural force of its act and the scope within which that force applies. A notation that the factual question was fixed as a remedy need not weaken its binding force. It identifies the authority a later official should honor and the historical inference an outsider should not automatically draw.

This treatment accommodates Nance’s proposal that facts be taken as fixed within litigation while retaining his concern about disguising evidence management as fact-finding. It adds a requirement governing the distinction’s survival across later uses. Nothing here decides whether any particular preclusion rule is justified. The point is conditional: even legitimate preclusion does not automatically carry unrestricted testimonial authority.

Institutions also earn authority by conducting inquiries for the public. Ross argues that criminal convictions should support rational belief in guilt and provide a basis for ending ordinary inquiry; his later work treats convictions as defeasible institutional testimony. These views concern authority earned through appropriate inquiry, rather than an authority automatically possessed by every institutional resolution. (Ross 2023, §IV; Ross 2026, “Conviction as Institutional Testimony.”)

A procedure which must attribute historical guilt to perform its proper function cannot repair inadequate proof merely by relabeling a conviction. It may have to withhold that verdict while another authorized body provides relief. Conversely, where a legitimate remedial act creates an entitlement, its recipient possesses that entitlement fully. Challenging Mara’s payment solely because contractor responsibility was not determined makes the converse mistake of treating the payment as proof of that responsibility.

Practical finality remains possible. The parties know what is owed, the administrator knows what to execute, and later officials know which procedural constraints apply. Ordinary inquiry can also continue, through testimony and reassessment as well as new documents. Respecting the decision does not require mistaking the questions it settles for all the questions that matter.

## 6. Evidence, privacy, and limits of repair

Perhaps even a remedial decision really is evidence of the underlying fact. Authorities do not compensate every person who asks; they may require a credible claim and proof of a material evidential loss. Someone learning that Mara received an award might rationally increase her confidence that the contractor damaged the house.

The proposal prohibits no warranted inference. It requires the inference to reflect the kind of decision actually made. Consider an artificial illustration. Among ordinary merit-based awards, ninety percent concern true underlying claims. Among awards addressing wrongful evidential loss, forty percent do. Suppose ninety percent of all awards belong to the first category. An observer told only that an award occurred might assign an eighty-five percent probability to the underlying claim. An observer who knows the award belongs to the second category should use forty percent instead. These numbers describe no actual institution.

The second observer may still regard the award as evidence relative to a sufficiently low prior probability. The fault arises when the institution knows its category, foreseeably invites the ordinary inference, and fails to preserve the distinction. A further verdict does not create fresh historical support simply by restating the earlier outcome. Where a recipient already knows all relevant grounds and a rule that fully determines the decision, learning that the decision follows that rule adds no confirmation of the original allegation.

Destruction itself can also be probative. Someone who selectively burns a file may reveal knowledge that it is damaging. The fund can communicate evidence of such selection when it exists. Mara’s example deliberately involves indiscriminate deletion for cost savings. Even where destruction supports an allegation, its actual evidential force remains distinguishable from its remedial significance. The fact that a sanction is justified does not show that the inference from destruction independently supports a historical finding of the same strength.

Disclosure creates a harder problem. Marking Mara’s award as remedial may stigmatize her as less deserving, or expose matters to people who have no legitimate interest in them. If a landlord needs to know only that Mara can pay rent, the fund should confirm her entitlement without inviting an inference about the contractor. The duty is to prevent misleading use and restore contestation, not to expose the entire file. Where a recipient has a legitimate reason to investigate the contractor, a carefully framed account should affirm the entitlement while identifying the determination’s limits. If no appropriate account can be given, withholding the misleading proxy may be preferable to broadcasting private details.

The duties also have different beneficiaries. The original preservation undertaking grounds a reparative debt to Mara. Protection of the contractor against unsupported accusations can arise independently from fair treatment and responsible communication. It does not require pretending that the fund owes the contractor the very same reparative debt. If the preservation undertaking also protected contractors, another directed debt may arise, but that additional premise is unnecessary here.

This matters for settlement. An informed agreement might replace some individualized assistance with adequate resources or release a claim to that assistance. Acceptance of compensation for the lost reimbursement opportunity alone does not establish such a release. And Mara cannot release the contractor’s rights or authorize the fund to mislead later recipients. A bilateral agreement can settle what is owed to her without creating permission to misrepresent others.

Third-party rights also constrain which substitutes are permissible. Suppose a board lost examination scripts and proposes to repair the loss by certifying everyone’s professional competence. It may owe funded reassessment or compensation. It cannot thereby establish competence, or justify transferring the resulting risks to clients who rely on the certificate. A credential’s formal validity does not settle whether issuing it was morally permissible. An entitlement whose costs the responsible institution can fairly bear differs from an assurance that imposes danger on strangers.

Finally, the residual duty must not become perpetual insurance against every later misuse. Once an adequate substitute is in place, a claimant’s capacity may no longer be relevantly impaired. Where correction requires costs grossly disproportionate to the surviving disadvantage, a duty to secure complete correction does not follow. Where the later error would have been equally difficult to contest with the preserved evidence, the causal basis is missing. And the recipient’s own misconduct remains its responsibility. Multiple agents can owe different responses without making one wrongdoer the insurer of the entire informational environment.

The same limits rule out compulsory success. The fund must support a fair opportunity to challenge the misdescription, not force a recipient to believe Mara or force Mara to pursue correction. Assistance should be available on terms that respect her agency. A repair which requires a claimant to adopt the fund’s preferred narrative would restore administrative control at the expense of the capacity it was supposed to replace.

## 7. What remains after closure

Wrongful evidence loss need not leave an empty space. Institutions fill the space with outcomes: they pay, refuse, certify, close files, and issue reports. Those outputs can acquire an apparent authority exceeding what their grounds support. Even a fair remedy can then become a new obstacle to the person’s ability to explain what happened.

The appropriate response has two parts. Preserve the scope of the decision as it circulates, and, where the original breach still impairs a protected means of contestation, provide proportionate substitute support. The second obligation can outlast accurate initial communication and reach errors committed by others, while remaining limited by the disadvantage the breach created.

Mara can possess a real remedy without a favorable institutional finding about the contractor. She can also know something the fund failed to establish. What she may demand from the fund is neither universal agreement nor universal suspension of judgment. It is help ensuring that the institution’s resolution of what it owes her does not become a counterfeit resolution of what happened.
