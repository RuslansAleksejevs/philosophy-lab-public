# Sincere, Forgiven, and Still Unacceptable

When someone refuses an apology, what are they refusing? They might doubt that the speaker means it. They might deny the speaker's right to contact them. They might be unwilling to forgive. But they might also believe that the acknowledgment offered to them gets the wrong itself wrong. Regret can be genuine while its account of responsibility is false.

This last possibility needs care. An apology accompanied by a false statement is not necessarily a false or defective apology. A recipient might accept the apology and reject the accompanying statement. Before using such an exchange to distinguish acceptance from forgiveness, we must establish what the speaker included in the apology and what the recipient refused.

I develop that distinction through two versions of one hypothetical exchange. The same guilty person expresses the same remorse to someone who has already forgiven her. The same false sentence appears in both versions. What changes is whether it belongs to the offered acknowledgment or to a separate factual note. The comparison supports a limited claim: assessing the adequacy of an acknowledgment need not amount to assessing sincerity or reconsidering forgiveness. It also exposes a limit. Whether refusing that acknowledgment counts as rejecting an apology in its defining sense remains a further question about the boundaries of the act. A theory cannot answer that question merely by repeating that sincerity has been established.

## The disputed step

Oliver Hallich argues that interpersonal apologies necessarily include requests for forgiveness. His argument in section 2 starts from their susceptibility to acceptance and rejection. He considers alternative interpretations in terms of uptake, sincerity and standing. In discussing cognitive content, he treats rejection on that ground as collapsing into a judgment about sincere remorse. He also argues that people can hesitate after sincerity is settled. His claim concerns the speech act, not the apologizer's private wishes, and allows some other uses of rejection. These qualifications matter: an already forgiving recipient or a selfless speaker does not alone refute his account. [Hallich 2025, §§1–2 and note 13](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10892-025-09519-7).

The local question is whether the content of the offered acknowledgment can supply a further object of acceptance. Sincerity concerns whether the speaker owns the admission, regret and commitment she expresses. Accuracy concerns whether the admission gets the wrong right. A person can mean every word while accepting too much blame, too little blame, or an inaccurate description of blame she really deserves.

There are two obstacles to using that familiar distinction here. First, the error might prevent an apology from being performed at all. Second, the disputed statement might accompany an apology without belonging to it. The following case avoids making the apologizer innocent or unaware of her own wrong. The comparison then addresses the second obstacle without pretending to eliminate every possible account of speech acts.

## The facts held fixed

Ada organizes a small conference. She is responsible for checking the invitation list and negligently leaves Bea off it. Cal, her assistant, sees the omission before the invitations go out. He has an independent responsibility and an easy opportunity to correct it, but deliberately does nothing. Bea consequently misses the conference.

Ada's negligence is not diminished by Cal's conduct. Her duty to check the list was hers, and his failure does not excuse it. Nor does she acquire his distinct fault simply because she made the first mistake. Two people culpably contributed to one exclusion. Full responsibility for one's own failure is different from exclusive responsibility for the episode.

Bea has reliable evidence of both failures and has forgiven Ada for her entire culpable contribution. This forgiveness does not depend on a future apology. Bea no longer harbors resentment against Ada and has not forgiven Cal. Ada knows that she has been forgiven. She fully understands her own negligent omission, its significance for Bea and her own obligation to avoid repeating it. But she does not yet know that Cal saw the omission. Her false belief about him is an innocent mistake, not a further culpable concealment or refusal to investigate. Bea knows this too.

Ada voluntarily writes to Bea, who is willing to receive her letter. She offers an apology and answers a separate factual question about the invitation process. There is no condition requiring Bea to agree with everything before Ada will acknowledge her own wrong. In both versions, Ada sincerely believes everything she writes, feels the same regret and makes the same credible commitment to check future lists. Her standing to address Bea is unchanged.

Call the following passage the common apology:

> I was negligent in failing to check the invitation list. That excluded you from the conference and treated your participation as unimportant. I was wrong, and I am sorry. I accept full blame for my failure. I will check future lists carefully.

The disputed sentence is:

> I alone was at fault for your exclusion; Cal had no opportunity to intervene.

Ada uses two headings in her letter: “My apology” and “Separate factual note.” The note contains the accurate sentence “The invitations were sent on Friday.” Everything just described remains the same in both versions. Only the location of the disputed sentence changes.

## Inside the acknowledgment and outside it

In the **integrated version**, the common apology and the disputed sentence appear under “My apology.” The separate note states when the invitations were sent. Ada presents the exclusive allocation as part of her acknowledgment: this is the description of responsibility under which she offers to answer for the exclusion.

In the **detached version**, “My apology” contains the common apology alone. The disputed sentence appears in the separate note, after the sentence about Friday. Ada offers an acknowledgment limited to her negligence, alongside a further factual account that she sincerely but mistakenly believes.

The headings make the comparison explicit. Typography has no magical power to change the nature of an act. Their role is to communicate the scope Ada gives each part of her letter. Moving the sentence means moving it across that publicly marked boundary, not changing its font while leaving its conversational role untouched. If that boundary were unclear, the example would not decide the classification. No other change in guilt, forgiveness, regret, belief or commitment does the work.

In the detached version, Bea can reply:

> I accept your apology for your negligence. Your separate account of Cal is wrong, though. He saw the omission and chose to leave it.

This response need not be understood as grudging or incomplete acceptance of the apology. The offered apology admits Ada's actual failure without making an exclusive claim. Cal's failure is not something every adequate apology by Ada must mention. The factual account still needs correction, but no further acceptance of the apology need await that correction.

In the integrated version, Bea can instead reply:

> I believe your regret, and I have forgiven you. I cannot accept the apology you have offered as an adequate acknowledgment while it says you alone were at fault. Your own negligence is exactly what you say it was. But Cal also failed me.

What Bea refuses here is not Ada's regret, her commitment to change or her standing to apologize. It is the offered acknowledgment under its claimed scope: an admission that presents Ada's fault as exhausting the responsibility for Bea's exclusion. Bea can grant that a real apology for negligence has been expressed while declining to ratify that broader acknowledgment as adequate.

Ada then examines the evidence, retracts the exclusive allocation and recognizes Cal's independent failure. The common apology remains unchanged. Bea can accept the amended acknowledgment without granting any further forgiveness to Ada, forgiving Cal or discovering that Ada's earlier regret was counterfeit.

The comparison has a useful control. The complete letter contains the same false account of Cal in both versions. Bea's satisfaction with the whole explanation can therefore remain equally low. Nevertheless, in the detached version she can regard the apology, considered on its own, as adequate already. In the integrated version she has a reason to withhold that assessment. The proposed difference concerns the scope of the apology offered, not merely whether the whole letter is true.

## What makes the sentence relevant to the apology?

Putting a false sentence under an apology heading is not sufficient to make it part of the acknowledgment that matters. A mistaken remark about the weather would not acquire moral significance that way. Conversely, a qualification placed in a footnote could still determine what the speaker admits. We need an explanation of relevance as well as a boundary marker.

The explanation has three parts. Ada publicly includes the disputed sentence in the acknowledgment she offers. Its content specifies how she locates her own admitted failure within the wrong done to Bea. And deleting it changes the acknowledgment from an exclusive attribution of fault for the episode to an admission of Ada's own culpable contribution. The admission of negligence survives that deletion; the claim to account for all the fault does not. This is a change in what is acknowledged, even though the extent of Ada's actual culpability remains fixed.

The distinction between full and exclusive blame is crucial. Bea does not demand that Ada assume less responsibility for her negligence. Nor does she demand an apology on Cal's behalf. She refuses the addition that makes Ada's self-accusation an exoneration of someone else. Taking full responsibility for one's own wrong cannot, by itself, settle whether another person also wronged the victim.

Bea could still separate the acceptable portion in the integrated version. She could say, “I accept your apology for your negligence, but not your claim to have been the only person at fault.” That possibility is a strength of the analysis. It shows why the whole offer need not be accepted or rejected without qualification. Partial acceptance identifies the narrower acknowledgment she is willing to receive. It does not establish that she has accepted everything Ada included in the apology as offered.

Another possible repair brings the distinction into sharper focus. Ada might remove the disputed sentence from her apology and expressly narrow her acknowledgment to her own negligence, while continuing to believe and separately assert the false account of Cal. That would not correct the letter's factual error. It could nevertheless remove this particular reason for rejecting the apology's content. An adequate admission of one's own wrong need not contain an accurate account of every other participant. The question is whether a speaker has actually undertaken the broader acknowledgment.

This does not make acceptance a certificate of factual perfection. It tracks the part of an account offered as acknowledgment of the wrong. The same general issue arises with an invitation: a mistaken weather prediction may be incidental, whereas a false description of the proposed occasion can matter to accepting the invitation as offered. The analogy proves no theory of apologies. It only warns against treating separability as sufficient evidence of irrelevance.

## The strongest response: two acts all along

A defender can accept every practical judgment so far and still object to the proposed description. On this view, both letters contain the same apology for negligence and the same separate assertion about Cal. Their arrangement does not enlarge the apology itself. Bea accepts, or can reaffirm acceptance of, the apology in both versions; she rejects a combined apologetic explanation in one of them. Calling the combined offer unacceptable does not establish rejection of the apology in its defining sense.

This is a serious response. The phrase “as offered” cannot turn any collection of speech acts into a single apology. Nor can Bea settle the issue by choosing the words “I cannot accept.” If the claim were that the comparison decisively refutes a necessary request for forgiveness, this response would defeat the argument: a forgiveness-directed component could be present and accepted throughout.

There is nevertheless a reason to take the broader acknowledgment seriously as an object of apologetic assessment. An apology admits a wrong under some description. Its content is not exhausted by the speaker's report that she has the right feelings. Here the speaker herself makes exclusive responsibility part of that description. The recipient's response follows the communicated scope and the moral relevance of the sentence, rather than simply reacting to its occurrence nearby. In the detached version, by contrast, the recipient can accept the complete acknowledgment the speaker offers without adopting the exclusive claim. This is evidence for a distinction between two kinds of offer, although it is not a decisive theory of their individuation.

We should therefore distinguish three questions. Has an apology for negligence been performed? Has any forgiveness-directed request been accepted? Has the acknowledgment offered in that apology been accepted as adequate under its stated scope? The first two answers may be yes while the third is no. Describing the third response does not entitle us to rename it as the second or to deny that the second is also present.

What, positively, does the third response accomplish? Bea recognizes Ada's admission as an adequate way of answering for the wrong within the boundaries Ada has chosen. She can acknowledge the reparative value of that admission without requiring it to be withdrawn, narrowed or corrected. Refusal withholds that recognition. It is more than receipt or belief in sincerity, but less than approval of the whole letter, reconciliation, forgiveness of Cal or a decision that no dispute remains.

Acceptance in this sense also differs from privately believing a proposition. Bea already knows the relevant facts. What she subsequently recognizes is Ada's offered way of acknowledging them to her. The false exclusive account can be rejected as a proposition in either letter. Only in the integrated version must that account also be removed or corrected within the acknowledgment before Bea can accept it under its offered scope.

One may reserve “accepting the apology” for the forgiveness-directed response and call the other response “accepting its acknowledgment.” That terminology is coherent. But then the arrangement of the components and the distinction between these responses still need explaining. The sincerity of the common apology does not settle the adequacy of the acknowledgment. The substantive issue cannot be resolved merely by selecting the narrower terminology.

## Does the error mean that there was no apology?

There is a different, principled objection. Perhaps an acknowledgment with this error fails to qualify as an apology in the first place. Cécile Fabre's discussion is particularly close. She separates sincerity, understanding and commitment, and treats certain sincere but inadequately understanding statements as falling short of apologies. Her account of acceptance also attends to understanding the wrong, not merely felt remorse; she separately distinguishes acceptance from forgiveness. The broad distinction defended here is therefore already present in the literature. [Fabre, §§2–3 and 4.3](https://www.cecilefabre.com/uploads/1/3/6/4/13640562/the_duty_to_accept_apologies.pdf).

Ada's case puts pressure on the boundary rather than simply reproducing a case of failing to understand one's own wrong. She accurately identifies the conduct for which she is guilty, why it wronged Bea and what she must change. Her mistake adds an unjustified claim of exclusivity. The common apology is therefore available as an actual apology for negligence in both versions. What is defective is the expanded acknowledgment in the integrated version.

This does not establish that the expanded acknowledgment must itself be a genuine apology on every account. Someone may classify it as a defective attempt surrounding a successful narrower apology. If so, rejecting the expanded offer does not supply a counterexample to a claim about the acceptance of genuine apologies. We must not evade that limitation by assuming the disputed classification at the outset.

The comparison still identifies what such a response costs. We must distinguish the successful admission of Ada's negligence from the broader acknowledgment she offered, and explain which sense of acceptance applies to each. These are questions about content and the conditions for a successful act. They are not questions about whether Ada is pretending to be sorry. Narrowing the class of genuine apologies may be defensible, but it is a different answer from reducing the relevant content assessment to sincerity.

## The resulting limit on the argument

The case supports an independence claim, not an equivalence. Sincerity does not settle the adequacy of an offered acknowledgment. Nor does adequacy settle forgiveness. It does not follow that adequacy is a complete definition of acceptance. A recipient might judge an acknowledgment accurate and still decline contact, reconciliation or the request for forgiveness that may accompany it.

This distinction also explains one kind of hesitation. Suppose Bea is certain about Ada's negligence and sincere regret, and has forgiven that unchanged wrong, but is not yet sure whether Cal could have intervened. She can postpone assessing the integrated acknowledgment while checking that question. Her hesitation concerns the exclusive allocation, not how much blame Ada deserves or whether to forgive her. This is one intelligible source of delay; it does not explain every delay after an apology.

The original reduction of content assessment to sincerity thus leaves out a live distinction. The two-act response and the failed-apology response offer ways to protect a narrower account of acceptance, but neither vindicates that reduction. They instead introduce claims about which content belongs to an apology and which errors prevent one from being performed. Those claims need support of their own.

The larger conclusion about a necessary request for forgiveness remains open. An apology can have several functions; recognizing one does not remove another. Even in this case a request to reaffirm forgiveness could accompany the acknowledgment. Equally, a contextual use of acceptance does not establish that it is the most common or central use. The argument offered here supplies neither a survey of ordinary language nor an independent account of linguistic convention. Its result is that content assessment cannot be discarded simply because sincerity has been established. Whether it defeats the wider argument depends on the further account of act boundaries.

The case also survives the possibility that forgiveness waives a claim to receive an apology. Suppose Bea has waived that claim. Ada may still offer an acknowledgment voluntarily, and Bea need not approve whatever she offers. Refusing an inaccurate acknowledgment does not by itself reinstate a waived demand for remorse or threaten to withdraw forgiveness. Bea can accept a correction without acquiring a new grievance against Ada for the forgiven negligence.

None of this makes victims responsible for improving apologies. Bea's willingness to examine the letter and explain its fault is part of the hypothetical. Another recipient may reasonably decline that work. A request to assess an acknowledgment can itself impose an unwelcome burden, even when it does not ask for forgiveness. Separating the responses therefore gives no general vindication of apologizing.

## What this adds

The ingredients are established. Nick Smith's guidelines distinguish the factual record, blame, standing, harms and moral principles, including complications involving multiple offenders. Marko Ahteensuu considers mistaken blameworthiness and correction instead of acceptance. Cristina Roadevin gives disavowal and communicated respect a role beyond seeking a change in the victim's feelings. None of the broad distinctions used here should be claimed as new. [Smith 2017, criteria 1–7](https://c4ejournal.net/2017/11/16/nick-smith-guidelines-for-evaluating-apologies-and-remorse-in-criminal-contexts-summary-version-for-practitioners-2017-c4ej-9/); [Ahteensuu 2019, §3.1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10677-019-09993-7); [Roadevin 2017, §7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11406-017-9868-2).

The narrower contribution is a way to test an apparent counterexample. Keep the wrong, forgiveness, sincerity and the false proposition fixed. Change whether that proposition is adopted within the offered acknowledgment. Then ask whether the apology can already be accepted while the separate explanation remains disputed. This prevents a disagreement about the whole explanation from silently doing the work of a disagreement about the apology. It also reveals when the objection has reached its limit: a defensible narrower account of the act can preserve a forgiveness-directed theory.

Before inferring from an unacceptable apology that forgiveness has been withheld, we should identify the object of refusal. Sometimes the person has already been forgiven while her offered acknowledgment still needs correction. Whether we call that rejecting an apology or rejecting its defective expansion, the unfinished judgment concerns the acknowledgment's content. Calling the speaker sincere does not finish it.
