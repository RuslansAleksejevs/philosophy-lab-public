# Revocable Consent and the Cost of Preserving Choice

Rowan lends Ellis a spare pump to supply a small settlement with water. The loan is expressly terminable on a day's notice. Rowan makes no promise to continue it. Ellis has a reserve pump that could take over within that day. Ellis considers selling it to fund another useful project. Without the reserve or another arrangement, a replacement would take two weeks to obtain. If Rowan then requests the loaned pump's return, the residents will face a serious shortage unless the loan continues.

Suppose a short extension costs Rowan little, while a shortage would gravely harm innocent residents. Rowan may have a duty to extend the loan. Yet Ellis might wrong Rowan by creating this predicament. The problem concerns an earlier decision: whether Ellis can fairly obtain the benefit of selling the reserve by making Rowan responsible for continuity.

Cheap backup and valuable choice do not, by themselves, answer that question. They show that preserving choice may be worthwhile, not that Ellis must pay. The answer depends on whether the cost belongs to Ellis's proposed change, to a common undertaking, or to a predicament that neither party could reasonably prevent. These distinctions matter even when the physical equipment and its price remain the same.

I defend a limited claim: when one participant proposes a discretionary change to otherwise fair terms of cooperation, that participant normally must include the reasonable cost of preserving the other's significant retained choices in the price of the change. This is a conditional burden of pursuing the change, not an unlimited duty to finance another person's independence. Shared responsibility can support shared payment; sufficiently weighty competing interests can justify a different transition. The difficult case remains willing assistance with ordinary costs reimbursed. A decision need not be exercised to matter, but its value must still justify its protection.

## 1. Three different kinds of control

A person may retain the power to withdraw permission, the moral liberty to secure cessation, and the practical means to do so. These can come apart. Healey distinguishes consent's effect on a duty owed to a particular person from overall permissibility: independent duties can survive that permission (2019, p. 503). Dougherty allows that revoking consent can be effective even when it breaks a promise not to revoke (2014, p. 39). These distinctions leave room for Rowan's predicament. Withdrawal might end the original permission while an emergency requires renewed permission, or independently justifies temporary retention. The argument here does not choose between those analyses.

Call an exit *morally available* when securing cessation would violate no undefeated duty. This describes an option; it does not establish an entitlement to have it. Nor does moral availability guarantee practical access to equipment.

Three further matters differ: how stringent the duty to assist is, how burdensome compliance is, and how significant the blocked decision is. A stringent rescue duty can be cheap to discharge while obstructing an important choice. Conversely, a brief restriction on an insignificant option need not warrant costly prevention.

The emergency duty in the example rests on grave harm to residents, the absence of timely alternatives, and Rowan's low cost of a bounded extension. Dependence alone does not establish it. Nor does one such duty establish a permanent role: later demands must be assessed against their cumulative burden and available alternatives. Ellis's misconduct does not remove the residents' claims, but the residents' claims do not settle the accounts between Ellis and Rowan.

## 2. Why preserve a choice that remains unused?

Suppose Rowan wants the loan to continue and Ellis reimburses maintenance, wear, and ordinary inconvenience. Why spend anything on an option Rowan does not currently intend to exercise?

One answer concerns revision under uncertainty. Plans, needs, and judgments can change. A usable exit allows Rowan to respond without choosing between abandoning a new purpose and failing the residents. Reimbursement for continued lending does not necessarily provide access to the pump for another purpose. Covering foreseeable expenses cannot specify in advance every reason Rowan may later have for wanting the contribution to end.

This reason has limits. A remote possibility of changing a trivial preference cannot justify an expensive duplicate system. The option's importance depends on what decisions it enables, for how long, and under what uncertainty. It cannot be inferred from the strength of the rescue duty alone.

A second reason concerns authority over the scope of a contribution. Consider two arrangements with the same present use and payment. In one, continued use depends on Rowan's continuing decision, subject to an agreed transition. In the other, Ellis has arranged matters so that refusing continued use would wrong the residents. Rowan currently welcomes both outcomes. Nevertheless, Rowan's decision now determines less: a revised judgment would no longer suffice to release Rowan.

People need some protection for the distinction between assistance they undertake and assistance others can make it their duty to provide. Otherwise, offering a limited contribution can expose them to expansions selected by whoever controls the surrounding project. Their capacity to arrange several commitments becomes dependent on another person's decisions about safeguards. Communicating a limit helps people cooperate while retaining a role in deciding what they undertake. Welcoming the current result does not necessarily authorize someone else to enlarge that undertaking.

This supplies a reason to protect choice, not its price or payer. The interests of beneficiaries and reliance that Rowan encouraged can outweigh it. Imagine also that Rowan's preference cannot change during a brief extension, every practical interest is secured, and Rowan attaches no significance to personal control. The planning reason then disappears. A complaint about changing the arrangement might support disclosure or acknowledgment without supporting redundant equipment. The claim that every unchosen restriction requires physical restoration is too strong.

## 3. Establishing what has been shifted

It would be circular to condemn departure from “responsible reliance” if responsible reliance simply means preserving Rowan's exit. The assessment must begin with facts that do not already contain the verdict.

First, specify each contribution and its limits. Express terms matter, but so do assurances, the disclosed purpose, and preparations each participant encouraged. A one-day notice term is evidence against an indefinite undertaking. It is not a waiver of residents' claims. Ellis's contribution has limits too: management or ownership of a reserve does not make Ellis a permanent guarantor.

Second, identify feasible alternatives and their full costs. Keeping an existing pump has opportunity costs as well as maintenance costs. Selling it might fund a substantial benefit. A replacement must be timely and dependable, not merely conceivable. Ending the project can also involve serious costs, especially where participants invited irreversible reliance. The fact that an alternative is physically possible does not make it a reasonable demand.

Third, separate costs already belonging to the cooperative arrangement from costs added by a proposed departure. The appropriate comparison is not a world where nobody ever helped. Nor may the successful creation of dependence establish its own justification. Compare ways of continuing or ending the actual undertaking that respect its independently defensible allocation of burdens. Whether that allocation is defensible depends on both parties' contributions, opportunities, needs, and invited reliance, not solely on their words.

Suppose the present arrangement is fair for a limited period; Ellis can keep it without serious sacrifice; selling is an additional project choice; and a modest reservation fee would preserve Rowan's significant one-day option. Why should Ellis normally pay that fee if Ellis sells?

The reason is that Ellis is proposing a different package: an additional benefit for Ellis together with a new restriction on Rowan. If Rowan must either accept the restriction or buy it off, Ellis can obtain the benefit on terms that Rowan never offered. Rowan's temporary duty to the residents makes refusal costly, but supplies no additional reason why Rowan should subsidize Ellis's new project. Charging the avoidable transition cost to the proposal leaves Ellis free to pursue it, postpone it, or seek a new agreement. Charging Rowan gives the proposer unilateral power to enlarge the other's contribution whenever an emergency duty can be predictably activated.

This argument does not rely on a separate promise by Ellis to keep that particular reserve. It relies on fair terms for continuing to use Rowan's limited contribution while changing the surrounding arrangement. Nor does it derive liability merely from causal control: a person can control a predicament without owing its entire solution. The asymmetry here is between pursuing a new discretionary project and maintaining the other's previously defensible contribution, not between a superior giver and an inferior recipient. If Rowan proposed an extra use that imposed new transition costs on Ellis beyond what Ellis fairly undertook, the same reasoning would apply to Rowan.

The fairness qualification does substantive work. An allocation that leaves Ellis bearing escalating common costs while Rowan insists on a costless exit may need revision. If both contributions were equally limited, calling Ellis “the organizer” would not establish that Rowan's limit wins. The opening story therefore supplies grounds for inquiry, not a verdict before these facts are specified.

## 4. Who pays in the intermediate cases?

The following cases are hypothetical. Monetary amounts fix feasible alternatives; they are not measurements of autonomy or moral worth. Unless a case says otherwise, Rowan willingly continues lending, ordinary expenses are reimbursed, both parties have adequate resources, and neither encouraged an expansion of the other's contribution. A permanent supplier will take over after a twenty-eight-day pilot. Keeping the reserve until then is a fair continuation of the existing arrangement, including its opportunity costs. Neither participant is committed beyond the pilot.

Rowan has no present intention to withdraw. Nevertheless, access to the pump within a day matters for revisable commitments over these weeks; a two-week loss of access is a meaningful but moderate restriction. A reliable outside service can preserve that access through the pilot. The quoted price includes protection against both pumps becoming unavailable. The service can meet its commitment without forcing an uncommitted volunteer to remain.

**An inexpensive unilateral change.** Ellis wants to sell the reserve for a recreational improvement. An adequate reservation costs twenty units; paying it only reduces the funds available for the improvement. Ellis should pay if proceeding. The fee is part of obtaining the additional benefit while retaining the existing loan on fair terms. Rowan's ability to afford it does not give Ellis a claim to that subsidy. There need be no request to withdraw: the fee protects an already significant option.

**An important, nonessential project.** Keep the same option but raise the reservation price to 120 units. Ellis would use the sale proceeds for a worthwhile professional course. The fee would require Ellis to give up another planned discretionary expenditure, but would not prevent the course or threaten housing, health, or livelihood. The cost is now appreciable and the benefit serious. Still, Ellis should normally bear the whole incremental fee if selling. A worthy project is a reason to pursue it; it does not by itself entitle Ellis to make Rowan partly finance it. Sharing merely because both care about the outcome would turn Rowan's continuing generosity into a claim on further generosity.

This verdict has a cost: Ellis must sacrifice something real. Its justification is not that Rowan's option produces more welfare than the course. Rather, keeping to fair limits normally constrains how people may finance worthwhile plans. That constraint is defeasible, but the mere fact that Ellis benefits substantially does not defeat it. Rowan can voluntarily contribute or accept a longer notice period. Ellis cannot treat either response as already owed.

**A jointly chosen departure.** Hold the price at 120 and keep both parties' interests and means comparable. Now Rowan and Ellis jointly request the sale for a shared improvement. Each has equal influence over the decision and comparable benefit from it; each knows the new dependence it would create. Neither agrees to surrender the one-day option, and that option is to be preserved for both. Their existing contributions are otherwise balanced. Here the safeguard belongs to their common proposal. With a divisible cost and no relevant asymmetry, equal shares of sixty are justified: assigning the entire cost to either would privilege the other's identical claim to have the common project supported. Ellis's ability to arrange the service makes Ellis a sensible coordinator, not its sole payer.

Inviting a change does not mean accepting every consequence or waiving all limits. Rowan can retain a claim to an adequate transition while owing a share of its cost. Different benefits, contributions, or resources could justify unequal shares; the equal split is warranted by the stated symmetry, not by a general rule that cooperation always divides bills in half.

**An unchosen common cost.** Suppose instead that no sale is proposed. An external service that previously secured both parties' exits withdraws despite appropriate planning, and replacing it costs 120. Both parties expressly limited their help; neither caused the failure, assumed its financial risk, or encouraged asymmetric reliance. Both have equally significant retained options and comparable resources and prior contributions. Equal sharing is again the appropriate starting point. The fee is a new common cost of keeping their limited undertaking workable. The person who operates the supply cannot automatically be required to purchase everyone else's freedom. If one has greater resources or a stronger independently grounded responsibility for the residents, the shares can change.

**Unequal burdens without a basic-needs emergency.** Consider that common-cost case with the same price and equally important exit interests, but different resources. Paying sixty would now force Ellis to abandon a unique training opportunity crucial to a long-planned change of occupation, although Ellis's present job and basic needs are secure. Rowan can cover 120 by forgoing a minor convenience. Suppose Rowan's total prior contribution is not already disproportionately large. Rowan has reason to bear most, potentially all, of the fee: equal nominal shares would protect equal interests through sharply unequal sacrifices. This conclusion does not follow merely from Rowan being richer. It follows from a common cost, no special assignment of responsibility, comparable existing contributions, and the disproportionate burden of an equal split. A rule that protected only basic needs would miss this case.

Return to the unilateral sale, however. Making Ellis's course more important does not automatically convert its costs into a common bill. Before shifting payment, we must ask whether postponing the sale, selecting another course, seeking assistance, or accepting a bounded transition avoids a comparably serious loss. If every feasible way to preserve Rowan's moderate option would impose a grave, lasting setback on Ellis, requiring full preservation can be disproportionate even outside a medical emergency. The appropriate result might be temporary restriction, voluntary funding, or a separate duty of assistance grounded in the parties' relative needs. It is not an entitlement generated by Ellis's wish to improve the project. The same price can therefore support different answers; “important” by itself does not select one.

**An excessive safeguard.** Finally, let the least costly dependable reservation cost thousands, while Rowan faces only a brief, low-significance restriction and a fixed, safe transfer after two weeks. No party has undertaken unusually stringent protection. Purchasing the option may be unreasonable regardless of who could technically pay. Advance disclosure, a bounded transition, and appropriate reimbursement may suffice. Conversely, if Rowan now needs the pump to avert equally grave harm elsewhere, the assumption that continuing this loan is cheap enough to be required fails. One must reassess the conflicting duties rather than invoke the earlier emergency verdict unchanged.

These comparisons yield no universal tariff. They do explain changes of payer without treating a valuable option as an invoice. Incremental costs generated by a unilateral optional project normally attach to its proposer; common costs call for a fair division; and the importance of the protected option and each party's sacrifice constrain both rules. Neither role nor chronological first possession settles the matter.

## 5. Adequate means, repair, and timing

Preserving choice is not synonymous with owning duplicate equipment. A reliable replacement service, an orderly transfer, or an informed revision of commitments can be sufficient. Money set aside works only if it can actually secure timely replacement. A promise to pay for additional wear does something different: it compensates a burden without necessarily making the decision available.

There is a further test. Suppose Sage offers a substitute pump but can retrieve it only by leaving the residents without water. If Sage has not fairly undertaken that commitment, the arrangement merely moves the disputed burden. An adequate safeguard must provide a defensible transition across the whole arrangement. This need not eliminate every risk; ordinary reliability standards must reflect the harm at stake and the cost of reducing risk. A detailed general theory of acceptable risk is beyond this argument.

Organizing a transition, paying for it, and repairing an earlier wrong are separate duties. Ellis may be best placed to contact suppliers even where Rowan owes part of the fee. Conversely, after an unjustified unilateral sale, Ellis has an additional reason to bear avoidable repair costs that Ellis created. That reason concerns the wrongful change, not every later expense of supplying water. Legitimate reliance that Rowan invited and later independent changes remain relevant. If proper maintenance could not prevent a breakdown, no wrongful-sale liability arises simply because Ellis managed the equipment.

Timing follows the protected interest. Suppose on day zero Ellis knows that selling would remove Rowan's one-day option. A replacement ordered then arrives on day fourteen; an order placed only when Rowan objects on day ten arrives on day twenty-four. An early order reduces the restriction, although it does not preserve the one-day option during the initial gap. Where that option deserves protection throughout, Ellis should retain the reserve until delivery or arrange an adequate bridge. Where only a bounded fourteen-day transition is reasonable, ordering on day zero may suffice. The procurement delay alone does not establish the morally acceptable delay.

After a wrongful sale, discovery and complaint also differ. Ellis should start proportionate repair when the restriction is, or with reasonable care should be, apparent. Rowan need not manufacture a wish to withdraw. Waiting can prolong an existing loss even when help continues willingly. Ignorance can matter to blame, and the cost of acceleration matters to the remedy; neither makes the date of a complaint the automatic starting point.

An agreed change should ordinarily be sought before removing the safeguard, when refusal can leave the existing arrangement intact. Later consent may validly authorize continued use without approving the earlier sale or waiving a repair claim. Those are distinct objects of agreement. The emergency does not automatically invalidate later consent, but a willingness to protect residents cannot simply be read as retrospective approval of the predicament.

## 6. What this account claims

Several parts of the argument have clear predecessors. Keller describes pressure through duties to clients and students that can make resistance to additional demands morally difficult (2018, “Blackmailing a Fiduciary”). His examples already expose how one person can exploit another's duties to beneficiaries. Negligence rather than deliberate manipulation does not, by itself, make the present argument new.

Spafford's moral tyranny constraint evaluates foreseeable, unilateral, discretionary losses under full moral compliance (2023, §§2.1–2.2). Its measure of advantage need not exclude control. His response to objections permits adjustments across a system of claims, not merely reimbursement of ordinary expenses. In his rescue example, one person refuses to save an already drowning child; another can owe the rescue while having a compensation claim against the person who refused (§2.5). The refusal transfers the burden; it does not create the original danger. His note 25 addresses the complication of impossible compensation. A full application could therefore recognize the same lost choice and support an early remedy. Comparing this essay only with payment for pump wear would understate that approach.

Rouméas explicitly recognizes the value of an exit one does not exercise and distinguishes affordable exit from fair allocation of its costs (2023, pp. 162, 164–166). She leaves more specific distributive criteria to contextual accounts (p. 166; n. 9, p. 174). The proposal here is one such limited account. It asks what kind of cost a safeguard represents before deciding who pays. It does not claim that concern for unused exit or advance protection originates here.

Nor should every imposed loss generate a right to payment. O'Brien contrasts wanton cost shifting with permissible self-protection and challenges the move from a frustrated preference to a compensation claim (2026, §§II–IV). The distinction between discretionary project costs and serious competing needs is therefore essential. Rowan's claim concerns a defensible limit on Rowan's own contribution, not a general interest in preventing Ellis from acting differently.

Consent nevertheless adds useful specificity. It helps identify the contribution, notice period, and reliance invited. It does not supply the whole foundation or necessarily add a distinct wrong. Someone made the only available rescuer without having lent anything could have a similar complaint. Likewise, Goodin's chapter abstract treats reasonable detrimental reliance as a reason against revocation and for compensation, and uncompensatable reliance as a reason for irrevocability (2024). That is a reason to examine what Rowan encouraged, not to presume every reliance reasonable.

Finally, effective permission and fair treatment need not coincide. Liberto's discussion allows some exploitative releases to be effective on the authority accounts of promising she examines, while other accounts yield different conclusions (2016, pp. 160–162). Her relevant cases also distinguish taking advantage of existing constraints from introducing them. This does not settle whether Ellis obtains valid consent after selling the reserve. It supports caution about collapsing continued permission, fair renegotiation, and repair into one question.

The claimed contribution is thus an argued allocation within a limited form of cooperation, not a new general category of wrong or a demonstrated historical first. A fuller application of existing theories may reach the same verdicts. The practical gain is to explain why payment is owed in the unilateral project case, shared in the symmetrical common-cost cases, and reconsidered when protection imposes disproportionate sacrifice.

Revocable permission does not insulate a person from all duties arising through cooperation. It can help define a limited contribution whose enlargement requires justification. Respecting that limit sometimes requires action while assistance remains welcome. But the bill belongs neither automatically to the person with the spare pump nor automatically to the person who wants an exit. Its allocation depends on the proposed change, fair prior contributions, responsibility for new dependence, and the sacrifices needed to preserve a significant choice.
