# Knowing the Experience You Changed

An observer attends to a faint inner image and finds it vivid. Did she discover imagery already present, or produce imagery by looking? Another observer tries to hold a passing thought still and finds nothing of its movement. Was that movement absent, or did the attempt destroy it? Suppose both observers accurately describe the experience available when inspection ends. Their historical questions remain open.

This essay defends a limited thesis: interference does not have one uniform consequence for retrospective judgment. When a specified feature can disappear but cannot arise across the relevant transition, its later presence can establish earlier presence. When it can arise but cannot disappear, its later absence can establish earlier absence. Each conclusion needs a warranted restriction in its own direction, not complete preservation of experience.

These are elementary deductive relations, not new laws of introspection. Their philosophical use is to distinguish an error about inspected experience from an error in carrying a truthful report backwards. I apply that distinction to a concrete dispute about coached visual inspection and to inventories assembled from successive reports. The argument does not establish that an actual human routine satisfies either restriction. Nor does it require exceptionless restrictions for every kind of retrospective evidence.

The problem has close predecessors. James distinguishes relatively stable imagery from transitions which attempts to arrest may destroy; he also describes present experience as retaining its immediate past. These are complementary observations, not a universal verdict against reflection. (James 1890, chapter IX, pp. 240–243 and 243–245, [text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin9.htm).) Schwitzgebel explicitly separates experience found through inquiry from experience before inquiry, proposes immediate retrospection, and considers timing and memory errors. (Schwitzgebel 2007, dated author manuscript, §§5–6 and 10, [text](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/ExpWOAttn060301.htm).) Schooler and Schreiber distinguish experience from its explicit characterization and identify detection, transformation and substitution as possible failures. (Schooler and Schreiber 2004, abstract, [record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233527104_Experience_Meta-consciousness_and_the_Paradox_of_Introspection).)

My proposal is a reconstruction within this established problem. It makes explicit which change matters to which inference and what additional assumptions an inventory requires. It does not claim priority for these distinctions or the examples below.

## 1. Two directions, two restrictions

“Experience” means conscious experience: seeing colour, feeling pressure, or entertaining an auditory image. “Inspection” means trying to attend to and judge a feature of that experience. This usage does not make inspection the only route to self-knowledge.

Fix a feature F and two intervals, I₀ before inspection and I₁ during the later judgment. Initially, let F₀ mean that F occurs somewhere in I₀, and F₁ that F occurs somewhere in I₁. Use the same property and the same temporal quantifier on both sides. “Some imagery” must not silently become “vivid imagery” or “imagery outside attention.”

Grant accurate knowledge of F₁ or ¬F₁. This is a substantial concession. Failing to find an image is not automatically knowing that none occurs. The concession isolates a different problem: even perfect local detection does not itself answer the earlier question.

A later positive result entails earlier presence only if the relevant histories exclude absence followed by presence. Call this **no gain**:

**F₁ → F₀.**

A later negative result entails earlier absence only if the relevant histories exclude presence followed by absence. Call this **no loss**:

**F₀ → F₁**, hence **¬F₁ → ¬F₀.**

“No gain” permits loss. An earlier image may disappear, but an image that survives cannot have come from earlier absence. “No loss” permits gain. Inspection may create images, but if an earlier image would remain, later absence rules out earlier presence. Requiring both restrictions would demand preservation of the feature's presence or absence. Each deduction requires only one.

The conditions describe a class of histories, not an accidentally true implication in one trial. Specify the procedure, feature, population, time span and circumstances. A random image generator could happen to follow an earlier image on one occasion; the two token implications would then be true. That coincidence does not establish either restriction as a property of the method.

The class must also be epistemically appropriate. A restriction which lacks support cannot become a warranted premise merely by receiving a name. Conversely, ordinary knowledge need not await the exclusion of every imaginable history. Relevant alternatives depend on the question and available grounds. An alternative in which inspection creates the disputed experience is especially relevant when the dispute concerns whether attention is necessary for that experience.

These restrictions cover the whole transition between the stated targets, including spontaneous changes, not just changes caused by inspection. Showing that inspection never creates imagery would not exclude an image beginning for another reason. A stronger, trajectory-wide prohibition on every onset would suffice for no gain, but is not required by the endpoint formula. The formula excludes only the histories incompatible with this deduction.

This is not a complete theory of knowledge. The deductions do not answer questions about luck, warrant for the restrictions or misleading evidence. They concern a route whose experiential premise is later presence or absence of the same feature. Direct memory, retained records and awareness of an extended episode may offer other routes.

Neither restriction is necessary for strong probabilistic evidence. Consider a stipulated model with P(F₀) = 0.5, P(F₁ | F₀) = 0.9 and P(F₁ | ¬F₀) = 0.1. Perfect observation of F₁ yields P(F₀ | F₁) = 0.9; perfect observation of ¬F₁ yields P(¬F₀ | ¬F₁) = 0.9. Gains and losses are both possible. This is not human data or a proof of knowledge. It shows why requirements for entailment must not be confused with requirements for evidence. A defensible stochastic transition model can support a historical claim without guaranteeing it.

## 2. The property matters

Consider an imagined routine which sometimes suppresses an image but never introduces one under specified circumstances. A surviving image licenses the positive deduction; a negative result does not license the corresponding negative deduction. Reverse the routine's capacities and the pattern reverses. These possibilities show why the bare label “reactive” does not settle an inference's value. They do not identify validated human methods.

Restrictions for coarse and fine features are not generally ordered. Let G be some imagery and F square imagery. A procedure can create circles from nothing while never introducing squares. It violates no gain of G but can satisfy no gain of F. Another can transform circles into squares while always preserving some imagery. It satisfies no gain of G but violates no gain of F. Although the square conclusion is more specific, its transition premise is not uniformly harder to satisfy.

This prevents two shortcuts. First, a bridge for the existence of imagery does not automatically transport its details. Inspection might preserve a faint image while supplying shape and colour. Earlier imagery and earlier square imagery need separate grounds. Second, failure of the coarse bridge does not automatically discredit every fine-feature inference. Which transitions are excluded must be checked for the actual feature.

Attentional status creates another difficulty. If F means “imagery outside focal attention,” attending to an image may remove F simply by changing its status. An accurate negative judgment about unattended imagery during inspection would then be a poor basis for earlier absence. Accuracy about a property defined partly by non-attention cannot repair a procedure which changes that very property.

The lesson is not to replace fine claims with coarse ones whenever possible. It is to avoid transferring a restriction from one feature to another without an argument.

## 3. A concrete payoff: coached visual inspection

Schwitzgebel's discussion of visual clarity supplies an actual inferential setting. He describes observers successively looking at objects, finding each clear, and concluding that they are clear simultaneously. He then proposes separating introspective attention from direct looking; many observers revise their earlier description. His account explicitly allows that the revision could itself be mistaken. (Schwitzgebel 2008, penultimate author manuscript, §vi, [text](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/Naive070907.htm).)

Consider what each observation would establish even if its local accuracy were granted. Let C concern a specified degree of experienced detail in a region at an earlier target time. A naive observer's accurate later finding of C establishes earlier C deductively only through no gain of C. If looking directly at the region supplies detail, that bridge fails. Better detection of the detail now present would not repair the historical inference.

Now grant that a coached observer accurately finds ¬C while keeping direct looking away from that region. To project that absence backwards requires no loss of C over the relevant transition. Evidence that the naive routine could introduce detail does not establish this different restriction. A coached routine might prevent an addition; it might also remove detail previously present. Those are different possibilities.

This is a diagnosis of argumentative burdens, not a verdict that coaching is unreliable. A bare possibility of loss does not defeat the coached judgment. Grounds about ordinary visual trajectories, the effects of the routine or retained evidence could favor it. But showing the first method's relevant defect is not itself showing the second method's historical reliability. The asymmetry identifies the missing premise precisely: not “coaching is nonreactive,” but “for this detail claim and target, the coached negative result reliably excludes earlier detail.” A probabilistic version may be sufficient for a modest conclusion.

A narrower claim can avoid the historical burden. The coached observer may be right that experience under the coached routine lacks the specified detail. That alone need not contradict a naive observer's truthful description of experience under direct looking. Conflict arises when both reports are extended to the same kind of uninspected episode. The framework therefore changes what must be defended before disagreement is attributed to failed local introspection. It does not explain away genuine disagreement or refute general introspective pessimism.

Phillips's cueing discussion offers a related warning. He distinguishes an informational account of later performance from the assumptions connecting it to earlier consciousness, and considers both production and improved retention interpretations of postcueing. (Phillips 2018, final-version §§3–4, [article](https://www.ianbphillips.com/files/papers/the-methodological-puzzle.pdf); corresponding accepted-manuscript §§2–3, [text](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/52810395/Methodological_Puzzle_Final_Version.pdf).) My premise is different: I grant a later phenomenal judgment rather than merely informational success. That stronger premise still does not decide the earlier phenomenal question.

Suppose a persistence account and a production account both permit the observed later image. Compatibility with both does not show that the report favors neither. They could assign it different probabilities. If, under the same background evidence, they assign the report equal positive likelihoods, observing it leaves their probability ratio unchanged. Deterministic models that predict the same report with certainty are a special case. In either case, increasing confidence in that shared later result need not resolve their historical disagreement. Additional evidence must bear on what distinguishes them.

## 4. Memory, extended experience and a stronger objection

The observer may object that the premise is too thin. She experiences an image as continuing, not simply as present. That adds evidence. A model predicting a bare abrupt onset may now fit less well. But two judgments remain distinct: that the episode has a character of continuation, and that this character accurately reveals an earlier episode. The first does not entail the second without a suitable connection.

A reliable retained presentation could provide that connection even if the original feature disappears. Let R₁ be the later record. A positive deduction would then use R₁ → F₀, not F₁ → F₀. A transformed or destroyed experience can leave informative evidence. Conversely, an unchanged feature can remain while its history becomes inaccessible. Preservation of an object and preservation of information about it are different achievements.

There is also an alternative to backward inference from a later slice. Phillips develops an account on which some extended periods of experience are fundamental relative to their instantaneous parts. (Phillips 2011, §4.3, pp. 397–399, [text](https://www.ianbphillips.com/files/papers/perception-and-iconic-memory.pdf).) If accurate awareness already covers an episode's earlier phase, the historical claim is within the granted warrant. My proposed bridge is then unnecessary. This is a conditional epistemic application, not something established merely by the metaphysics of temporal extension.

Nothing in the interval notation makes experience a collection of instantaneous atoms. The question is whether the granted judgment's warrant covers the disputed earlier phase. If it does not, invoking temporal unity cannot substitute for an account of that warrant. If it does, insisting on a later-to-earlier inference would misdescribe the route.

Blackmore presents a stronger challenge. Her 2002 discussion connects inspection to retrospective constructions and notes that practice can change experience itself. Her earlier draft of a 2012 chapter also contrasts inquiry about the present with inquiry about the immediate past. These are close antecedents, not problems first identified here. (Blackmore 2002, especially the discussion of retrospective streams and “Looking harder,” [text](https://www.susanblackmore.uk/articles/there-is-no-stream-of-consciousness/); Blackmore 2012, earlier chapter draft, “Observations,” [text](https://www.susanblackmore.uk/chapters/turning-on-the-light-to-see-how-the-darkness-looks/).)

Her stronger rejection of a determinate probe-independent stream does not follow from this essay's argument. Failure of inspection to discriminate between histories is compatible with both histories having determinate truth conditions. One can hold that there was an earlier image, or that there was none, while holding that the later finding alone settles neither. Thus the inspection problem supplies a reason for caution about knowledge, not by itself a reason to deny the earlier fact.

This reply does not refute all Blackmore's arguments. It marks a substantive assumption of my own: for the specified questions, determinate earlier and later feature claims are available. If that assumption fails, the Boolean reconstruction does not answer those questions. The framework cannot use its inability to identify an earlier state as proof that such a state exists.

## 5. Truthful reports, false inventories

Schwitzgebel's successive-clarity example is already a direct antecedent of the positive-inventory failure. A search can introduce each feature as it becomes the target and remove it as attention moves elsewhere. Every local report can be true while the alleged simultaneous collection never existed. My point is not to discover that pattern anew, but to state its temporal requirements and consider its negative counterpart.

Consider two possible features, an imagined tone A and an imagined shape B. Initially both are present. The first inspection removes A while B remains; the observer accurately reports ¬A. A then returns before B disappears; at the second inspection the observer accurately reports ¬B. The sequence can pass through (A,B), (¬A,B), (A,B), (A,¬B). Both local absence reports are true, yet both features were initially present and they were never jointly absent during the inspections.

A supposed finding of emptiness can therefore be assembled from true local negatives. This is a countermodel, not evidence about the frequency or causes of actual emptiness reports. It also shows why switching from positive to negative inspection does not automatically avoid an inventory error: gain threatens the relevant positive deductions, while loss threatens their negative counterparts.

The inventory's temporal target must now be explicit. Let Fᵢ(t*) mean that feature i is present at one shared earlier target time t*. Let Lᵢ mean its presence at its respective later inspection. Over a warranted class of histories, positive bridges **Lᵢ → Fᵢ(t*)** for every listed i, together with the corresponding local positives, entail that all the listed features were present at t*. Negative bridges **Fᵢ(t*) → Lᵢ**, together with all the local negatives, entail their joint absence at t*. These are sufficient deductions about the same target, not just separate earlier occurrences.

Using a target time need not make instantaneous experience fundamental. Such facts may depend on surrounding extended experience. A target interval can also be used, but then its predicate must match the desired conclusion. There are at least three different claims:

- Each feature occurred somewhere in the interval.
- All features occurred together somewhere in the interval.
- Every feature was present throughout the interval.

The first does not entail the second:

**∀i ∃t ∈ I₀ Fᵢ(t)** does not entail **∃t ∈ I₀ ∀i Fᵢ(t)**.

A tone only in the first half and a shape only in the second make the difference plain. Bridging each later finding to occurrence somewhere in I₀ does not repair it. Bridges to persistence throughout I₀ would establish co-presence there, but are stronger than what an occurrence inventory needs. One must choose the historical question before choosing the bridge.

For negatives, absence of every listed feature throughout I₀ does establish their joint absence throughout that interval. It still does not establish that experience was empty. An unlisted bodily sensation may remain. A total emptiness conclusion needs a further premise: the tested domain covers every conscious feature whose presence would defeat emptiness. Directional transport cannot supply completeness.

The exact absence countermodel is only a toy construction; no claim of unprecedentedness is warranted. Its useful consequence is the separation of three tasks often compressed into “taking an inventory”: accurate local judgment, transport to a common historical target, and coverage of the claimed domain. Success at one does not establish success at the others.

## 6. What could warrant the bridge?

A transition restriction is not justified merely by repeating the observation it is meant to interpret. Finding imagery after inspection a hundred times could show consistent discovery or consistent production. Agreement between several measures also fails to discriminate if the same disputed assumption explains all of them equally well.

That does not require a wholly independent certificate of consciousness before inquiry begins. Block's proposed alternative to such a demand is inference to the best explanation across psychological and neural evidence; he also considers modeling errors through fallible measurements. (Block 2007, copyedited prepublication target article, §8, printed pp. 12–13, [text](https://www.mind-and-brain.de/fileadmin/downloads/Lectures/Block-09282006.pdf).) A transition model and claims about earlier experience could likewise receive support together. The directional reconstruction specifies what a model must support for the inference in question; it does not establish that explanatory support is impossible.

Different measures are useful when they impose genuinely different constraints. An ongoing task, a retained presentation and earlier reports might support a restricted account of what a checking routine changes. Each is fallible: performance may occur without the disputed experience, retained presentations may be reconstructed, and an earlier report may itself interfere. Those risks do not automatically cancel converging evidence. They demand examination of rival explanations rather than multiplication of reports which leave the same rival untouched.

Training poses a transfer problem as well as a detection problem. An observer can improve at recognizing fine differences under a trained routine. That achievement does not itself establish what a novice experienced before adopting it, or what the expert experiences outside it. Training can improve discrimination, alter experience, or do both. Calibration with attended imagery does not automatically transfer to wholly unattended imagery, because attention is precisely the difference at issue.

No actual calibration sufficient to settle these disputes has been established here. The essay consequently does not show that coached observers, novices or advocates of rich or thin experience are right. Its practical reach remains limited: it identifies the transition and transfer claims on which their historical uses of locally accurate reports depend. A more complete account would need justified exceptionless restrictions, defensible probabilities, reliable retained evidence or some other route covering the earlier target.

Ordinary people need not formulate these principles before reporting a lingering headache or remembering breakfast. Knowledge need not wait for an explicit theory. The demand is argumentative: when a philosophical dispute concerns whether inspection creates or removes its object, local accuracy alone cannot be offered as though it settled that disputed transition. Implicit grounds may suffice in ordinary cases; challenged extensions of authority still need defense.

The resulting position respects first-person reports without automatically accepting the histories built from them. An observer may know exactly what she inspected while lacking warrant about what preceded inspection. Conversely, a process may alter experience and still inform us about its earlier state. For the specified later-feature route, possible loss is compatible with a positive deduction, and possible gain with a negative one. Other routes and probabilistic evidence widen the options.

“Does inspection change experience?” is therefore only the first question. The more useful next question is: “Which historical conclusion, about which feature and time, would this change undermine?” Answering it does not solve every problem of introspection. It prevents current accuracy, historical accuracy and completeness from being mistaken for the same achievement.
