# When the Record Ends: How Rebuttable Decisions Become Final

An institution may be justified in making a decision and also justified in destroying its records. It does not follow that it may continue applying the decision on the same procedural terms. If the continuing imposition was justified partly by its remaining open to correction, destruction can turn a rebuttable arrangement into effective finality. That transition requires justification even when nobody has acquired a new reason to doubt the original verdict.

The obligation at issue is specific. The institution must permit an appropriate challenge to the *terms of continuation* without requiring the affected person first to disprove the historical finding using the unavailable material. It need not reopen the historical question, reverse the finding, or relax its standard of proof. A sufficiently justified policy adopted in advance can already answer the challenge. But the original verdict and the permission to delete, considered separately, cannot themselves settle it.

I call this the principle against automatic finality. Its significance lies in identifying an easily obscured exercise of power: preserving a restrictive classification while eliminating the conditions under which it was legitimately treated as rebuttable. The remedy can be better preservation. It can also be a limit on what the institution continues to do with the classification.

## 1. Three arrangements, one verdict

A cooperative controls access to a unique workshop. After a fair investigation, its board finds that Lea deliberately damaged shared equipment. The investigators were competent, Lea had an opportunity to respond, and their report provides strong evidence. The cooperative imposes an additional charge whenever she uses the workshop.

Consider three ways to administer such a charge. Under **Limited Finality**, it ends after three years. A short initial period is available for factual challenges; the charge then runs its course. Under **Continuing Review**, it has no fixed expiry, but Lea can seek competent review of the original finding. The investigation's recordings are retained because examining the sequence of actions, rather than merely reading the report, is the only feasible way of substantiating certain relevant errors. Under **Unreviewable Continuation**, the charge has no fixed expiry and no practically available route to correct those errors.

The names identify arrangements, not verdicts about their justice. Limited Finality can be objectionable if the initial process is poor or three years is too long. Continuing Review can be objectionable if access to the recordings is prohibitively expensive. But the differences are morally intelligible. A fixed end limits how long an undetected error can burden someone. Continuing review provides another kind of protection: an opportunity to identify and correct errors that an initial investigation reasonably missed. Unreviewable Continuation supplies neither protection for the affected class of errors.

Suppose the cooperative's defensible reasons for selecting Continuing Review depend on this protection. A substantial, indefinitely recurring charge would not otherwise be adequately justified by the report's reliability and the workshop's interests. Nothing here assumes a promise of perpetual hearings. The point is that a feature of the procedure helps justify the duration of the imposition. Lea may lose every challenge she brings, yet the availability of correction can still matter to whether this arrangement is fair.

Now suppose continued retention creates a serious danger to other people shown in the recordings. No secure substitute is available, and deletion is morally required. The board retains its accurate report. To isolate the procedural issue, stipulate that deletion supplies no new information about the event, the investigators' honesty, or the quality of their original assessment. The surviving report warrants the same confidence as before. No new interpretation or exculpatory discovery has appeared.

The board has nevertheless changed the arrangement. The indefinite charge remains, while a significant part of its corrigibility disappears. Its previous reasons supported Continuing Review. They do not, merely by surviving in a file, support Unreviewable Continuation. Nor does the danger that required deletion establish that Lea should bear an indefinite charge under the new conditions. The cooperative must assess that package or modify it.

This controlled comparison holds present evidential support constant. It does not hold future opportunities for correction constant: those are precisely what deletion changes. The claim is consequently neither that deletion proves innocence nor that procedural interests float free of all possible effects on accuracy. A loss of future protection can matter without changing what anyone should presently believe.

## 2. Why the conversion needs justification

An institution exercising continuing power cannot rely indefinitely on a justification whose materially relevant conditions have ceased to obtain. This requirement allows justification to persist through time. It does not require a fresh hearing every morning. But it rules out treating the continuing use of a decision as justified simply because making the decision once was justified.

The argument has four steps. First, some continuing impositions are adequately justified only in conjunction with effective opportunities to contest their basis. Second, an opportunity to request review is not equivalent to an opportunity to obtain review that could detect the relevant errors. Third, evidential closure can remove the latter while leaving the former's language intact. Therefore, closure can remove part of the justification for the continuing arrangement even where the historical finding retains its evidential force. Where that happens, the institution must supply adequate other grounds or change the arrangement.

The first step is substantive. Why should correction sometimes matter after a competent initial hearing? Reliability tells us how often a procedure errs. It does not, by itself, settle which consequences may follow for those against whom it errs. Two institutions with equally reliable initial investigations can expose people to very different prospects: a bounded loss, an indefinite loss with meaningful opportunities for correction, or an indefinite loss insulated from those opportunities. Justifying one of these prospects involves the seriousness, duration and distribution of possible wrongful treatment as well as its initial probability.

Consider the viewpoint of a member who accepts that investigations must sometimes end with uncertainty. She can accept a substantial recurring charge for damage while resisting a system that provides neither an endpoint nor a feasible response to certain later demonstrations of error. Her complaint need not be that the investigators are unreliable. It can be that the institution takes its interest in avoiding repeated investigations as decisive while leaving her interest in escaping an indefinitely persistent mistake without comparable protection. A corrective route can answer that complaint. Removing it reopens the question of whether the remaining arrangement answers it.

This argument establishes no universal right to indefinite factual review. Some decisions can reasonably be final, and some procedures can be justified by the benefits they produce at acceptable cost. Dworkin's response to Bone distinguishes civil disputes involving independent moral rights from cases for which an instrumental assessment of procedure may be appropriate (2010, pp. 1084–1085). The present principle does not resolve that disagreement. Its conditional force is common to different accounts: where correction is part of the adequate justification, its removal must figure in the assessment of continuation. On an instrumental account, lost prospects of correction can alter expected costs. On a rights-based account, their removal may undermine protection against unacceptable treatment. Neither account is entitled to count an unavailable safeguard as available.

The principle is not about preserving an institution's preferred rhetoric. An official may invoke openness to review when adequate, unmentioned reasons actually justify a final arrangement. In that case the discovery of those reasons can vindicate continuation. Conversely, merely announcing that a procedure is fair cannot make it so. The dependence must be normative: would the available grounds adequately support the imposition under conditions of effective finality? Public explanations and design choices are evidence about that dependence, not its sole determinants.

Pettit's account of contestatory democracy treats the availability of challenge, and arrangements that anticipate it, as sources of control over institutions (2005, section 2, pp. 9–12). The present claim specifies a temporal problem within that broad concern. A procedure can retain its formal channels while changing the conditions that made those channels morally significant.

## 3. Which burden changes?

There are three different burdens in the workshop case. Lea bears the practical charge. A rule may require her to produce sufficient evidence to obtain reconsideration of the historical finding. The cooperative bears responsibility for adequately justifying its continuing imposition. These should not be collapsed into a single burden of proof.

The principle against automatic finality concerns the relationship between the last two. The cooperative cannot use the entry requirement for a *factual* challenge to block a distinct *procedural* challenge: that the existing terms of continuation no longer have the protection on which their justification depended. Lea need not prove that she did not damage the equipment to raise this issue. She needs to identify the continuing imposition, the lost corrective route, and why its loss matters to the arrangement's justification.

The relevant loss is practical and selective. A claimant should identify a recognizable class of errors that the missing resource could have helped to assess and explain why adequate alternatives are absent. She need not identify an actual error in her case. The question concerns whether a protection against errors still exists, not whether she can already establish that she needs its successful operation. Nor is every imaginable possibility sufficient. Losing duplicated footage is different from losing the only usable record of an event; a speculative hope for unlimited future technology is different from an established practice of independent re-examination.

This entry condition is not a novel solution to the general problem of proving the significance of destroyed evidence. Blackmun's dissent in *Arizona v. Youngblood* considers evidence type, exculpatory potential and available substitutes instead of requiring knowledge of destroyed contents (1988, pp. 69–71). Stevens's concurrence also allows that lack of proven bad faith need not exclude fundamental unfairness (pp. 60–61). These are separate opinions, not the Court's holding. Their close antecedence should be acknowledged. My narrower extension concerns a completed, initially fair adjudication and a morally permissible later closure that changes the terms of a continuing imposition.

What must the institution do in response? It must provide an assessment of whether the continuing arrangement remains adequately justified, through a procedure capable of considering that issue. Depending on the stakes, an accessible written explanation of an existing policy may suffice. The principle does not always require oral testimony, a new panel, or a second investigation. It does require that the assessment address effective finality, rather than answer only that the finding remains unrebutted.

This obligation can be discharged prospectively. Suppose the board selected a five-year charge after fairly considering reliability, error costs, privacy, planned deletion and legitimate reliance. Its justification explicitly covers the period after the record expires. Following deletion, it can point to that assessment, provided it remains applicable. No new hearing or changed result follows merely from the scheduled loss. The institution is already operating a justified form of Limited Finality; it has not silently converted Continuing Review into something else.

Nor does the principle transfer the ultimate burden of persuasion on every factual issue. Elfarissi's proposal for reforming the *Youngblood* framework shifts production concerning official good or bad faith while retaining the framework's fault-based orientation (2024, section III). The obligation here concerns access to an assessment of practical continuation. A person can fail to disprove a historical finding and nonetheless establish that an indefinitely continuing restriction is no longer justified on its former terms.

## 4. A change the principle actually requires

Return to the original cooperative. The indefinitely recurring charge was defensible only because meaningful correction remained available. Deletion is now required; adequate substitute review is impossible. Suppose further that the retained report and other relevant interests justify a three-year final charge but do not justify an indefinitely continuing one. This is a stipulated judgement about the case, but it is not the principle itself. It states what the cooperative's reasons support, given the different burdens and safeguards.

The principle then has a determinate consequence. The cooperative must give the charge an endpoint no later than the justified final term. If Lea has already paid for three years, the additional charge must cease. Her historical finding need not change. The cooperative may continue reasonably to believe that she damaged the equipment. What it lacks is adequate justification for using that belief to sustain this particular indefinite imposition after correction has been closed.

Why is this more than a restatement that unjustified burdens are unjustified? It identifies which question the ordinary evidential gate would suppress. “No new evidence of innocence” may correctly describe Lea's position. It does not answer “What now justifies the indefinite duration?” The procedure needs a route for the second question that is not conditional on winning the first. Without that distinction, officials can correctly evaluate every permitted factual submission while never addressing the change their retention policy produced.

The example also shows the limit of a duty merely to explain. Repeating the original considerations cannot make the indefinite term adequate when its necessary safeguard is gone. An explanation suffices only where there are sufficient grounds to explain. Otherwise the institution must restore a suitable protection, alter the continuing imposition, or end it. Transparency about an unsupported arrangement does not justify the arrangement.

The broader application concerns classifications that outlive their evidential records. A system may delete detailed incident files while retaining a durable label such as “misuse established.” The label can be reliable testimony about a past investigation. Its survival therefore does not necessarily create a defect in belief. But if other decision-makers automatically attach an indefinitely recurring disadvantage to it, they inherit a practical problem that the label alone cannot answer.

An abstract finding can preserve the grounds for trusting the original decision more successfully than it preserves the grounds for correcting it. It may record that competent investigators examined the evidence while omitting the details that would permit a competing explanation to be assessed. Deletion can thus be asymmetrical without being deceptive: support for accepting the institutional conclusion survives, while a significant route for dislodging it does not. Landström's analysis of archival selection and redress cautions against treating surviving records as neutral representations of everything that mattered (2021, “Archives” and “Archives and epistemic injustice”).

The practical question is consequently not only when information should expire, but what uses of its surviving conclusions remain justified after that expiry. Records need not have identical lifetimes to every effect they support. A fair settlement may legitimately outlive its underlying documents. The objection is to letting a conclusion's continued storage automatically transmit a form of authority whose justification depended on more than the conclusion's truth.

## 5. Belief, authority and the value of forgetting

The argument deliberately separates an epistemic question from a practical one. A report can remain evidence after its underlying event is no longer inspectable. At the same time, the absence of successful correction cannot be given the evidential significance of a passed test if the test could no longer be performed. Harris makes a nearby point about how control over available contributions can affect what apparent consensus supports (2024, section 5). That caution blocks an illicit increase in confidence. It does not itself establish the procedural duty defended here.

Likewise, the present argument neither establishes nor needs the claim that all delegated epistemic authority dissolves when its evidential basis becomes unauditable. A recent preprint advances that stronger constitutive thesis while distinguishing it from subsequent normative remedies (Sophia 2026, sections 2–3). A retained report and destroyed recording do not straightforwardly refute the thesis: some relevant evidential base survives. My claim addresses a different issue, namely which practical uses remain justified after a particular corrective resource has disappeared. We should avoid answering that issue by treating either complete epistemic collapse or unchanged authority as automatic.

Preservation remains one possible response, but maximizing it is not the aim. A protected copy accessible to a competent reviewer may preserve an effective challenge without preserving general public access. A carefully selected record may do what an indiscriminate archive cannot. Conversely, a summary useful only to confirm the original conclusion may not substitute for material needed to test relevant alternatives.

Competence matters as much as availability. Nguyen argues that public demands for intelligibility can distort expert reasoning and the understandings of communities; expert oversight reduces but does not remove these costs (2022, section 6). A person's claim to a meaningful review need not be a claim to understand every technical reason independently. Institutions can arrange suitable representation and assessment while acknowledging that no review procedure eliminates dependence on expertise.

There are also positive reasons for forgetting. Pressly connects limits on searchable personal history to the possibility of relationships not preconditioned by that history and to confidence in one's agency (2024, sections I and III). His argument concerns internet availability, not a general entitlement to destroy protected review records. Still, it demonstrates why preserving information is not an unqualified benefit. My principle leaves room for stronger, independently defended reasons to delete as well.

Indeed, the principle can favour deletion accompanied by an end to certain restrictions. Keeping a permanent adverse label while removing its contestable basis can combine the social persistence of remembrance with the inability to correct official interpretation. One alternative is to preserve more. Another is to cease automatically treating the label as a source of continuing disadvantage. The latter response protects people from being governed indefinitely by an evidentially insulated past without requiring a fictional statement that the past was different.

## 6. Responsibility without blame

The authority imposing the continuing restriction owes the present assessment, whatever caused the loss. A fire can remove a safeguard just as deletion can. If two boards have identical surviving evidence and identical review-dependent justifications, they face the same question after their records disappear. Their obligations to justify continuation do not differ merely because one operated the delete command.

Choice matters primarily to planning. An institution that can foresee closure must assess the conjunction of its retention policy and the continuing uses of the resulting classifications. It should not delegate retention to one office and sanctions to another, allowing each to justify its own action while nobody assesses the combined arrangement. Where several morally permissible options exist, the institution is responsible for selecting a defensible package. Where deletion is required to protect a witness, there may be no morally available preservation choice. The institution then need not defend failing to preserve, but must still decide what it may continue imposing with the evidence that remains.

This allocation also clarifies a difficult case: Lea herself requests deletion because the recordings expose her private life. Her informed participation can matter to the fairness of subsequent procedures. If an adequate explanation and fair options were available, an agreed finite settlement may appropriately remain binding after she chooses deletion. But operating the switch does not by itself assign every later disadvantage to the cooperative, and requesting privacy does not automatically waive all objections to indefinite treatment. What matters is whether the resulting arrangement was adequately justified, including what Lea could reasonably understand and what alternatives she genuinely had.

A similar answer applies to strategic challengers. The principle does not reward waiting for evidence to expire with an automatic reversal. A planned, justified finality policy may defeat the procedural claim. Deliberately frustrating an adequate earlier opportunity may also affect what is owed. Repeated assertions of abstract uncertainty need not secure repeated assessments once the relevant policy question has been answered. The institution must distinguish these cases from an unanswered challenge to a lost safeguard; it need not abandon all thresholds for review.

Finality protects other people too. Members may rely on a fixed allocation of costs, and endless disputes can be harmful. These considerations can justify a decision's surviving its evidence. But the settlement of who pays a finite sum and an indefinite classification producing new restrictions are different exercises of power. Reasons protecting the former cannot be transferred to the latter merely by calling both consequences of a settled finding.

The principle against automatic finality is thus limited but actionable. It protects a procedural question from being excluded by a demand for unavailable factual evidence. It permits an advance policy to settle that question, and it allows the answer to favour continued enforcement. Where the answer does not, something about the continuing arrangement must change. The end of a record need not end rational belief in a verdict. It can end the justification for treating the verdict as indefinitely operative until the person burdened by it proves it wrong.
