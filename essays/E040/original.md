# Institutional Error and Changes of Authority

An association elects its chair by secret ballot. A copying error reverses the candidates’ totals. Everyone, including the candidates and election committee, sincerely believes that Mara won. They let her chair meetings. The original ballots, which nobody has inspected since the mistake, show that Leon received the majority. Who is the chair?

There is no answer until we know more about the association. Perhaps receiving the majority makes someone chair, and the announcement merely reports that result. Perhaps certification makes someone chair even when it misreports the majority. Perhaps the committee can choose a chair at its discretion. These arrangements can produce the same unanimous belief, announcement, and pattern of obedience while making different institutional facts true.

The familiar lesson is that social dependence does not entail dependence on present opinion. My concern is how we distinguish an institution surviving an error from a change in its rules. I argue that a useful diagnosis must separate the governing criterion, the effect of an official decision, the validity of a remedy, and the remedy’s practical availability. Changes in these can leave different claims intact. Treating every unsuccessful challenge as evidence that authority has moved to the official erases those differences; treating every surviving standard as evidence that nothing important has changed erases them too.

This is a diagnostic argument about organized statuses such as officeholding and membership. It is not a criterion for the numerical identity of institutions, a definition of all social reality, or a derivation of moral authority from institutional validity.

## 1. Recognition needs an object

Searle distinguishes the dependence of institutional reality on human attitudes from the epistemic objectivity of particular institutional facts. He also describes interlocking constitutive rules and the powers attached to statuses. Thus it would be a mistake to attribute to him the simple thesis that whatever everyone presently believes about a particular officeholder must be true. The question is which attitudes, toward which objects, do the constituting. [Searle 2006, pp. 13–18](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/SPIG/SearleSO2006.pdf).

Epstein provides a useful distinction. Facts that establish the conditions for occupying an office need not be the facts that satisfy those conditions. His distinction between anchoring and grounding separates these inquiries; his discussion of historical grounds also makes room for present statuses partly constituted by past events. [Epstein 2016, pp. 153–158](https://epstein.org/wp-content/uploads/Epstein-A-Framework-for-Social-Ontology-in-Phil-of-Social-Sciences.pdf).

Neither distinction is my proposed contribution. Indeed, Dörge and Holweger explicitly describe a case in which an accepted succession rule makes someone heir despite concealment of the relevant family relationship. Their category of indirect recognition accommodates purported facts that participants do not directly recognize, although their own conclusion rejects institutional-fact realism. Universal ignorance of a particular status is therefore an old and available possibility, not an objection that recognition theorists have simply overlooked. [Dörge and Holweger 2021, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-03010-6).

The further problem arises when recognition conflicts with recognition. The association accepts majority election. It also accepts the committee’s announcement. These are not two independent observations that happen to disagree. Each might plausibly be assigned a constitutive role. Saying that the accepted rules determine the answer moves the inquiry to a more informative level, but it does not yet tell us which accepted practice is a rule of appointment and which is a fallible way of applying one.

We cannot settle this by giving every accepted representation equal constitutive weight. If accepting majority election and accepting Mara’s victory each sufficed, without qualification, to establish the corresponding institutional result, the mistaken association would have incompatible unique chairs. Nor should we infer that the association must therefore have ceased to exist. Fallible administration is compatible with an institution’s continuing operation. What is missing from the flat inventory of accepted representations is their relative authority.

## 2. Three roles for the same announcement

Consider three versions of the association. In each, Leon received more votes, the committee announced Mara, and everyone currently accepts the announcement. Set aside ties, fraud, and interpretive uncertainty.

In the first association, its established arrangements make majority receipt sufficient for office. The committee is charged with discovering the result. Authenticated ballots override the announcement, and a later recount recognizes the winner’s title from the original election date. Mara is treated as chair but is not the elected chair. The institution can protect third parties who relied on her apparent authority; that would be a further rule, not evidence that the election rule has vanished.

In the second, certification appoints the chair. The committee must certify the majority winner, but a properly issued certificate is effective even when mistaken. No timely challenge was filed. Mara is chair, although her appointment resulted from the committee’s failure to satisfy its duty. A recount could establish that failure without removing her or making Leon retrospectively chair.

In the third, the committee’s designation is the only standard of selection. Members call the occasion an election, and the committee normally consults ballots, but the committee may disregard them without violating any rule of appointment. Mara is chair, and selecting her is not an incorrect application of the appointment standard.

The differences are not merely disagreements about words. In the first association, recognizing Leon requires no exercise of a power to replace the chair. In the second it would require a new institutional act, assuming such an act is available. In the third even condemning the committee for applying its appointment standard incorrectly misses the nature of its power. Moral condemnation and criticism of its wisdom remain possible in all three.

The second case is crucial. Hart’s distinction between a scorer’s final authority and the correctness of the scorer’s application prevents us from identifying institutional objectivity with the possibility of successful appeal. An adjudicator may have the last word while remaining bound by a standard the adjudicator can misapply. [Hart, *The Concept of Law*, VII.3](https://deepwrite.github.io/HartConcept/book-original/chapter_07).

Hart supplies more than this warning. His introduction of a final scorer while retaining the scoring rule, followed by his contrast with discretionary scoring, already supplies the structure behind the second and third associations. Nor is the possibility of a rule change leaving current outcomes unaffected a new discovery. The contribution pursued here is narrower: using these inherited distinctions to diagnose the different losses concealed by the phrase “correction is no longer possible.”

In the second association, three propositions must be kept separate. “Mara is chair” is true because certification confers title. “Mara received the majority” is false. “The committee complied with its selection duty” is also false. There is no single proposition that becomes both true and false by an unexplained shift of standards.

An account based simply on available correction would wrongly merge the second and third associations. An account based simply on the rule that the majority ought to win would risk merging the first and second. We need to ask both what the official was required to determine and what the official’s act actually settles.

For these purposes, three roles should be kept separate. Their “priority” means which evidence or act has which institutional effect, not one universal ranking of authorities. **Application** assesses a case against an existing standard. **Settlement** fixes an institutional consequence, sometimes despite defective application. **Amendment** changes the standard that future applications are to use. An act can perform more than one role. The distinctions concern its effects and constraints, not three necessarily separate offices.

This separation enables a precise description of institutional error. A status attribution is erroneous relative to a specified standard when the conditions that standard establishes are not satisfied. That error may or may not defeat the attribution’s practical consequences. It may also influence later standards, but it does not become an amendment merely by being accepted as correct on this occasion.

## 3. Priority before agreement

How do we establish these roles without assuming the answer? In an actual association, we would investigate how announcements, ballots, challenges, and amendments have been treated. An explicit charter would be evidence, sometimes very strong evidence, but not a metaphysical device that makes every printed provision effective forever.

Suppose officials preserve ballots specifically for recounts, accept the correction of transcription errors in analogous elections, distinguish correcting a name from appointing a successor, and reject purported rule changes made by an election committee. These features jointly support the first arrangement. They connect the election rule to a practiced distinction between finding an answer and making a new one. Present unanimity about Mara is compatible with this structure because everyone is mistaken about an input the structure makes decisive.

By contrast, suppose the association routinely treats certificates as sufficient for office, regards electoral irregularity as a ground for disciplining the committee but not for invalidating a completed appointment, and requires a removal vote to replace a certified chair. These features support the second arrangement. They show that two consequences have been separated: correction of the assessment and reversal of the appointment.

These investigations are fallible. People can be dishonest about the significance of their practices. A charter can conflict with entrenched administration. Some institutions may have no determinate answer. But the possibility of uncertainty is not an objection to the distinctions. An ontology that automatically derives a determinate outcome from unanimous current attribution would hide the uncertainty by selecting one source of authority in advance.

Nor do the distinctions require unanimous acceptance of the whole structure. Many members may not understand appointment rules; dissidents may reject them. What matters here is whether the relevant structure is maintained in the operation of the institution, not whether every affected person endorses its legitimacy. This restriction is especially important because contested institutional constraints need not disappear when their subjects reject them, a concern motivating Brännmark’s approach to institutional facts. [Brännmark 2019, introduction](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-018-9994-7).

A limited sufficient condition for error surviving agreement can now be stated. Suppose a framework establishes who acquires a status, separately identifies how its rules may be changed, and continues to reserve changes to that procedure. Suppose further that the framework makes an ordinary announcement report the result rather than appoint its subject. Where the accepted announcement conflicts with the status criterion and no authorized transition has occurred, acceptance of that announcement alone does not amend the framework. Its reserved amendment procedure blocks that inference.

This condition is sufficient only given the persistence of the specified framework. Evidence for that persistence might include continued use of the amendment procedure in other matters and settled rejection of attempts by the election committee to amend appointment rules. The condition is not a complete theory of what ultimately sustains any framework. Its point is that collective mistake at one level can coexist with a separately maintained way of determining what that level is authorized to do.

“Framework” matters here. A rule might stipulate that certification becomes conclusive once membership reaches one thousand, or that an appointment criterion expires on a fixed date. The operative criterion then changes without anyone amending anything. Such a transition implements the existing framework. It is neither an error nor necessarily a change in the framework itself. A diagnosis must therefore allow three possibilities: mistaken application, a transition already provided for, and change of the governing framework. It cannot infer the third merely from observing a changed operative criterion.

Nor does the argument deny that repeated deviations can eventually change a framework without a formally valid amendment. The practices supporting a rule can themselves change. Frequency and unanimity do not by themselves settle when this happens. Habitual failure to recount can coexist with a valid entitlement to a recount; habitual disregard can also erode that entitlement. The difficult boundary requires evidence beyond the unsuccessful challenges themselves.

## 4. One failed challenge, four diagnoses

Consider a single hypothetical association whose charter says that the majority winner becomes chair immediately. A panel must hear timely election challenges, and authenticated ballots are decisive. The general assembly alone may amend these provisions. Mara genuinely wins the present election and receives the certificate. Leon challenges, reasonably but mistakenly suspecting a miscount. The challenge fails, and Mara continues in office.

Now vary what lies behind that failure. In the first version, the administrator refuses to release the ballots despite the panel’s authority to inspect them. The refusal is effective: no hearing occurs. The panel and assembly continue to recognize the hearing requirement, and the ordinary amendment procedure remains operative. Leon’s procedural entitlement has been violated. It does not follow that Mara’s title is defective; she won. Nor does it follow that the administrator has acquired a power to make certificates conclusive. The obstacle is misconduct within a maintained arrangement.

In the second version, the assembly validly abolishes election hearings, leaving the rule of immediate title by majority unchanged. Assume it also excludes any other procedure for reversing an erroneous announcement during the term. Leon has no remaining institutional entitlement to a hearing. Nonetheless, the announcement still reports rather than confers title. If the next certificate names a minority candidate, that person will lack the elected title even though no authorized body can practically vindicate the winner during the term. This is an uncomfortable arrangement, but nothing in the stipulated rules makes it contradictory. A framework can leave a status without an effective or even legally available means of enforcement.

In the third version, the assembly instead makes a valid certificate sufficient for title once issued, while retaining the committee’s duty to certify the majority winner. Hearings remain, but their powers are limited to identifying mistakes and disciplining officials. Here a later mistaken certification would appoint the minority candidate. Ballot evidence could establish misconduct but could not undo the appointment. Unlike the second version, the framework now gives the official act a constitutive effect that can survive defective performance of the committee’s duty.

In the fourth, the assembly removes that duty too. Designation is discretionary; ballots are advice. There is still an institution of officeholding. But disagreement between designation and vote totals no longer establishes a violation of its selection criterion.

Mara remains chair in every version. Votes, certificate, incumbent, and the failed attempt to unseat her can all match. This is not an assertion that every present institutional classification matches. In the third version, for example, the committee has a power it lacked before. The claim is specifically that identical assignments of people to offices, even combined with identical failed challenges, can conceal different allocations of authority.

The comparison exposes two tempting but unsound inferences. From “nobody can reverse the certificate,” one cannot infer “the certificate now confers the title.” Version two makes reversal unavailable while leaving title independently determined. From “the majority rule and a review panel survive,” one cannot infer “the old constraints on appointment survive.” Version three preserves both a selection duty and review while changing what a certificate makes effective. Counting formal procedures or observing successful reversals will not discriminate these arrangements.

These mistakes matter because they misidentify the appropriate institutional demand. In the first version, demanding the promised hearing invokes an existing entitlement. In the second, securing such an entitlement requires restoration of a remedy, while recognizing the actual majority winner still applies the appointment rule. In the third, establishing that the minority candidate received a certificate through misconduct does not by itself establish that the candidate lacks title. Replacing that person requires a distinct power, if any exists. In the fourth, applying the old majority requirement as though it still bound the committee mistakes a demand for reform for a claim under the current selection rule.

Those are claims about what follows institutionally, not judgments that subjects ought morally to accept the arrangement. The distinctions can make criticism more accurate. A reformer opposing the third version can identify the transfer of title-conferring power even if the committee has so far selected every majority winner correctly. A dissident in the first version can resist an administrator’s assertion that successful obstruction has already changed the rules. Neither must wait for a changed list of officeholders to articulate the dispute.

## 5. How far can collective error extend?

The hard persistence problem should not be concealed by easy evidence. We must distinguish noncompliance, sincere misinterpretation, and repudiation.

Universal noncompliance with a particular remedy can coexist with its continued recognition: everyone might be frightened to invoke a right they still acknowledge. But this does not establish that a rule survives universal repudiation. Still less does it provide a complete account of rule persistence under conflicting practice.

Sincere misinterpretation creates a different case. A newly appointed committee unanimously thinks its certificates are conclusive. Nobody currently challenges that interpretation because nobody suspects a miscount. Yet the association continues to use a separately established amendment process, denies the committee authority to alter appointment provisions, and treats those provisions as continuing unless that process changes them. Assuming their content is determinate, these practices can sustain the distinction the committee misunderstands. A later finding that Leon won applies the existing arrangement; it need not create a new one merely because nobody previously stated the correct conclusion.

This case does not stipulate that everybody is mistaken about every relevant rule. It shows how agreement at the level of status attribution or committee interpretation can be defeated by a separately maintained limit on that committee’s authority. The hierarchy is sustained elsewhere in the association’s practice. That is the limited sufficient condition, and its limits should be respected.

Suppose instead that all relevant participants knowingly treat the charter as a historical artifact, treat certificates as appointments, and treat replacing a certified incumbent as a new selection. The old text alone does not establish that the original framework still governs. A historian may correctly say that Leon won an earlier election without thereby establishing his present institutional entitlement to exercise the office. That further claim needs an account of the continuing force of the older arrangement.

Between these cases, there may be conflicts with no settled priority: the assembly invokes the charter, administrators persistently ignore it, appeals bodies disagree about their jurisdiction, and no accepted amendment practice resolves the conflict. The present account allows institutional indeterminacy. It does not supply a hidden exact moment at which widespread mistake becomes a new rule. It instead prevents one kind of evidence—failed challenges—from deciding the question prematurely.

A regress of authorized correctors is unnecessary. Final settlement can terminate at an authority whose act remains assessable under a standard it can violate. Where it is the persistence of that standard that is disputed, the inquiry turns to the practices sustaining it, not automatically to a still higher official. This leaves difficult empirical and interpretive questions. It does not collapse the distinction between an official’s power and the correctness of its exercise.

## 6. What the distinctions establish

The argument establishes neither that institutions are independent of social activity nor that institutional facts must be irreducible. An anti-realist may accept the described relations among people, rules, and acts while refusing an additional ontology of offices. Behavioral consequences alone do not refute that view: as Dörge and Holweger emphasize, conduct can follow beliefs whose purported objects do not exist. [Dörge and Holweger 2021, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-03010-6).

An adequate description must nevertheless preserve what follows from the relations in each case. “Everyone obeyed Mara, and nobody overturned the certificate” does not tell us whether a hearing is owed, whether the certificate confers title, or whether a miscount violates a selection duty. These are different features of the organized practice, even if ultimately reducible to other facts. The proposed diagnostic contribution is to keep their persistence and disappearance separate.

Everyone can be wrong about an officeholder when the maintained framework makes that attribution answerable to facts they have misunderstood. Everyone can instead be right about the officeholder while wrong about the vote or the propriety of the appointment. And an institution’s rules can change while the same people remain in office. To assess which has happened, ask separately what determines title, what an official act makes effective, what correction is institutionally available, and what correction is practically possible. Losing one of these is not automatically losing the others.
