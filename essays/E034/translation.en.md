# The Man Who Was Not in the Letter

*English reading translation prepared for this repository. The [Russian original](original.md) is the manuscript evaluated at midterm. This translation is a separate editorial artifact.*

When the letter had been read to the end, an old man appeared outside the embassy door. In place of a rib cage, he had an aquarium. In the aquarium swam the only fish that remembered tomorrow. The old man scratched the door with glass fingers and asked them not to open it: on this side, he had already grown attached to his life.

The letter consisted of five words:

“Something is scratching quietly behind the door.”

There was no old man in it, no aquarium, no request. The sender had not yet decided what exactly was behind the door. She liked that unresolvedness. She had begun a story and wanted to continue it with the recipient.

In the recipient's world, correspondence was a difficult craft. A received fictional description could not simply be imagined. Translation from words into the local language of events immediately created a scene. If there were several ways to embody the letter, the machine chose one. It could choose randomly or tastefully, but it could not leave a physical “something in general” behind the door. In the created scene, some particular object or creature was scratching.

This is a law of an invented world. It does not follow that human imagination works the same way. We need a tighter knot: the recipient is forced to introduce determinacy before having time to decide whether to agree to it.

The embassy could discuss the machine's operation in ordinary administrative speech. Such speech created no new scenes. Correspondence about the story and the story embodied by correspondence remained distinguishable. Let us add one more condition: once created, beings persisted independently of whether the participants regarded the scene that produced them as part of their shared story. Changing the story did not by itself destroy anyone. Otherwise the experiment would quietly destroy its own conditions: every remark about an error would become another world, with nowhere left to check what had actually been corrected.

The embassy's question was simple: how can one deliver someone else's unfinished thought if every delivery completes it?

At first glance, the answer is simpler still. Create something that does not contradict the letter. The old man scratches; he scratches quietly; he is behind the door. Every condition is met. There should be no complaint about the translation.

But a minute later, a second letter arrives:

“Let it be something inanimate. A key, for example, trying all night to open the door from the wrong side.”

The old man hears people in the next room discussing whether his existence is a translation error.

The embassy will have to distinguish three things it had until now conveniently treated as one: what now exists, what was said, and what the participants agreed to add to the shared story. In ordinary correspondence, these distinctions often do not get in each other's way. Here, they stand at a single door.

Imagine two embassies. In both, the first letter creates an exactly identical old man. His fingers, fish, fear, and the sound he makes are identical. The translators also understand the sender's wish in the same way: to begin a story while leaving the nature of the scratching thing open.

In the first embassy, everything the machine adds for the sake of embodiment counts as its own proposal. The recipient may live beside the old man, but cannot yet honestly tell the sender: “We have already established that a person is there.” He replies to her second letter: “Yes. The machine added the person. In the shared story, that place is still open.”

In the second embassy, a rule applies: everything embodied counts as already said by the sender. The recipient replies: “You changed your mind. In your first letter, you had an old man behind the door, and now you are replacing him with a key.”

Both recipients see the same thing. Both can allow the story to proceed along any path afterward. Even the second embassy can graciously agree to the replacement. The difference is not in the number of available endings. It lies in what must happen to reach one of them. In the first case, the translator removes his own addition from what he attributes to the sender. In the second, the sender must retract a claim she never made.

This is already an error of transmission. We can identify it without deciding who should have the final word in collaborative writing. Even a despotic editor is entitled to say, “I need the old man, so I reject your continuation.” But his authority over the manuscript does not entail that she wrote the old man.

I propose checking this kind of transmission in reverse. If the original utterance left a question open and the translation was forced to settle it, an admissible specification of that question should return to the recipient as a correction to the translator's addition. It should not return as an admission by the author: “I previously asserted the opposite.”

This requirement to preserve the kind of correction is a necessary but insufficient condition of faithful transmission. One can carefully mark every addition and still distort what was explicitly said—for example, by replacing quiet scratching with loud scratching. The requirement concerns translation that purports to continue someone else's utterance in joint work. It does not mean every translation must allow its author to change the work endlessly. Nor does it mean only kindly interlocutors can understand another's words. A hostile recipient may understand the letter perfectly and then deliberately attribute more to it. The violation occurs at the next step: when the result is presented as a faithful transmission of what the author contributed to the conversation.

Why is simple agreement with the letter insufficient?

Because compatibility cannot distinguish an implication from a choice. That something scratches behind the door does not entail that a person is there. A person fits. A key fits too. If the means of transmission is forced to choose a person, it acquires its own history of actions. The original utterance does not thereby acquire a new logical consequence.

Suppose the translator objects: “But here we cannot embody a message without additional details. You are demanding the impossible.” We demand nothing of the sort. Let the details arise. The need to choose a detail explains why the translator added it; it does not explain why the sender must answer for it. A forced addition remains an addition.

Here we should object to the sender herself as well. A phrase does not leave open everything it does not explicitly name. Words operate against a background of shared language, genre, and an already accepted story. If it was previously established that the only person is locked behind the door and nothing else can scratch, the new letter may be about that person. An author does not acquire the right to cancel this context by saying “I did not write that.”

Our case therefore needs a more precise stipulation. Before the first letter, neither a person nor a key had been selected. Both possibilities were allowed by the shared story; the participants acknowledged this. The sender was not hiding a settled answer from the reader. No answer yet existed in the joint work. We are protecting this openness known to the participants, not a secret authorial intention against every consequence of language.

The incompleteness of fiction has long been recognized. David Lewis proposes speaking of a plurality of story worlds: some details differ among them and are therefore not settled by the story itself. He also takes account of the background against which a text is read, rather than restricting himself to literally written sentences. This picture already explains why one suitable realization does not exhaust the letter's content. [“Truth in Fiction,” pp. 41–45](https://www.andrewmbailey.com/dkl/Truth_in_Fiction.pdf).

Is the entire embassy, then, merely a noisy illustration of this old distinction?

In part, it is. I do not claim to have discovered incompleteness. But the letter has another dimension. Even if the two embassies preserve the same set of possible continuations, they can describe the transition to a continuation differently. “You specified an open place” and “You retracted what you said” can lead to the same key behind the same door. They do not say the same thing about the conversation.

Possible-worlds semantics can accommodate this. To do so, it must describe not only the world inside the story, but also the participants, their utterances, additions, and rules of revision. My example does not prove such a description impossible. It shows which difference must not be lost in constructing it. A map of all possible scenes is not yet a map of who said what.

The closest predecessor here is another thought of Lewis's. A conversation has a changing state: new utterances can change accepted assumptions. The change is not always mandatory; an objection can prevent it. Discussing a shared plan, Lewis also distinguishes its content from what the interlocutors take to be reality. [“Scorekeeping in a Language Game,” pp. 339–340, 345–347, 357–358](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/s06/readings/lewis79.pdf).

In the embassy, I am interested in the case where creation comes before such an objection. The machine has already done its work. We cannot call the result accepted on the ground that nobody stopped its appearance: stopping it was impossible. The opportunity to object must therefore be preserved after embodiment, and preserved specifically as an objection to having too much attributed to one. Otherwise, the physical structure of the channel silently acquires the right to edit someone else's commitments.

Take a less convenient case. The translator sincerely considers the old man accepted: he sent a description of the result and received no objections. But the reply has not yet reached the sender. Her silence says nothing about the old man so far. Acceptance might be grounded in her explicit agreement after seeing the result, or in a previously adopted rule under which the machine's choice becomes part of the shared story. Even an agreement that “silence means consent” would require its specified conditions to be met: deliver the description and allow time for a reply. The old man's presence substitutes for none of these events. The recipient can therefore correct the mistake without an accusation of lying: “I prematurely treated our addition as agreed.”

If the parties dispute whether the description arrived, whether there was consent, or what the earlier context allowed, our criterion will not resolve that dispute by itself. Grounds for establishing this history of communication are needed first. The criterion tests the treatment of openness and agreement already established; it does not determine the content of an utterance from scratch. Its usefulness is more modest: it shows where the search for a suitable scene needs to be supplemented by an inquiry into how a detail became a shared commitment.

It may seem that the solution lies in reading intentions accurately. Let the machine know what the sender wanted. But our two translators already know this. Such knowledge helps them understand the letter; by itself, it does not make them conduct the shared conversation honestly.

Grice distinguishes a communicative action from simply producing an effect, even a desired one. In his analysis, recognition of the intention must play the proper role, rather than being an extra cause alongside others. In the later pages of “Meaning,” he directly discusses the distinction between a reason for the recipient and a mere cause of the recipient's response. [“Meaning,” pp. 381–386](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/f07/pragmatics/grice57.pdf).

Our criterion of translation does not follow from this as a ready-made conclusion. But it helps us avoid declaring successful materialization a successful conversation. The machine created a suitable being. Perhaps the sender even liked him. For him to become her contribution to the shared story, an intelligible transition is still needed—for example, her saying “Let's keep it that way.” This is acceptance of an addition, not the discovery that it was always contained in the five words.

A defender of the second embassy may offer a stronger argument. The participants knew the local laws. The sender sent the letter herself. Did she not agree to the inevitable result?

Suppose she agreed to the risk that a being would appear. Perhaps she is responsible for that risk. But agreeing to set the machine in motion does not amount to approving every detail it will produce. One can give someone authority to complete a scene. One can accept all their decisions into the shared text in advance. The situation then changes: openness has deliberately been transferred to another. But that is a separate condition of cooperation. It cannot be inferred merely from the machine's design.

If the embassy openly warns, “Every result becomes our shared story without further agreement,” the sender may accept that rule. The second letter then really does propose revising an already accepted scene. Even then, a historical distinction remains: she approved the machine's choice in advance; she did not describe the aquarium herself. The proposed criterion does not prohibit delegating authority. It prohibits deriving that delegation retrospectively from the fact that something has already come into being.

Meanwhile, the old man is still standing outside the door.

Our distinctions do him little good. If authorial freedom requires turning him into a key, he would prefer to be a gross error that must not be corrected.

This is the most serious objection to the word “correction.” It too easily turns a person into a blot to be erased for the sake of fidelity to the original. Suppose the old man feels pain and wants to live. The letter did not authorize his creation, but the absence of authorization does not now authorize his destruction.

That is precisely why semantic correction cannot be equated with physical rollback. We can acknowledge that the old man exists and that he was not in the letter. We can stop presenting his existence as the sender's decision without ending his life. In the shared story, a key can scratch behind the door; meanwhile, the old man can receive a room in the embassy. His biography will begin with a translation error, but need not remain part of the translation.

Here the fantastic arrangement particularly clearly breaks apart a convenient coincidence: removing an addition from the attributed meaning and removing the addition itself turn out to be two different acts. When the consequences of interpretation live lives of their own, fidelity to the source requires correcting attribution, but may prohibit a literal return to a state the source left possible. Sometimes the openness of an utterance can be saved only by refusing to make the entire existing world an illustration of it. A similar distinction arises without fantasy: a mistaken interpretation of an ordinary document can produce consequences that correcting the text cannot undo. The old man makes the separation visible, but does not establish the uniqueness of this fictional case.

Of course, there may not always be a room. Creating translations of this kind may prove unsafe altogether. The moral conclusion would then be to reject the machine, not to recognize every result it produces as something said. The problem of harm can constrain permissible means of transmission, but does not turn a consequence of transmission into the original's content.

There is a less dramatic limit too. Not every addition needs its own signature. Conversation would become unbearable if every filled-in speck required agreement. The distinction can persist for a long time as a general rule: incidental details of embodiment do not by themselves count as established by the shared story. It is tested when a detail begins to justify a next step, or someone disputes its origin. The embassy does not need an endless register of fish scales. It needs to be able to stop invoking the letter where the letter ends.

My conclusion is therefore limited. I am not proposing a new theory of all words, promising a single correct reading of every novel, or giving authors authority over readers' imaginations. I propose one necessary condition of fidelity in transmitting an unfinished utterance in collaborative fiction: forced completion must preserve the distinction between correcting an addition and retracting something actually said. If that distinction disappears, one can preserve every stated fact flawlessly and still make a person say more than they did.

In the morning, they let the old man in. He asked for paper. The fish advised him to write briefly, or another embassy would incur expenses.

He wrote to the sender:

“Thank you for the door. I was invented afterward.”

It was the first faithful message about him.
