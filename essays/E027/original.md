# Being Right About What You Meant

An arts centre announces a competition: “Submit a black-and-white picture of the town.” Some entrants make photographs with shades of grey. Others construct images using only solid black and solid white. After the deadline, the curator explains that only the second kind was intended. A dated planning document confirms this. She has neither changed her mind nor invented an interpretation to favour a friend. She excludes every picture containing grey.

Suppose both readings were reasonable for the audiences the centre addressed, and nothing supplied with the announcement settled the difference. Suppose entrants also reasonably treated the notice as sufficient guidance for beginning their work. These are features of the imagined case, not claims about every use of “black and white.” Does establishing what the curator meant settle what the centre may now do?

It does not. The photographers can accept the curator’s interpretation while retaining a claim about their lost opportunity. But that claim need not give them a right to submit grey photographs. Admitting those photographs could disadvantage entrants who organised their work around a restriction they reasonably expected everyone to face. The correct interpretation can matter to the remedy without discrediting the earlier conduct that makes a remedy necessary.

That is the distinction I will defend. Public clarification has two different connections to time. It can reveal the content of an earlier direction and help determine which standard should govern the activity. It cannot, merely by succeeding, establish that recipients previously had an adequate opportunity to act under that standard. Repair must respect both facts. In a competition, this means protecting an opportunity to work under a sufficiently communicated common condition, rather than either enforcing the clarified condition as though guidance had been adequate or fulfilling each audience’s preferred interpretation separately.

Three questions should be kept apart. What did the announcement communicate? What were entrants justified in making before clarification? What treatment of them is justified now? These concern content, the earlier basis for action, and present institutional authority. They are connected, but an answer to one is not a substitute for answers to the others.

Give the curator more than evidence about her intention. Suppose a defensible theory establishes that the announcement actually communicated a requirement to use only solid black and white. The photographs failed to conform. This does not establish that their makers acted unreasonably. Nor does their reasonable conduct establish that the photographs conformed. Both claims can be true together. If, instead, the announcement lacked uniquely settled content, the centre’s preferred interpretation has less authority as a description of the original requirement. The practical problem remains, but it becomes harder to identify which requirement should supply the basis for repair. My central case grants determinate narrow content to avoid relying on that additional difficulty.

Grice distinguishes producing an effect from communicating through the intended recognition of intention, and extends his discussion beyond assertions to imperative cases. A response can thus differ from the response a speaker meant to induce. An account of what was meant does not, without additional premises, allocate responsibility for that difference. The argument here does not refute Grice. It resists treating an answer about meaning as an answer to every question about the encounter. See [“Meaning,” pp. 381–384](https://www.uh.edu/~garson/Grice_Meaning.pdf).

The same caution applies to common ground. Stalnaker distinguishes what participants share from what each takes them to share, and common ground from straightforward shared belief. His framework already permits failures of alignment. After clarification, everyone might accept that the curator meant to exclude grey. This agreement need not include the claim that her intended restriction was previously accessible to everyone. Present agreement about a past intention is not past agreement about that intention. See [“Common Ground,” pp. 702–707](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/f07/pragmatics/stalnaker02.pdf).

There is a useful controlled comparison. Imagine two centres whose audiences received the same evidence and acted in the same ways. Stipulate that, in one case, the actual communicated rule excluded grey, whereas in the other it permitted grey. Any features that fix this difference were unavailable to the audiences at the relevant time. Their earlier reasonableness must remain the same: the later discovery supplies no difference in their earlier grounds for action. The appropriate remedy need not remain the same. If other conditions are met, the first centre can preserve a narrow competition while giving affected entrants a fresh opportunity to meet its restriction; the second can preserve a broad competition while giving unnecessarily restricted entrants a chance to revise. Meaning is relevant to what was offered without being an instrument for rewriting what recipients could know.

Now consider the strongest objection to the photographers. The centre says: “We accept that you acted reasonably. Our notice was inadequate, and we owe you a response. But this prize is for a work using only two solid colours. Eligibility concerns the work, not your blameworthiness. Paying for our communicative failure does not require abandoning our artistic project.” This is a sound distinction. Exclusion does not always accuse someone of being inattentive or undeserving. A restriction can help constitute an activity independently of whether following instructions is itself what the activity assesses.

The photographers’ complaint should therefore not be that their reasonable interpretation automatically suspends the requirement. It should be that the centre solicited their efforts through inadequate guidance, thereby exposing an opportunity it invited them to pursue to an avoidable failure. Authority to retain a legitimate requirement does not by itself establish authority to disregard that failure when arranging assessment.

The normative premise is a familiar one, stated here for this setting. When an issuer invites people to pursue a specified opportunity and foreseeably supplies inadequate guidance for doing so, it owes reasonable measures to prevent or repair the resulting loss. If a measure can preserve the invited opportunity at modest cost, without defeating the activity’s legitimate purpose or comparably important claims, the issuer needs a reason to reject that measure. Reimbursing expenditure is insufficient where it leaves an avoidable loss of the invited opportunity untouched.

Why protect the opportunity, rather than merely the resources spent? Because that is what the centre asked people to spend their resources on. Someone who would never have purchased a week’s artistic labour simply to have its expenses refunded is not made whole in the relevant respect by that refund. The centre recruited an effort directed at participation. Where participation under legitimate terms remains readily achievable, substituting reimbursement treats an incidental cost of pursuing the invited end as though it were the end itself. This is not a general right to successful participation: a poor picture may lose, and a participant may decline a suitable chance to revise. It is a defeasible claim to reasonable protection of the opportunity the invitation induced them to pursue.

This premise does not follow from semantics alone. It is a principle about induced reliance, and the essay does not offer a new foundation for it. Kolodny and Wallace’s discussion of Scanlon distinguishes loss prevention from the stronger obligation of fidelity; it also connects assurance to planning and coordinated activity. Their account is not limited to reimbursing out-of-pocket expenses. A charitable reliance account can recognise opportunity loss and interests whose satisfaction depends on how others are treated. The task here is to specify what such an account must track when clarification intervenes. See [“Promises and Practices Revisited,” pp. 125–130](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~ngkolodny/PandPR.pdf).

The specification has practical consequences. Suppose the centre’s narrow artistic restriction is legitimate, the intended rule has been established, and the assessment date is not integral to the artistic project. The submissions remain sealed. The centre can readily fund another preparation period long enough to produce eligible work, give every entrant the explicit specification, allow all to revise or begin again, and reasonably cover additional expenses caused by its mistake. There are no comparably serious scheduling or third-party obstacles. In this version, the centre may retain the narrow requirement. It should offer the further opportunity before making exclusion final. If a photographer then insists on submitting an unmodified grey picture, exclusion can be justified.

The conclusion distinguishes eligibility from its administration. The original photograph need never become an eligible entry. What the centre cannot justify, under the stipulated conditions, is ending the photographer’s participation when a reasonable measure would preserve the opportunity under the very requirement it wishes to maintain. Calling the original restriction constitutive does not answer this complaint: the proposed remedy preserves it. To insist that only the original deadline counts, after stipulating that timing is not integral to the project, merely introduces another barrier whose significance needs defending.

Contrast an exhibition selecting works for a fixed installation that can display only solid black and white. The opening cannot be postponed without defeating the event, and no feasible adaptation or further preparation opportunity remains. A grey photograph is unsuitable for the project, however reasonable its maker was. The centre may exclude it while acknowledging responsibility for the inadequate guidance and owing reasonable repair of the resulting loss. A refund and apology may be part of that response, though they should not be described as restoring the lost opportunity if they do not. Responsibility for a loss can remain when preventing it is no longer possible.

The difference is not that one centre has a meaning and the other has an excuse. In both cases, content helps specify the governing condition. What differs is whether preserving the opportunity under that condition is reasonably possible. A legitimate purpose can justify retaining a requirement; necessity can justify a particular adverse consequence despite the loss caused by defective guidance. Mere repetition of the requirement supplies neither showing. By contrast, an event whose purpose is to compare creative responses to adequately communicated common constraints has especially weak grounds for treating differences generated by its own inadequate announcement as an acceptable basis for comparison.

Reasonable interpretation is not enough to activate every part of this argument. Acting on it must also have been reasonable. A notice may plainly direct entrants to consult an accessible specification. Someone who ignores that direction cannot rely on the announcement’s incompleteness in the same way. An entrant who notices serious uncertainty and can resolve it cheaply may reasonably be expected to inquire. An explicit, legitimate allocation of uncertainty can also make a risky response the entrant’s responsibility. The relevant circumstances are those before the commitment of effort, not whichever reading is attractive after the outcome.

Yet inquiry cannot be unlimited. Public guidance would lose its function if each reader had to privately reconstruct every intention before acting. A clarification channel may be unavailable; an issuer may expressly present a notice as sufficient; a reading may be so ordinary for an invited audience that its members have no reason to notice a rival. Drożdżowicz treats clarification as a substantive activity involving interpretative responsibility and unevenly distributed resources. Her discussion supports examining these circumstances rather than assuming that everyone who needed clarification could simply have obtained it. See [“Making it precise,” section 4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-022-03544-x).

The second audience complicates repair. A printmaker says: “I abandoned a design that used grey because I understood it to be forbidden. Now you propose to admit photographs that use it.” This is not a claim to win, an equal chance of winning, or access to every technique that would improve the work. It is a claim concerning a supposedly common condition around which the centre invited entrants to plan.

Under the original narrow rule, the printmaker has no claim to recover the chance to submit a grey design: that chance was not part of the legitimate offer. The complaint is instead that waiving the restriction for the photographers changes the field of permitted techniques after the printmaker has prepared work on the reasonable understanding that the restriction applied to all. The invitation, rather than the mere existence of different production histories, gives this difference its significance.

Hold the finished pictures and expenditures fixed, but change the notice to one that clearly permits grey. A printmaker knowingly chooses to use only solid colours for personal artistic reasons. That printmaker cannot complain, on the ground just given, that other entrants use grey. The centre did not induce the belief that the self-imposed restriction governed the field. Competitors routinely face different talents, preferences and private constraints. Repair is owed here for a relevant difference in the guidance-induced basis for participation, not for every difference in the conditions under which pictures were produced.

There is a further contrast. One printmaker discarded a stronger design because of the understood restriction; another happened to make exactly the work they would have made under either reading. The latter lacks the former’s claim about a lost choice of work. But if the actual rule is narrow, both can object to an unannounced waiver that changes the common eligibility condition. A procedural interest in the announced comparison need not depend on showing that one’s picture would otherwise have been better. The first printmaker has an additional production-related complaint; the second does not. Neither complaint licenses a demand that reasonable photographers simply absorb every consequence of the centre’s mistake.

The repaired narrow competition described above respects this distinction. It does not promise to restore the printmaker’s abandoned grey design to eligibility. It preserves the narrow common condition, while giving everyone adequate notice and a practical opportunity to produce or revise eligible work. Keeping submissions sealed avoids giving anyone advance access to rivals’ solutions. Covering reasonable extra expenses places some of the burden where the failure of guidance originated. Allowing everyone to revise also avoids treating extra preparation as a benefit reserved for one audience.

Residual differences will remain. Some entrants already possess useful work; others must begin again. A prior constraint may have improved one picture and impeded another. An adequate period cannot equalise all accumulated practice, insight or motivation. But the protected interest is not equality in every resource or exact restoration of each imagined outcome. It is an effective opportunity to prepare work under a sufficiently communicated common condition. In the stipulated case, the additional period supplies that opportunity while preserving the activity’s legitimate identity. Further harms, such as an independently significant lost engagement, may require additional repair; their possibility does not make the procedure described inadequate merely because the past cannot be erased.

The baseline matters. If the actual rule had been broad, retaining a narrow requirement would itself need justification as a change to the offer. A broad repair could give the entrants who unnecessarily restricted themselves an opportunity to revise with grey, while allowing the photographers to participate under the originally permitted range. The audiences’ earlier reasonableness would be unchanged from the narrow case, but their claims concerning the common condition would differ. Thus the correct content can select a defensible remedial baseline even though it cannot select which past interpretations were reasonable. Where the actual content remains disputed, there may be no uniquely privileged baseline; agreement on a replacement procedure may then be needed.

This answers a tempting but mistaken response to ambiguity: honour all reasonable readings. Such a response can be generous to each reader considered against the issuer alone and still mishandle their relations to one another. A two-colour picture satisfies both readings, so the problem is not an impossible conjunction of contents. It concerns which techniques may be used in a common assessment, and what opportunities entrants received to work within that condition. A reliance account can capture the problem, but only by including the relevant relational interest. Individual claimants need not have isolated claims.

Nor does admitting every reasonable interpretation avoid the need to adjudicate. It selects a broader range for present purposes, thereby making a decision about what participants must now compete against. That decision may sometimes be justified, especially when extending participation imposes no relevant comparative burden. It cannot be justified simply as neutral respect for every understanding. The move from protection of reasonable reliance to fulfilment of the relied-on interpretation requires an argument of its own.

There is an important connection with retraction. Kukla argues that retracting a speech act is not a reset: normative effects can remain, and success differs from ethical permissibility. The present argument shares that resistance to erasing history. Its central case, however, need involve no withdrawal. The curator can retain the original narrow direction and correctly explain it. The problem concerns the application of a continuing requirement after reasonable action under different guidance. A successful clarification can therefore leave remedial obligations even when it retracts nothing. See [“The Ethics of Retraction,” preprint v1, pp. 1–6 and 15–17](https://www.qeios.com/read/PDR3QV/pdf).

Does this inflate an ordinary moral duty into a linguistic commitment? It would, if every direction were alleged to contain an unspoken promise of compensation or a complete procedure for handling mistakes. No such semantic claim is needed. Using language to invite assessed conduct establishes a practical relation; principles concerning that relation help determine its consequences. Similar principles can apply to nonverbal guidance. The contribution is an account of how facts about public interpretation enter this relation, including where their authority ends and where it continues.

Lewis’s conversational score can include permissions and other practical statuses, and he explicitly allows linguistic action to blend into wider social practices. Such a framework can preserve the history and participant-specific claims described here. A register containing only the current agreed interpretation would be insufficient for this practical purpose, even if adequate for a narrower semantic task. The issue is what an account of public clarification needs to retain, not whether semantic theories must become complete moral theories. See [“Scorekeeping in a Language Game,” pp. 345–346 and 356](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/s06/readings/lewis79.pdf).

The account is consequently a specification of opportunity-sensitive reliance in a setting where semantic authority can distort its application. It does not discover that misleading people creates duties, or that comparative opportunities matter. Its result is that the historical assessment of reliance and the present selection of a remedial standard respond differently to clarified content. Confusing them produces errors in opposite directions: dismissing reasonable recipients because their reading lost, or treating their reasonable reading as something the institution must fulfil regardless of the legitimate offer and the other participants.

Many ordinary corrections require little repair. They arrive before action, affect an irrelevant detail, or permit an easy adjustment that burdens nobody. The cases that matter are those in which public guidance has already organised costly conduct and the issuer proposes to make the clarified distinction decisive. There, the proper response is more exact than either “the meaning settles it” or “the audience must be accommodated.” It asks what was offered, what recipients could reasonably do, and which available response now protects their interests consistently with that offer.

The curator may be right about what she meant and entitled to retain the artistic restriction. She may still owe the photographers a further opportunity before ending their participation. Providing it need not turn grey photographs into eligible works or deprive printmakers of the common restriction around which they planned. Those conclusions become visible once we stop making semantic correctness answer a question about earlier opportunity—and stop supposing that its failure to answer that question makes it irrelevant to repair.
