# Can Tomorrow Cancel Yesterday's Survival?

An object can acquire a new description because of something later. A letter becomes the last letter someone ever wrote. But can a later event determine whether an object had already survived an earlier transition? If a person was reconstructed on Monday, could activating a second reconstruction on Tuesday make it false that the first reconstruction was ever that person?

This is not a question about changing a fixed past. A theory can assign an unchanging verdict to each complete history while making Monday's identity depend on Tuesday's events. The issue is whether facts about a completed interval settle persistence through that interval. Call the requirement that they do *completed-persistence stability*.

I defend a conditional result about continuity accounts of identity. In delayed-replication cases, stability is incompatible with jointly accepting that a sole adequate reconstruction preserves identity and that equal continuity from the original demands equal identity verdicts. The last condition is stronger than indifference to activation order. It excludes additional identity-makers, including a rule that gives an established causal continuation priority over a later reconstruction from the original. That restriction matters: causal priority is a serious response, not obviously an arbitrary preference for whoever arrives first.

The result identifies a choice between evaluating every candidate against the original and evaluating later candidates against an already established continuation. Those procedures can agree in ordinary cases yet disagree about whether future events may determine earlier survival. Identity's prohibition on branching does not select between them.

## 1. What must remain stable?

Consider two histories with the same prefix: the same history up to and including Monday. The shared facts include the identity-neutral base accepted by the theory under examination: bodily organization, psychological dispositions, apparent memories, causal processes, and relevant social practices, described without assuming the disputed identity. We must not include “A remembers doing what O did” if remembering already entails being O. Nor may we assume the identity of a legal person when describing institutional facts.

Stability requires corresponding completed-persistence claims to agree across these histories. This does not require an observer to know the answer on Monday. Relevant facts can be inaccessible. Nor does it require identity to depend only on the intrinsic qualities of two endpoints. Causal ancestry, intervening history, and surrounding circumstances before Monday may all matter. Only later differences are excluded.

Subjects must also be specified without selecting them by their future careers. We designate an original occurrence and reconstruction events by their causal origins, then ask whether the individuals at those occurrences are one individual. This formulation is appropriate for theories that recognize a determinate bearer at the original occurrence. A theory of overlapping complete lives may reject that individuation. Such a theory is outside the argument's immediate scope unless it supplies an appropriate interpretation of the claims. Local designation does not settle that dispute by itself.

Why prefer stability? Ordinarily, surviving an interval explains how an object's history reached its endpoint. A tree's producing an independent offspring next spring should not constitute its failure to survive this winter. Later evidence can show that our earlier description was mistaken; that differs from later events making an unchanged earlier transition fail to preserve the object. Stability expresses this difference as a constraint on an account of persistence.

The constraint is defeasible. The longest uninterrupted performance is selected partly by its complete history. But such examples do not establish that every locally designated object's identity is similarly global. A defender of future dependence must justify its application to the relevant objects, rather than invoke the existence of some future-dependent descriptions.

The literature already recognizes the issue. Gustafsson discusses future-dependent identity and asynchronous replication (2019, PDF pp. 8, 17). Neither delayed duplication nor discomfort with future dependence is a new discovery here. The contribution below is a restricted compatibility diagnosis and an explanation of what a causal response must change.

## 2. An explicit incompatibility

Suppose an original person, represented at an occurrence O, is destroyed while two equally complete records are preserved. Neither dormant record is a person. Either can generate a reconstruction through the same kind of procedure, reproducing the psychological organization and causal connection required by the continuity theory being tested. No practical possibility of this technology is asserted; the argument is conditional on the theory admitting such cases.

In history One, the first record produces reconstruction occurrence A on Monday. A is followed by ordinary, unbranched causal continuation, including occurrence A-later on Tuesday. The second record is never activated and is eventually destroyed. In history Two, the entire prefix through Monday is the same, but the second record produces reconstruction occurrence B on Tuesday. B and A-later occupy separate bodies and continue separately. Neither reconstruction process descends from the other.

Candidate continuers are thus specified by reconstruction events and causal paths, not by counting already individuated persons. “Sole reconstruction” does not mean that A has only one later stage. Both histories include the same ordinary A-to-A-later continuation. We assume that continuation preserves identity; it is not itself at issue.

The relation from O to A at activation and the relation from O to B at activation tie in the specified kind and degree of continuity. The later A-later occurrence does not tie with B in every causal respect: A-later descends from A, whereas B descends independently from O's record. Keeping those comparisons separate is essential.

Three commitments are now precise:

**Sufficiency:** In history One, the single adequate reconstruction event, followed by ordinary continuation and never joined by an independently activated rival, preserves O as A.

**Stability:** The identity verdict about O and A agrees across histories with the same identity-neutral prefix through A's activation.

**Equal treatment of original-continuity ties:** In history Two, the matched O-to-A and O-to-B continuity connections require the same identity verdict. Additional selection by activation date, subsequent branch history, record labels, or primitive identity is excluded.

The third premise deliberately does not say merely that switching dates must leave identity unchanged. A non-temporal selection rule could satisfy that weaker condition while privileging one record. The premise instead expresses a substantive account of what can ground a difference: for these candidates, differences in the stipulated original-continuity relation are required. With those differences absent, unequal verdicts are prohibited.

Assume determinate, classical identity verdicts. Sufficiency identifies O with A in history One. Stability identifies O with A in history Two. Equal treatment then identifies O with B there. Since ordinary continuation also identifies A with A-later, transitivity identifies B with A-later. But these are the stipulated distinct simultaneous individuals. Contradiction.

The result is conditional on the premises, not a demonstration that one individual can become two. It also does not prove that primitive identity facts are impossible. An account admitting such facts rejects the restricted explanatory requirement in the third premise. Indeterminacy can provide another departure from the assumed framework. Neither should be disguised as something already ruled out by the logic of identity.

The dormant record is present in both histories. An account that takes its mere existence to defeat the first reconstruction rejects sufficiency in the stipulated case. That is coherent but expands competition from actual reconstruction events to capacities for producing them. It then needs an account of which capacities matter. The incompatibility does not force that position; it makes clear what the position abandons.

## 3. An existing criterion under the test

Gustafsson's proposal (11) defines an identity-unifying relation I using temporally ordered continuity R and a condition detecting branching between endpoints. Its final witness can lie outside their interval (2019, PDF p. 16). Apply it to the following minimal model of our case: O is R-related to A and B, while A and B are not R-related. Ordinary descendants of A remain on its branch.

For the O–A pair, choose the proposal's intermediate witnesses as O and A themselves. They are within the endpoint interval and each is R-related to its corresponding endpoint. B supplies the distinguishing witness: O bears R to B, but A does not. The condition therefore excludes O I A. In the history without B, the unbranched chain supplies no such distinction, and O I A holds. The criterion rejects stability in this model. This is an application of the formula, not an objection Gustafsson overlooked: he explicitly addresses later-dependent verdicts (PDF p. 17).

The model separates two kinds of temporal restriction. Keeping the compared branching locations between O and A does not suffice to make the verdict independent of the future, because their classification as different branches still consults a later witness. To obtain stability one must also control which facts can establish that difference. A criterion may be local in the positions it compares while global in the evidence constituting their relationship.

The argument assumes that stored records transmit the relevant continuity across a gap. Excluding such connections changes the model rather than yielding a different verdict on the same model. Gustafsson mentions a no-gap response without favoring it. More generally, restricting admissible continuity can remove either the tied successors or the original reconstruction's adequacy. What cannot be claimed is that bounding one pair of witnesses automatically bounds every dependence in the criterion.

## 4. The strongest case for priority

The best defense of stability is more substantial than an arbitrary first-arrival rule. It begins with a causal sequence: O leads to A; A leads to A-later. On Tuesday, A-later continues the most recent established bearer, whereas B restarts from a preserved earlier record. The two Tuesday individuals therefore differ in how they relate to the intervening life.

A local account can use that difference. Having recognized the O-to-A transition as identity-preserving, it treats A as the relevant source for the next transition. Ordinary continuation gives A-later its identity. B's connection to the older source cannot independently reopen a settled allocation. Transitivity explains why B cannot also be O once O, A, and A-later have been identified.

This response rejects equal treatment of original-continuity ties. It does so because ties measured only from O cease to capture all the relevant causal structure. It need not deny that the original-to-activation relations tie; it denies their sufficiency to determine equal identity verdicts in the complete case. This is a credible way to preserve stability, and it significantly reduces the intuitive force of the neutrality complaint.

Suppose A lives for a year before B awakens with O's old memories. A's year of experience makes the relevance of the intervening bearer especially clear. If a theory gives the two equal identity verdicts because each matches O at activation, it deliberately excludes that intervening life from the comparison. That is an intelligible global policy, but no longer an obviously mandatory fairness between candidates.

Does a one-second interval differ in principle? A transition account can say no: what matters is whether an adequate continuation has already occurred, not how much life has accumulated. This introduces a boundary at the first successful transition. The boundary follows from the account's commitment to preserving established transitions rather than from a psychological threshold of accumulated memories. If an adequate transition can occur in a second, the corresponding identity is already settled.

This causal response has limits. Transitivity alone does not establish the initial O-to-A identity in history Two. It only propagates an identity verdict once accepted. The priority account needs sufficiency and stability, or an independent local grounding rule, to license that initial step. It also needs further rules for simultaneous first reconstructions, interruptions, and later fusion. The sketch is a response to this delayed case, not a complete theory.

The point of the incompatibility is therefore not that causal priority is metaphysically arbitrary. It is that priority cannot be presented as both preserving completed transitions and treating original-continuity ties as decisive. The account has changed the reference point of later assessments. A later reconstruction must continue the established bearer, not merely reproduce an equally good relation to an earlier origin.

## 5. What the alternatives preserve

A global nonbranching account instead considers the complete distribution of successors. It can deny both reconstructions' identity with O once both are actual. In an eternalist setting, there is no alteration of Monday: different whole histories contain different Monday identity facts. The cost is rejection of stability, and the benefit is retaining equal verdicts for the tied original connections. Calling this “cancellation” must not suggest backward causation.

An account may instead deny that even a unique reconstruction preserves O. Psychological and informational continuity could survive without numerical identity. This rejects sufficiency while leaving both stability and equal treatment available. A separate claim that both successors deserve concern remains possible. Nothing in the incompatibility assumes that numerical identity exhausts what matters in survival.

Sider's stage theory supplies a further alternative analysis of ordinary temporal claims. It uses temporal counterparts to vindicate tensed predication without timeless numerical identity between distinct stages (1996, section 2). It should not simply be accused of changing the subject: it disputes the analysis of the ordinary subject. Our proof concerns the strict identity interpretation, so its contradiction does not transfer automatically to the counterpart relation.

Similarly, theories individuating people as overlapping complete careers dispute the unique-bearer framework used in the setup. The number of complete careers may differ while their shared prefix does not. Whether that captures ordinary talk of an individual already surviving is a further question. The present argument cannot settle it through a stipulation about reference.

Nor is stability a theorem about all physical worlds. Exact matching prefixes with different futures require a suitable space of possible histories. Backward causation that changes the matched prefix would invalidate the comparison. A deterministic theory may reject the stipulated divergence under fixed laws. The result applies where the histories and continuity relations are admitted.

Within that scope, the lesson is definite. A rule that protects completed persistence and accepts a unique adequate reconstruction must cease treating equal original-continuity connections as enough for equal identity verdicts. A causal account can justify this by letting an established continuation become the source against which later candidates are assessed. A global account can refuse that updating procedure, but then later witnesses may determine earlier survival. The disagreement concerns which history grounds a continuation claim, not just whether identity can branch.
