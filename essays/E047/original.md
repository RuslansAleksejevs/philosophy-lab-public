# Who Gets to Declare the Transformation Complete?

Suppose someone changes your life and, in doing so, changes the values with which you assess what they have done. You later approve. Your approval may be sincere, informed, and carefully considered. It may also be exactly what the person who changed you intended to produce. These possibilities are compatible. The question is what your approval entitles its recipient to claim.

One possible claim concerns consent: you have now authorized something that required your permission. Another concerns procedure: the other person respected you by submitting an exercise of discretion to your judgment. These claims can come apart. A valid act of consent does not establish that the procedure through which it arose gave the consenting person the authority that its defender says it gave her.

I defend a constraint on this second claim. An institution cannot establish that it submitted an intervention to its subject's judgment if its procedure counts competent approval as a delivered answer but treats equally competent rejection as unfinished personal development simply because it is rejection. The person's refusal must be able to acquire the operative standing assigned to a negative answer, even while further discussion remains welcome and later ratification remains possible. I call this the *closure condition*. Closure here means the delivery of a judgment with present consequences, not the permanent end of reflection.

The general observation that future satisfaction cannot by itself justify transformative intervention is familiar. Howard argues that predictions of gratitude must take account of the attitudes that alternative interventions would produce (2015, §§4–5). Paul and Sunstein likewise explain why favourable endogenous preferences may fail to discriminate between alternatives, and why the process producing them matters (undated, pp. 5–9). My concern is a particular feature of that process: an institution's power to choose which temporal stage of a changing assessment receives the standing of a verdict.

Consider a residential institute that helps adults explore unfamiliar vocations. Its advisers arrange classes, encounters, and practical work that can change residents' deepest commitments. An adviser redirects Arden's programme toward environmental restoration. Whether this falls within the adviser's discretion is disputed: Arden agreed to explore unfamiliar work but did not expressly authorize this particular redirection. After several months, Arden values the work and reflectively approves the initiative. There has been no deception. Arden can leave without penalty and has access to people who reject the institute's outlook. Stipulate that Arden's resulting judgment is autonomous.

The institute defends its discretion by saying that residents judge its exercise afterwards. To give this claim a concrete meaning, suppose its review distinguishes ratification from an unresolved justification. Ratification accepts the disputed initiative for the purpose of this review. A competent refusal leaves it unratified and triggers a funded, independent assessment of whether the adviser exceeded the permitted discretion and what response is owed. Referral does not guarantee that the resident wins. It requires the institute to answer the challenge on grounds other than the resident's acceptance. Neither enjoyment of the vocation nor permission to continue it automatically constitutes ratification.

Compare two protocols. Both schedule review at the same date, provide the same information, and apply the same independently justified requirements of understanding and competence. Under the first, either ratification or refusal receives the specified standing. A refusal triggers referral within a week, although Arden can continue residing at the institute and considering the issue. The institute may offer further experiences with appropriate permission. It does not suspend referral merely because those experiences might change Arden's mind.

Under the second, approval completes the review. Refusal is classified as an assessment made during an unfinished transition. Further encounters or a different mentor are offered, and the referral remains pending while the resident stays in the programme. Referral follows departure if the resident still refuses to ratify; otherwise the institute waits for approval. It records critical statements accurately and does not compel further participation. Nevertheless, a resident must leave the programme to obtain the review that a refusal was supposed to trigger. Continued engagement is made conditional on postponing the institutional force of the negative answer.

Arden approves at the first review in both protocols. All actual experiences and reasoning are identical. The difference lies in what an equally competent refusal would have done. In the first protocol, it would have changed what the institute must do next. In the second, the institute would have continued treating the verdict as pending until agreement or departure. Although Arden's actual consent may be equally effective in both cases, the second institute cannot infer that its procedure submitted the disputed initiative to Arden's judgment. It offered a way to ratify while reserving a veto over when nonratification became operative.

The relevant authority is limited. Arden does not decide every question about whether the programme was good or the adviser acted rightly. The independent reviewer may reject Arden's complaint. What Arden decides is whether to ratify; under the advertised procedure, a refusal settles that question for the present and transfers the burden to another justificatory route. Thus recognizing refusal need not mean agreeing with the resident. It means acknowledging the answer and discharging the obligations that follow from it.

This is why the difference is more than administrative terminology. If both protocols immediately recognized the same refusal and provided the same review, calling one file “incomplete” would not establish the practical defect described here. The present argument does not rest on that label. It rests on withholding the consequence of a delivered negative judgment in the hope of obtaining a later positive one. An opportunity to leave is not equivalent to an opportunity to have one's judgment recognized while remaining in a relationship one is otherwise entitled to continue.

The argument has three steps. First, a defence that invokes procedural respect through submission to judgment attributes some decision-making role to the subject. It says more than that the subject happened to agree. Second, the attributed role includes a rule for when an answer changes the institution's obligations or permissible claims. A statement that can never acquire that standing against the institution's preferred conclusion does not exercise the role as advertised. Third, if the institution may withhold that standing solely because the answer is unfavourable, the institution retains the relevant discretion. A favourable endpoint cannot establish that the discretion was surrendered.

The first premise applies to the specified procedural defence. It does not characterize every morally significant appeal to consent. A permission can exercise a normative power without conferring general adjudicative control over the recipient's policies. Nor does the second premise give the subject unilateral authority to choose every review date. A fixed deadline, jointly agreed schedule, or independent assessment of readiness can supply legitimate timing rules. The condition excludes a particular ground for withholding a verdict's standing: that it does not yet vindicate the intervention. It does not eliminate other grounds for deciding that a purported assessment is premature.

For example, missing essential information may justify postponement. But the information requirement must be defensible without making agreement its test of fulfilment. If the institute accepts an approving resident's understanding as sufficient, it cannot deem an otherwise comparable resident insufficiently informed merely because she declines to ratify. Differences in reasons, evidence, or comprehension can matter; the direction of the answer cannot by itself supply those differences.

The underlying distinction between agreement and control has substantial predecessors. Enoch distinguishes autonomy as alignment with one's commitments from autonomy as sovereignty; his composer example permits persuasion while denying that overriding a person's decision thereby respects that sovereignty (2017, §VIII). Pettit's account of political legitimacy requires control that does not depend on the governing party's willingness to comply (2012, “Three Conditions on Suitable Control”). Lindblom's discussion of contestability distinguishes the opportunity to voice concerns from a forum in which those concerns can affect the exercise of authority (2019, §4). The closure condition applies this family of distinctions to temporal ratification. It is not a discovery of the general difference between a favourable actual outcome and a distribution of control.

What transformation adds is a distinctive way to conceal the retention of discretion. In an ordinary dispute, rejecting every unfavourable answer can look like simple unwillingness to defer. Where evaluative standpoints change, the same policy can be presented as patience with personal development. The resident has not yet learned how to appreciate the experience; the present refusal belongs to a transitional self. Sometimes that diagnosis has independent support. But when approval is what qualifies a standpoint as sufficiently developed, the institution has selected the judge by selecting the judgment.

This selection need not involve irrational influence. At one time a resident may competently reject the intervention; later she may willingly encounter new reasons and change her assessment. Each conversation may engage her agency. Yet the institution's recognition policy can ensure that only the approving temporal assessment acquires practical force. Examining the rationality or consensual character of each encounter will miss that policy. The additional question is what happened to the authority of the earlier refusal during the interval.

Suppose Arden refuses on Monday and welcomes further discussion. The institute can acknowledge the refusal, initiate the promised review, and arrange that discussion. If Arden ratifies on Friday, the new act may change what is owed thereafter, within its proper scope. What it cannot establish is that Monday contained no delivered answer merely because Friday contained a different one. The point is not that the earlier self has superior values. It is that the institution must respond to the judgments it receives under the authority it claims to recognize, rather than reserving recognition for the temporal self it hopes to produce.

The interval matters even if reconsideration is likely. Between Monday and Friday, the institute has an unratified initiative to answer for under the procedure. It cannot invoke predicted Friday assent as Monday's exercise of Arden's authority. If an independent ground permits delaying the review, it may invoke that ground. Expecting to obtain a different answer is not itself evidence that the answer already received has no standing. Nor does a later revision show that the earlier verdict was defective: competent judgments can be revisable judgments.

Three questions should therefore remain separate. Has the person ratified the past initiative? Has she authorized further contact or experiences that might prompt reconsideration? Has she exercised another normative power, such as waiving a particular claim? “I do not ratify, but please keep helping me think” answers the first two differently. “I have no verdict, but do not contact me again” terminates engagement without delivering a ratification verdict. A later waiver can have an object narrower than approval of the original intervention. Respecting the person requires tracking those objects, rather than treating her general satisfaction or continued participation as an answer to all three questions.

This formulation avoids forbidding permissible persuasion. Consider a fundraiser who hopes Rowan will donate. Rowan initially refuses, then freely accepts an invitation to learn more, acquires a new commitment, and gives an informed donation. The fundraiser stops soliciting once Rowan says yes but would have offered further permissible invitations after a no. That project has an asymmetric success condition. There is nothing in the closure condition that makes the donation unauthorized or requires the fundraiser to abandon all hope after the first refusal.

The difference from the institute concerns what follows from no. The fundraiser recognizes that it presently lacks permission to take Rowan's money. Its hope of later success does not suspend that fact. If further contact is welcome, the solicitation can continue while the refusal retains its present force. The bad institute instead postpones the consequence attached to nonratification until the resident accepts its intervention or leaves. Calling both activities an ongoing search for agreement obscures a difference in their treatment of current authority. Calling the institute's next phase a new project would not fix it: changing a file number does not discharge the postponed obligation.

A stronger objection starts from Arden's actual approval. Pettigrew considers preferences that are actually formed through free agency even though a regime would have imposed them had that been necessary. He treats consent based on the actually free formation as effective (2026, §4). Why should a rule that never interfered with Arden's actual deliberation undermine anything here?

It need not undermine Arden's consent. If Arden now authorizes a further residency, the defective rule alone does not show that the permission is invalid. A counterfactual policy of overriding decisions can coexist with respecting a particular decision on the actual occasion. The objection succeeds against an attempt to infer invalid consent merely from the institution's disposition.

But the procedural defence makes a different claim. Whether a policy assigned Arden a role in controlling the review depends partly on what Arden's contrary judgment would have changed. That is a fact about the role, not a hidden defect in the actual reasoning. The institute may truthfully say that Arden approved autonomously while falsely saying that its protocol made an equally competent refusal operative. The closure condition limits the latter claim. It neither makes alternative possibilities necessary for every valid consent nor licenses suspicion of the transformed person's present agency.

Another objection concerns subsequent consent more directly. Chwang defends its possible moral efficacy, rather than reducing later approval to gratitude or the waiver of compensation. In addressing objections, he distinguishes problematic causal relations from mere temporal lateness (2009, §§3.2, 5.2–6). Suppose Arden expressly ratifies the entire procedure, including its asymmetry. If that act is competent, informed, and otherwise effective, why should it not vindicate what happened?

It may have substantial moral force. Arden may waive an available complaint about the procedure, release an obligation owed to Arden, or authorize a comparable arrangement in the future. A strong theory of subsequent consent may even make the earlier intervention permissible. The closure argument does not refute that theory. A subsequent-consent defence could succeed while the further claim that the earlier procedure distributed adjudicative authority in the advertised way remains false.

The remaining point concerns the basis of the defence. Approval of another's retention of discretion is not evidence that the other had surrendered that discretion. The distinction survives even if later consent changes moral status: the protocol's allocation of authority was what it was. There need be no outstanding personal remedy after a valid waiver, and this argument does not establish an unalterable moral wrong under every theory of subsequent consent. It establishes a limit on what can be inferred from a ratifying act about the procedure that produced it. Respecting Arden's present authority includes giving that act its proper force without treating it as proof of every description the institute attaches to it.

One might now think the result too modest. Once the bad institute withholds a promised review, ordinary principles about keeping commitments and hearing complaints already condemn it. Why add a principle about transformation?

The proposal does not require an irreducibly new kind of wrong. Its use is diagnostic. A promise to submit discretion to a person's later judgment is incomplete until we know when that judgment becomes operative. Transformative settings invite an apparently benevolent answer: once the person has developed enough to appreciate what was done. The closure condition identifies when that answer covertly reserves the relevant authority. It also identifies what an adequate repair must preserve. More informed conversation, more sincere testimonials, and a richer range of voluntary experiences do not repair the missing standing of an already delivered refusal. Recognition of that refusal and appropriately authorized continuation can.

The diagnosis applies even without an explicit promise whose breach is independently wrong. Whenever an institution offers submission to later judgment as a reason its discretion was respectful, that reason has content only insofar as the judgment can play the attributed role. An institution can abandon that defence and invoke benefits, professional authority, or valid prior delegation instead. Those grounds might suffice. The closure argument does not determine their strength or require every educational institution to make its students arbiters of every decision.

In particular, education can have independently justified standards of understanding and achievement. A student's rejection need not decide whether a proposition is true or a skill mastered. Neither, however, does failure to master a skill automatically show that the student has failed to deliver a competent judgment about the manner of instruction. An institution that relies on educational authority should defend that authority. If it additionally claims that the student ratifies its use of discretion, it must distinguish an actual exercise of that power from a judgment it hopes further education will eventually produce.

The same limit protects voluntary projects of self-change. Someone can ask a guide to persist through disappointment while introducing a demanding artistic practice. Permission for persistence supplies a ground for further engagement; it need not imply that every present negative assessment has been withdrawn. How far such permission extends is a separate question. The crucial error would be to infer the authorization of continued transformation solely from the approving outlook that continued transformation is expected to create.

Finally, recognizing this error need not require restoring an earlier self. A resident may rightly retain the attachments acquired through a defective procedure and choose to remain. A critic of that procedure has no automatic authority to reverse those attachments. The present person's permissions govern new interventions within their proper scope. This allows us to acknowledge procedural failures without requiring their subjects to repudiate the lives they now value.

The closure condition is thus a necessary constraint on a specific appeal to respect, not a complete theory of transformation or consent. Its distinctive focus is the institutional selection of the temporal assessment allowed to acquire authority. We need not decide which possible future self would have the best values to ask whether a current refusal has been acknowledged. Nor must we forbid later change to insist on its present consequences. A person can change her answer. An institution that claims to have submitted to her judgment cannot treat the possibility of that change as permission to wait until she becomes someone who agrees.
