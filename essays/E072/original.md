# Recovery Without Vindication: Changing Desires and the Comparison That Matters

A person can cease wanting what she has lost and build a life she sincerely enjoys. That development can be good for her. It does not follow that the loss did her no harm, that she has been fully compensated, or that bringing about the loss together with the recovery was a good way to help her. These are different claims. The tempting inference from successful recovery to the superiority of its route fails even when the recovery is entirely genuine.

The difficulty is especially pressing for desire theories of well-being. If welfare depends on getting what one wants, why not improve a person's life by making her want what remains available? It can seem that resisting deprivation requires distrusting the new desires. I argue that this is sometimes unnecessary. This is a compatibility claim, not an allegation that desire theorists generally overlook better alternatives. We can give new desires their full prudential importance while refusing to let the comparison that establishes the value of recovery settle the value of the package that made recovery necessary.

I defend a constraint on that comparison. When the goods acquired through recovery could also have been provided without the loss, a prospective assessment must consider that alternative. Call this the **portable recovery constraint**. Its force is conditional: it applies only where the relevant enrichment really is transferable. It does not show that all adaptive preferences are defective, or even that adaptation cannot fully compensate a loss. It identifies a welfare reason favoring an alternative to some policies that create losses and then change what people want. It does not establish a requirement to maximize welfare. Throughout, outcomes are deterministic and known; comparison of realized lives alone would not settle a decision under uncertainty.

## Three questions about a changed life

Consider a community choir whose rehearsal venue becomes unavailable for a year. Mira loves singing there. During the first month of closure she misses valued rehearsals and experiences substantial disappointment. She then joins a ceramics group, develops a strong interest in pottery, and eventually stops wanting to sing. Her enjoyment and fulfilment in the ceramics group are entirely genuine. There is no deception, coercion, or external judgment that singing is a superior activity.

Suppose the organization that controlled the venue could instead have kept it available and introduced her to ceramics. In that alternative she would have enjoyed the first month of singing and then voluntarily moved to pottery. Her later life, including her interests, would have been just as good. These stipulations identify a possible case; they are not empirical generalizations about how people respond to deprivation.

To distinguish the comparisons precisely, label four histories. In **N**, the organization undertakes no package: the choir remains open and Mira never discovers ceramics. In **L**, it closes the venue and supplies no new activity. In **R**, it closes the venue and subsequently introduces ceramics. In **E**, it keeps the venue available and introduces ceramics. The organization can choose E at the same relevant cost as R. Initially suppose that if it did not undertake R, it would choose N. The no-package counterfactual is therefore N, even though E is feasible.

First, is recovery beneficial conditional on closure? R is better than L. This establishes remedial improvement. Second, does R leave Mira at least as well off as she would have been without that package? If R is at least as good as N, it establishes **welfare restoration relative to N**. Third, is R best for her among the available interventions? On the stated facts, E is better than R. Restoration can coexist with an avoidable welfare shortfall.

The event removed in the second question is the whole package. Removing closure alone while holding the ceramics intervention fixed instead yields E. Neither counterfactual is the uniquely correct meaning of “without the loss.” They answer different questions. A claim about restoration must name its reference event and the resulting baseline.

This qualification is substantive. Suppose another organization had already committed to providing ceramics regardless of closure. The same realized sequence could then be evaluated against a no-closure history containing ceramics. Restoration against that history could fail, although it succeeds against N in the original case. Nothing mysterious has happened to Mira's actual welfare: the contrasted history has changed. What would be misleading is to report these relational results as if they identified an unqualified property of her recovery.

Nor does welfare restoration establish that an organization has discharged a compensatory obligation. Benefits already owed, rights infringements, and the appropriate normative baseline can matter to compensatory credit. Eggert argues that net beneficiaries may retain compensation claims and that relevant counterfactuals sometimes concern what agents should have done (2024, abstract and §1). My comparison does not settle those rectificatory questions. “Restoration” here describes a welfare relation, not payment of a moral debt.

The positive result is that a new life can be better than the no-package alternative yet worse than another available route. Mira's sincere statement that her life now goes well is compatible with this. Even a preference for ceramics over singing does not establish that closing the choir was the best way to introduce her to ceramics.

## What desire theory must and need not say

The background problem is familiar. Heathwood discusses changing tastes and considers concurrent desire satisfaction as a way to avoid imposing things people once wanted but no longer want (2006, §1.1, pp. 541–542). The relevant lesson is that an earlier desire need not govern what should now be provided. It would be strange to insist that Mira return to singing solely because she used to love it. Recognizing this does not require treating her missed first month as worthless.

A different familiar problem concerns the acquisition of desires. Dietz distinguishes proactive desire satisfactionism, on which acquiring new satisfied desires can benefit someone, from reactive approaches that resist this implication. His discussion also presses the importance of satisfying desires once acquired, and of a bystander's ensuring satisfaction of desires someone else will create (2023, §§2–3). Those issues reveal the cost of treating the origin of a desire as sufficient to exclude it from welfare. The present proposal takes no such exclusionary route. It allows ceramics to become a basic good for Mira and asks which complete histories should be compared.

Nothing in the argument requires the claim that all desire satisfaction is intrinsically good. A hedonist, an objective-list theorist, or a pluralist can apply the same comparison. Its application within a desire theory yields a limited compatibility result: subjectivism about what makes lives good allows differences between routes to a new set of desires to matter. Differences in missed satisfaction, frustration, and available routes can all matter on the theory's own terms.

The key distinction is between respecting old desires forever and counting what happened while they mattered. Suppose Mira genuinely enjoyed each of the first month's rehearsals in the no-closure history. The value of those episodes need not depend on her continuing to want singing years later. Similarly, a month of genuine frustration does not become a month of fulfilment when she changes her interests. Giving her current desires authority over present provision does not by itself give them authority to rewrite every earlier episode.

That claim needs a qualification. Some desires concern extended achievements or later events. Their satisfaction may not be settled when the desire ceases. Bruckner's abstract explicitly defends the possibility that later fulfilment of past desires affects past welfare (2013). My argument does not presuppose that every prudential fact about the past is settled immediately. The choir case instead specifies completed episodes: wanted rehearsals that did or did not occur, and frustration that was or was not experienced. No later fulfilment can turn a missed rehearsal into an attended one. A theory may still claim that later attitudes transform its value, but that is an additional holistic thesis, not a consequence of allowing tastes to change.

## The portable recovery constraint

The argument can be stated without numbers. Take two feasible interventions. In one, a person undergoes a loss and then receives an enrichment. In the other, she receives the same relevant enrichment without the loss. Suppose three conditions hold.

First, the better early period in the second history matters prudentially. There is genuinely more fulfilment or less frustration there, according to the theory under consideration. Second, the subsequent lives are equally good for the person: the enrichment has not been weakened, delayed, or made less authentic by removing the loss. Third, there is no further whole-life feature that reverses the advantage of the better early period. Under these conditions, an ordinary temporal dominance principle ranks the second history higher: an otherwise equally good life is better when one of its periods is better.

This is not a proof of a complete theory of welfare. The substantive work lies in identifying when the conditions hold and preventing a shift of comparison from hiding their relevance. The conclusion is nevertheless stronger than the claim that adaptation is sometimes incomplete. Even restoration relative to the no-package history does not establish that the package is best for the person. That comparative claim requires attention to other feasible routes.

An ordinal ordering suffices to make the point: E is better than R, R is better than N, and N is better than L. R improves on both unsupported closure and ordinary continuation; E nevertheless improves on R. No cardinal measurement or subtraction of welfare quantities is needed. Calling R an improvement relative to N supplies no ranking of R against E.

This principle must not become a rule that the best conceivable life is always the baseline for restoration. N remains the stipulated no-package history even when E is better. Restoration relative to N and the ranking of feasible interventions are separate assessments. An independently valuable enrichment can restore welfare relative to N; its availability without closure explains why R is nevertheless not the best available intervention. This distinction allows both facts to be reported without inflating either one.

## Improvement and reasons for choice

There is a close established debate here. Carlson, Johansson, and Risberg use cases in which the only counterfactually beneficial action is not the best available option. They argue that this conflicts with plausible principles linking benefit and prudential permissibility or requirement (2024, Three Buttons, Four Buttons, Claims 1–4). Their conclusion challenges the counterfactual comparative account of harm and benefit. The present account instead retains explicit counterfactual improvement while declining to treat it as a sufficient decision criterion. This difference is a substantive limit on what the word “benefit” can do here.

Consider why the restriction is justified. “Better than what would otherwise happen” compares each action with its own fallback. “Best available” compares the actions with each other. Varying fallbacks can vary the first result while leaving all obtainable welfare levels fixed. Consequently, inference from the first relation to the second needs an additional premise connecting the fallbacks. In particular, only one action can improve on its fallback without thereby beating all alternatives. A chooser seeking the best outcome should compare obtainable outcomes directly.

This does not refute every objection to counterfactual accounts of ordinary benefit. Someone may reasonably reserve “benefit” for a notion with stronger normative connections. My claim concerns the legitimate, narrower relation of specified-counterfactual improvement. Rejecting its sufficiency for choice does not render it useless. It can answer whether a particular intervention improved an otherwise expected history, just as conditional remedial improvement answers whether help was effective after a loss. Neither question need be the final question for action.

The reason this matters for changing desires is that the welfare currency and the comparison structure are independent choices. We may count newly acquired satisfactions fully while rejecting an inference from improvement against a poor fallback to superiority among available routes. No prohibition on manufactured desires follows, and none is needed for this limited result. A proactive satisfactionist can accept it throughout.

## Why portability cannot be assumed

The obvious objection is that the two histories are unrealistically easy to match. Perhaps losing the choir is exactly what enables Mira to care deeply about ceramics. Had singing remained available, she would have declined the invitation. Perhaps the process of overcoming disappointment is itself meaningful to her. Perhaps time spent singing would crowd out the social relationships that make ceramics valuable. Then the enrichment is not portable in the required sense.

This objection marks the boundary of the argument. Practical accessibility is stronger than logical possibility. Evidence for it in a concrete version of the example would include an invitation arranged independently of closure, Mira’s known willingness to attend, compatible scheduling, and no benefit dependent on experiencing the closure. In that version removing closure would leave ceramics in place, so the no-closure baseline would be E, not N. The superiority comparison survives; the restoration claim must change with the baseline. An organization cannot assume it can install the same later fulfilment by offering the same class. Nor can an evaluator invent a psychologically impossible combination of every benefit and no cost. The no-loss alternative must include a credible route to equally good later welfare, with its time and resources counted. Identical desires or activities are unnecessary: different pursuits can suffice if there is independent support for their equal welfare value. Merely matching the number or strength of desires does not establish that equality for every desire theory.

But the objection also identifies what is needed to establish the package’s superiority. If a loss is presented as necessary to the best available outcome because it enabled adaptation, we should ask what exactly made it necessary. Did it supply an indispensable experience, remove a genuine obstacle, or merely happen to precede access to a previously neglected opportunity? These are different causal structures. In the last structure, the loss receives credit for an enrichment that could have been supplied directly. A story about personal growth does not settle which structure obtains.

This does not assign a universal presumption that loss is unnecessary. It identifies evidence needed when its alleged indispensability is doing justificatory work. If ceramics was already attractive to Mira and a compatible schedule was available, the portable alternative is plausible. If her new interests depend on a deep transformation inseparable from the loss, the comparison must be revised. The value of adaptation is compatible with both structures and so cannot discriminate between them by itself.

Nor is this a requirement that Mira herself wish she had taken the alternative route. A person's retrospective endorsement is important evidence. It may also contribute to welfare if she wants her life to have a certain history. If she has a powerful, satisfied desire specifically to have overcome the closure, the two later lives are no longer equal in the stipulated respects. That is a reason to revisit the case, not to suppress the endorsement. But simply preferring her current activity to her former one does not establish a preference for having been deprived of the former activity before making the change.

## Two further objections

One might object that temporal dominance neglects the shape of a life. A life of overcoming adversity may be better than an otherwise richer life lacking that development. The argument does not assume unrestricted addition across time. Its third condition explicitly excludes a compensating whole-life difference. Where the closure contributes meaning, coherence, or achievement, those are candidate benefits to be assessed. What would be question-begging is to infer such a benefit merely from the fact that the person recovered. Recovery must not serve simultaneously as the evidence that later life goes well and as unexplained proof that its painful route improved the whole.

The example can also be simplified to weaken this objection. Suppose the closure causes a short interval of bored disappointment that Mira never regards as transformative. The ceramics invitation had already been arranged, and she would have accepted it on the same date whether the choir remained open or not. In both histories she later evaluates her development in the same way. A theorist can deny that the lost rehearsals matter even here. But such a denial now needs a substantive account of why an otherwise unredeemed improvement in a lived period has no value. It cannot rest merely on the familiar fact that people move on.

A different limitation is more decisive against any general vindication of desire theory. Suppose a controller closes the choir and instantaneously changes Mira's interests before any wanted rehearsal is missed or frustration occurs. Stipulate equal lifetime fulfilment and no other welfare difference. The first condition of the argument fails. There is no welfare shortfall for the portable alternative to avoid. My account supplies no welfare reason against the manipulation in this case.

That is an accepted boundary, not an invitation to invent a hidden deficit. Objections concerning consent or control may remain, and another theory of welfare may identify a prudential cost. But the present argument does not derive either from desire satisfaction alone. Similarly, if the loss is indispensable to the uniquely best attainable life, the portability argument gives no reason to prefer another route. It answers a restricted inference about recovery, not the whole adaptive-preference objection.

The contribution consequently uses ordinary opportunity-cost reasoning. It does not discover that principle or resolve the general harm-and-benefit debate. Its more focused use is diagnostic. If the no-loss alternative is rejected, the dispute may concern feasibility. If it is feasible but not better, the dispute may concern the temporal or holistic structure of welfare. If it is better but the worse option is permissible, the dispute concerns the relation between welfare rankings and practical permission. Mira may have latitude to choose a less good life; an organization's role may introduce different obligations. Calling her new desires adaptive settles none of these questions.

## What recovery establishes

A person's new satisfactions should not be discounted merely because they arose after deprivation. Equally, their presence does not establish that the route through deprivation was best for her. The argument supports three compatible assessments: recovery improved the deprived history, the package restored welfare relative to a specified no-package baseline, and another feasible intervention would have produced a better life.

Keeping those assessments distinct preserves a welfare reason to favor the better route without denying the reality of recovery. The question is not simply whether a changed desire benefits someone. It is also which alternatives that judgment compares, and whether the same or equally valuable enrichment was available without the earlier loss.
