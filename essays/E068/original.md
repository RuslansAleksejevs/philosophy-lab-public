# What a Complete Explanation Must Leave Open

A machine gives a prize to one of two people. Its rules guarantee a winner and give Anna and Ben equal chances. The process contains no bias or earlier feature that favors either person. Anna wins. We know how the machine works. We also know why the prize was offered and why these two people qualified. Have we explained everything about the result?

There are several sensible answers because “everything” can express several demands. The machine may causally produce Anna's victory. Its rules may explain why someone wins rather than nobody. Yet nothing before the final random event may favor Anna over Ben. These claims can all be true. Trouble begins when a theory moves between them without saying so.

This essay separates three kinds of completeness. **Causal completeness** requires a cause for every event. **Fact completeness** requires an explanation for every contingent fact, where a contingent fact could have been otherwise. **Difference completeness** requires an explanation of why the actual possibility obtains instead of each genuine alternative within the theory's scope. The third demand is strongest. It does not follow merely from the first, and whether it follows from the second is disputed.

I defend a conditional conclusion. A theory cannot use a common explanation of several possible outcomes to establish difference completeness. Nor can it establish difference completeness merely by moving the difference into an earlier contingent event. This does not disprove chance or free choice. It shows that causal production, explanation of a broad fact, and a reason for the actual difference support different metaphysical conclusions.

## Why a difference can matter to completeness

Suppose the machine has three possible outcomes: Anna wins, Ben wins, or nobody wins because it is switched off. The fact that the machine is working explains why someone wins rather than nobody. The same fact would obtain if Ben won, so it does not explain why Anna wins rather than Ben.

An explanation need not guarantee its outcome. If a defect gives Anna a nine-in-ten chance, the defect may explain why she won even though Ben could have won. The point is that the defect bears on the difference. It treats Anna's victory differently from Ben's.

Van Fraassen argues that a why-question depends on the alternatives under discussion and on the kind of answer that counts as relevant. The same event may be explained under one comparison and remain unexplained under another. He also rejects the thought that complete information automatically forms one context-free explanation. These ideas appear in chapter 5 of *The Scientific Image*, especially sections 2.8–2.9 and 4.3–4.4. I use this established contrastive framework for a narrower purpose: testing what follows from a metaphysical claim of complete explanation.

The key premise is this:

> When A and B are mutually exclusive, genuine possibilities, a difference-complete explanation of A must contain some feature that plays a different explanatory role in an otherwise matched B-history.

Call this the difference-coverage premise. It does not say that the feature must make A more probable. It might be a deterministic perturbation, a particular exercise of agency, or another relation that explains why the histories diverge. Nor does the premise say that every legitimate explanation must cover every contrast. It says what a claim of *difference completeness* must cover.

Why accept it? “Anna won” already excludes “Ben won.” But merely citing a property shared by both possible victories tells us only why the actual event belongs to a broader class, such as someone's winning. If that were enough for difference completeness, arbitrary broadening would make completeness cheap. Anna's victory is also an event, an occurrence, and something that happened. An explanation of why something happened would then explain every detail of what happened. It plainly does not. A complete account of the specific obtaining of A must therefore cover what separates A from the alternatives it accepts as real.

This defense has a limit. Someone may define fact completeness so that any cause of A explains the whole fact A, including its incompatibility with B. That view denies the difference-coverage premise. It is coherent. My point is then about what the view licenses: it cannot rely on that explanation alone to claim that the account shows why A rather than B obtained. Calling the cause an explanation of “the whole fact” does not add a discriminating feature.

## Four questions that should stay separate

An account of an outcome can succeed in at least four ways:

1. It can identify what produced the outcome.
2. It can make the outcome more probable than an alternative.
3. It can identify a feature that explains why the histories diverged.
4. It can give a sufficient reason in the demanding sense that leaves no contingency unexplained.

These successes do not coincide. A later report can raise our confidence without producing the result. A deterministic cause can explain a result without anyone assigning probabilities. A stochastic process can produce a token outcome although nothing earlier favors its exact time. A basic act by an agent may distinguish two histories without being selected by a prior motive.

Consider a pencil balanced almost perfectly on its tip. A tiny gust pushes it left. The general setup is symmetric, but the token gust is not. The gust does not merely rename the fall; it exerts force before the pencil moves, and changing the gust while holding the rest fixed changes the fall. It can therefore explain why the pencil falls left rather than right. The explanation passes the difference-coverage test even if the coarse description “an unstable pencil was released” does not.

Now consider radioactive decay. An atom has a constant chance of decaying in each short interval. It decays in interval ten, triggers a detector, and opens Anna's box. The decay clearly explains why Anna's box opens: it stands in the relevant causal chain, and without it the detector would not fire then. But what explains why the atom decays in interval ten rather than eleven?

There are two serious answers. One says that the stochastic law, the atom, and the actual decay together explain the timing. On this view, stochastic causation can explain a token event without an earlier probability difference. The other says that the law explains the distribution and the decay event records the timing, but nothing explains the final transition at precisely that interval. Symmetry alone cannot decide between these accounts. A theory of stochastic explanation must do that work.

The distinction still helps. On the first answer, the theory should state the explanatory relation by which a probabilistic law plus its token realization explains the exact timing. On the second, the exact timing is basic. Either answer may be defensible. Neither permits us to infer, merely from the law's covering all possible intervals, that some prior reason selected interval ten.

## Moving a difference backward

Suppose Anna won because a selector entered state A; had it entered state B, Ben would have won. We have found an earlier event that explains the later result. That is real progress. We now know where the histories split.

If entry into A was determined by a tiny electrical fluctuation, that fluctuation may explain the split just as the gust explains the pencil's fall. If entry into A was a stochastic transition, the dispute about stochastic explanation arises. If the theory treats the transition as basic, it stops there.

There is no defect in stopping for an ordinary explanation. Lipton's opening discussion in “Contrastive Explanation” notes that a cause can explain an effect even when the cause itself is not explained. We need not know the cause of every cause to understand a broken window. The issue appears only when an account also promises a reason for every contingent difference.

Adding intermediate stages does not by itself satisfy that stronger promise. A switch may depend on a signal, the signal on a selector, and the selector on a fluctuation. Each link may explain the next. But if the account ends with a contingent split that it treats as basic, it has found a causal explanation for later facts while accepting a limit to difference completeness.

This yields a practical audit. Begin with a difference between possibilities that the theory accepts. Follow the explanation to the earlier point on which the difference depends. Then ask what the theory says about that split. Does it explain the split? Does it argue that only one option was possible? Does it show that the two options describe the same reality? Or does it stop and accept the split as basic? The audit does not decide which answer is true. It prevents a change in description from hiding the answer.

## Choice between equals

Leibniz and Clarke disagree about a similar case. Leibniz holds that choice requires a reason and denies that God selects between alternatives when nothing recommends one over the other. He also argues that some apparent alternatives are only different descriptions of the same state. These responses differ: the first demands a sufficient reason; the second denies that there are two realities to explain. See Leibniz's *Fourth Paper*, sections 1–6, 13, 15, and 18–19.

Clarke replies that an agent need not behave like a balance with equal weights. There may be good reason to act although the available ways of acting are indifferent. The power to act belongs to the agent and need not come from unequal motives. See Clarke's *Fourth Reply*, sections 1–2.

Suppose two roads are equally good and a traveler must get home. Her need to get home explains why she takes a road instead of staying put. Her exercise of active power may also cause and explain why she goes left. An agent-causal theory need not treat the choice as an event awaiting a prior motive that favors left. The agent herself may be the contrastive source.

That is a direct challenge to any account that equates explanation with a prior favoring reason. The difference-coverage premise does not settle it. If the agent's exercise of power plays a different explanatory role in the left-history than in the right-history, it can satisfy the premise as a causal explanation.

Yet a further question remains for a Leibnizian demand. Was there a sufficient reason why the agent exercised her power in this way? Clarke may reject the need for one. He can hold that the action is explained by the agent even though no prior reason determines or favors it. This gives causal and perhaps difference explanation without the strongest form of sufficient reason. Describing that position as simply “unexplained” would beg the question. The exact claim is that it leaves no *prior favoring reason* for the selection.

The same issue matters in accounts of why this world exists. A reason to create some good world may explain creation rather than non-creation. If several worlds are equally eligible, that common reason is compatible with each. It therefore neither entails the actual world nor supports it over the others. A particular divine choice may causally explain which world exists, as Clarke's view suggests. But uniqueness or selection by sufficient reason requires a further premise. The common goodness of the eligible worlds cannot supply it because that goodness does not distinguish them.

## What each completeness claim permits

We can now state the consequences more carefully.

Causal completeness says that every event has a producing source. If stochastic transitions or basic exercises of agency are causes, this thesis may include them. It does not by itself say that every cause was favored by a prior reason.

Fact completeness says that every contingent fact has an explanation. Its force depends on what counts as explaining a fact. If a cause of A automatically explains all that A excludes, fact completeness may be weaker than difference completeness. If explaining A requires covering its actualizing difference from B, the two claims draw closer. This is the contested bridge, and no argument should cross it silently.

Difference completeness says that every genuine contingent split within scope is covered by something that plays a different explanatory role across the alternatives. This does not require probability raising, but it does require more than a feature common to every outcome. A stronger principle may add that the differentiating feature must itself have a sufficient reason. That creates a regress or demands necessity, identity of apparent alternatives, or some acceptable stopping structure.

This classification changes what can be inferred. If a principle says only that every event has a cause, it may allow several worlds whose originating cause treats them alike. The principle cannot by itself show why this world exists, why it was preferred, or why every difference in reality comes from a difference in prior reasons. If a theory needs those conclusions, it needs difference completeness or another premise that does the same work.

## Conclusion

The argument rules out one way of defending a complete explanation: explain a broad outcome, then treat that as an explanation of every specific result. It also rules out treating the mere relocation of a contingent split as its elimination.

It does not rule out chance, stochastic explanation, or agent causation. A random transition may causally explain a later result. A token perturbation may distinguish symmetric outcomes. An agent may explain a choice without a prior motive that favors it. These possibilities show why causal explanation, probabilistic support, contrastive discrimination, and sufficient reason must remain separate.

A theory should therefore name the completeness it claims. If it promises causes, producing sources may be enough. If it promises an explanation of every contingent difference, a reason common to several alternatives is not enough. And if it accepts a basic stochastic transition or act of selection, it should present that as its considered stopping point. The important question is not simply whether the world has an explanation. It is which differences between possible worlds the explanation covers, and what kind of explanation it offers at the point where they divide.
