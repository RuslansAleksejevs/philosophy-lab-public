# When Repair Becomes a Bargaining Chip

Ecological restoration can be valuable even when the destruction that occasioned it was wrong. A repaired wetland can sustain lives and relationships worth protecting. Yet a promise to restore does not automatically justify destruction in advance. Sometimes restoration changes a project's acceptability; sometimes it merely allows the proponent to present fulfillment of an existing duty as a concession that others must purchase.

The difference is especially important when control is divided. An agency can control conservation funds while a separate regulator controls development permission. If the agency wrongfully withholds conservation unless permission is granted, the regulator may confront a choice between a destructive package with substantial conservation benefits and a still worse ecological outcome. What the regulator should accept and what the agency was entitled to demand are then different questions.

This essay defends a limited constraint: fulfillment of an independently binding, presently feasible conservation duty cannot, merely because its bearer threatens to withhold it, supply a missing justification for new ecological damage. The constraint is an application of an established argument about moral baselines. The further task is to explain why applying it to the proponent does not require a regulator to ignore the real consequences of refusal. The same proposed restoration can lack the justificatory role claimed for it by one actor while providing another actor with a powerful reason to act.

## The inherited baseline problem

Barry and Cullity distinguish a standard baseline for assessing risk from a moralized baseline that includes independently obligatory risk reduction. Their Business Opportunity example concerns someone who cleans a river under contract and then adds pollution through recreational boating. The cleaning cannot simply cancel the demand to justify the additional risk. Their analysis also distinguishes independently sourced duties from duties to offset one's own new risk: the mere fact that an offset is obligatory does not disqualify it. This is a direct predecessor of the duty-based argument developed here. I apply that distinction to conservation governance without claiming to derive it anew or to assess their wider theory of risk. [Barry and Cullity 2022, introduction and §II](https://doi.org/10.1086/716871).

Related environmental literature already questions which comparisons have moral authority. Edvardsson Björnberg considers separating licensing and compensation when assessing biodiversity offsets. Armstrong distinguishes anticipated opportunities from the opportunities people should have when calculating conservation costs. Neither the licensing distinction nor the need to scrutinize opportunity baselines is new. [Edvardsson Björnberg 2020, §7](https://doi.org/10.1111/theo.12287); [Armstrong 2023, “Status quo baseline”](https://doi.org/10.1111/cobi.14018).

Existing policy analysis is not merely a demand for accurate forecasts. Maron and colleagues assess whether withdrawal from conservation commitments is reasonable, distinguishing resource constraints from withdrawal by adequately resourced countries. IUCN requires consideration of alternatives, allows reference scenarios better than predicted outcomes, and warns against making conservation funding depend on destruction elsewhere. The argument below explains a division of responsibility within that already normative territory. [Maron et al. 2016, Figure 1 and “Counterfactual scenarios”](https://doi.org/10.1111/conl.12222); [IUCN 2016, §§5–8 and 10.2](https://iucn.org/sites/default/files/2022-06/iucn_biodiversity_offsets_policy_jan_29_2016_0.pdf).

I use “causal additionality” for a deliberately thin criterion: the conservation gain would not occur without the proposed package. This is not a complete definition of a valid offset under those policies. The question is what follows even when that thin criterion is satisfied beyond doubt. Can an accurately predicted gain provide an illegitimate reason for its proponent but a legitimate reason for the regulator?

## A package with two decision makers

Suppose a regional agency has a binding duty to restore a degraded marsh under a justified conservation plan. Funds and practical capacity are available; no competing emergency suspends the duty. A separate regulator decides whether the agency may construct a recreational development that will destroy another habitat. The regulator cannot order immediate expenditure of the conservation funds, and enforcement through a separate institution would arrive too late to save the marsh. This separation of powers gives the agency control over restoration without giving the regulator an equally timely way to compel it.

The agency announces that restoration will occur only if development is approved. The announcement is credible. Restoration would produce greater ecological benefits than development would destroy. Stipulate favorable comparisons even after accounting for timing, risk, location, affected organisms and human relationships; failures of measurement are not the present objection. Stipulate also that the recreational benefits, considered apart from the threatened withholding of restoration, are insufficient to justify the development's ecological burden.

Three courses matter. The agency could restore without developing. It could develop and restore. Or it could do neither. Relative to the agency's credible refusal to do anything, the package improves the ecological outcome. Relative to fulfilling the duty without development, it adds damage whose recreational benefits do not justify it. The agency can choose the latter comparison's better alternative. The regulator, under the stipulated enforcement limits, cannot currently secure it.

This is not a dispute about whether the restored marsh has value. Both comparisons fully count that value. They differ because one assesses the agency's conduct among the choices for which it remains responsible, while the other assesses the regulator's response to choices the agency has already restricted. Treating the agency's threatened inaction as the only comparison for both actors allows its wrongful refusal to rewrite the conditions of its own justification.

## What the constraint defeats

An independently binding duty directs its bearer's choices. Announcing an intention to violate it does not remove compliance from the options against which the bearer's conduct must be assessed. Otherwise, the agency most reliable in refusing its duty would acquire the strongest apparent argument for permission to impose new damage. A cooperative agency restoring the marsh unconditionally would have to justify the development on its other merits. An uncooperative agency could cite the identical restoration as a concession. Mere willingness to refuse a duty cannot produce this moral advantage.

The conclusion concerns a particular reason, not automatic contamination of everything in the package. Suppose instead that the development provides compelling public benefits sufficient to justify its ecological burden independently of the threatened withholding. The threat can remain wrongful while development and restoration are permissible. It would be incorrect to infer from defective bargaining that the underlying land use must be forbidden. What fails is the appeal to withheld duty fulfillment as the missing reason for permission. Other reasons may supply it.

Nor is every use of obligatory restoration invalid. A duty to repair the very damage under consideration may legitimately govern an acceptable project. That duty is not an independent conservation requirement that the proponent is attempting to discharge twice. The distinction is between repair owed because a permissible disturbance occurs and conservation already owed whether or not the disturbance occurs. Difficult cases may involve mixed obligations; the clear case should not be expanded by pretending that their independence is obvious.

The agency need not reason circularly. It may openly acknowledge that its withholding is unjustified and use it purely as pressure. Its conduct is then objectionable because of wrongful control of the available alternatives, not because it commits a logical error. A circular defense arises only if it tries to justify withholding by the need to secure permission while justifying permission solely by its threatened withholding. The main argument rests on continuing responsibility for a feasible duty, whether or not the agent offers that defense.

The constraint is therefore narrower than a general test of whether a project has “adequate reasons.” It identifies a benefit that cannot play one claimed justificatory role. It does not decide which independent development benefits are sufficient, settle all conservation duties, or establish a complete theory of environmental permission.

## Why the regulator may nevertheless accept

One response is to apply the duty-inclusive comparison everywhere: restoration without development is the right baseline, so the regulator should refuse the package. But a baseline used to identify what the agency owes is not a prediction that the regulator can make it perform that duty. The marsh can be lost while both actors correctly describe the obligation. Refusing to count a real benefit in the regulator's choice would confuse recognition of an entitlement with power to realize it.

Another response is to use the predicted no-project outcome everywhere. Approval saves more ecological value, so both the regulator's acceptance and the agency's proposal are justified. That reasoning makes the opposite mistake. It imports the regulator's enforcement limitation into the agency's own options. The agency can restore unconditionally; its responsibility does not disappear because another institution cannot compel it quickly enough.

A consistent assessment can consequently say all three of the following. The agency wrongfully imposes the condition. The ecological burden remains unjustified by the reasons the agency offers. Yet the regulator has a serious reason to accept the package to avert a worse loss. Acceptance is not conclusively required: the regulator must also consider its own duties, the distribution of burdens, and the consequences of authorizing damage. The point is that the marsh's rescue is relevant to its decision even though the agency cannot use its own refusal to rescue the marsh to manufacture permission.

This permits an asymmetry in assessments of connected actions. The regulator may permissibly authorize an act that the agency should not have proposed in this way. That is not an assertion that the act is simply permissible and impermissible in the same respect. The regulator answers for exercising its authority under a constrained choice; the agency answers for imposing the condition and for its own destructive conduct among a wider set of alternatives. An all-things-considered defense of the regulator must explain why facilitating that conduct is permissible as a lesser evil. Beneficial consequences alone do not settle that further question.

The distinction has an institutional consequence beyond how blame is described. Approval should not by itself discharge any independent obligations that were excluded from the authority's control. A regulator might accept the immediate package while preserving enforcement of the original conservation commitment and scrutiny of the new damage. Whether additional remedies are appropriate depends on what wrongs remain and on the regulator's powers. The important point is that an emergency permission is not evidence that the offered conservation gain was freely disposable bargaining currency.

## The problem of repeated acceptance

Separation of responsibilities is not an institutional cure. If agencies learn that credible withholding wins permission, a repeated policy of rescue can encourage more threats. Explicitly denying endorsement may do little: actors respond to what acceptance enables, not just to how it is described. Conversely, a categorical refusal can leave valuable habitats exposed to predictable and irreversible loss.

Immediate and prospective decisions must therefore be distinguished. At the moment of threatened loss, the regulator may have no harmless way to recover the excluded option. Earlier institutional design may have offered ways to prevent its exclusion: protecting committed funds from unilateral diversion, establishing timely enforcement, or separating approval decisions from an agency's control of already required conservation. These measures are plausible responses to the example's structure, not empirically demonstrated solutions for every jurisdiction.

Their philosophical importance is that they target the gap between justified alternatives and practically attainable ones. Merely selecting a morally improved baseline for the ledger does not close that gap. An institution also needs capacity to secure the conduct that the baseline assumes. Where it lacks that capacity, both the short-term consequences of refusal and the long-term effects of acquiescence belong in its decision. The present argument specifies what must remain distinct during that evaluation; it does not determine the optimal strategy against every threat.

This also guards against a misleading appeal to consequentialism. In the stipulated case, unconditional restoration is better overall than the agency's package because the independent development benefits are insufficient. A consequentialist can therefore criticize the agency while favoring the regulator's acceptance if acceptance produces the best result among its actual options. If development instead creates sufficient independent value, that argument changes. What remains constant is the need to compare each actor's conduct with the alternatives available to that actor, including the future effects of its choices.

## Boundaries of the argument

The duty premise does substantive work. A private association with resources to restore land does not necessarily owe restoration of this particular site now. Its resources may have competing justified uses; no general duty of unlimited generosity follows from physical capacity. Where the obligation is genuinely disputed, the constraint cannot decide the case until that dispute is resolved. Armstrong's analysis of conservation opportunity costs shows why those underlying distributive judgments cannot simply be replaced with present willingness to pay or accept. The present essay leaves their full specification open. [Armstrong 2023, “Status quo baseline” and “Willingness to accept baseline”](https://doi.org/10.1111/cobi.14018).

Equally, some projects genuinely generate otherwise unavailable resources. A development that satisfies independent requirements concerning its purpose, alternatives and burdens may fund appropriate conservation without holding an existing duty hostage. The constraint does not establish that such projects are acceptable; it simply does not condemn them on the ground examined here.

Repair can also properly affect prospective justification. A necessary flood barrier might cause temporary ecological disturbance, with reliable on-site rehabilitation reducing the burden enough to make construction proportionate. That is an example about repair generally, not automatically a biodiversity offset: policy distinguishes rehabilitation at the affected site from compensation for residual impacts. Its lesson is limited but important. We need not assess every project as though repair were impossible. We must instead distinguish repair that changes a project's effects from an independent duty repackaged as a discretionary inducement. [IUCN 2016, §6 and glossary](https://iucn.org/sites/default/files/2022-06/iucn_biodiversity_offsets_policy_jan_29_2016_0.pdf).

Finally, the account assumes that ecological damage can demand justification and that conservation obligations sometimes bind. It does not settle whether those obligations ultimately rest on animal welfare, human interests, ecological relationships or non-instrumental natural value. Its application to environmental governance depends on some such grounds, but the distinction between an actor's duty and another actor's response does not require resolving every dispute about them.

Ecological accounting and moral responsibility answer connected but different questions. A conservation gain can be real, additional and worth securing without being a concession its controller is entitled to demand payment for. Recognizing that limit should not require sacrificing the gain when institutions cannot immediately enforce the duty. It should require preserving the distinction between rescuing value under a wrongful condition and justifying the creation of that condition.
