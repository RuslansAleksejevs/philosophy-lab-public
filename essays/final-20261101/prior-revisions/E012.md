# When a Remedy Becomes Evidence

Consider a hypothetical municipal fund that reimburses residents for damage caused by its contractors. It provides inspections and undertakes to preserve the photographs. Mara files a timely claim. Before deciding it, the fund deletes its photographic archive to save money, despite knowing its preservation obligation. The deletion is indiscriminate: nobody examines the photographs or selects inconvenient claims. The fund considers Mara’s testimony, the inspection receipt, and reasonable alternative sources, but cannot adequately determine the contractor’s responsibility.

The fund recognizes that it owes Mara a remedy for destroying her opportunity to substantiate the claim. It awards a payment. Later, another office receives a list of successful claims and treats Mara’s entry as evidence that the contractor damaged her home. Alternatively, suppose the fund refuses reimbursement but separately compensates her for the lost opportunity. A list of unsuccessful claims then becomes evidence that her accusation was unfounded. In either case, a decision about what to do under damaged evidential conditions appears to answer a question the proceeding did not determine.

The problem can survive a fair payment and an honest hearing. I argue that wrongful evidence loss can leave a continuing duty to help the affected person contest consequential misrepresentations of the decision, where the loss still impairs a means of defence protected by the original undertaking. Accurate communication sometimes fulfils that duty. Sometimes assistance remains due after another agency distorts an accurate communication. This is an application of a reparative obligation, with limits both on the assistance owed and on what it can establish about the past.

## 1. Three questions hidden in one outcome

Three propositions must be distinguished:

1. The contractor damaged Mara’s home.
2. The fund wrongfully destroyed evidence relevant to her claim.
3. Mara is now entitled to the payment awarded by the fund.

The proceeding may establish the second and third without establishing the first. The entitlement need not be a polite fiction. If the fund has a justified power to award compensation for an evidential wrong, exercising that power can create a real entitlement. Mara may insist that the money is hers without citing the award as a finding against the contractor.

Nor is contractor responsibility therefore unknowable, or even unknown. Mara might have witnessed the damage. An outside investigator might reasonably give her testimony greater weight than the fund did. The qualification is that this payment decision did not determine contractor responsibility. It is not that nobody has adequate grounds for believing the contractor responsible. Institutions overreach when they turn their own non-findings into declarations of universal ignorance.

The propositions can diverge in the other direction. Recovered evidence might establish that the contractor caused no damage, while the fund still owes Mara compensation for expense or delay caused by the destruction. An evidential wrong does not guarantee a true underlying claim; a true claim need not involve an evidential wrong. These are differences in the grounds of entitlement, not degrees of sympathy.

Institutions nevertheless need categorical outcomes. Payment cannot remain both authorized and unauthorized while a fact-finder suspends judgment. But a categorical practical answer need not settle every historical question on which the dispute originally depended. A remedial entitlement can provide another ground for bringing deliberation to an end.

The distinction between action and belief is familiar. The further question concerns what a decision communicates to people who did not observe the proceeding. A record may correctly tell a cashier that payment is authorized while misleading an investigator about contractor conduct. Its adequacy depends on its use, which may change after its author has stopped attending to it.

## 2. The contribution and its antecedents

The thought that evidence loss itself calls for redress is established. Porat and Stein defend liability for wrongfully worsened uncertainty, including compensation and changes to the burden of persuasion. Friedman discusses remedies adapted to different circumstances. The available comparison is limited to Porat and Stein’s repository abstract and Friedman’s repository introductory passage; their full texts were not accessible. Those passages establish relevant antecedents but do not support a complete comparison of their accounts. (Porat and Stein 1997, abstract; Friedman 1997, repository introduction.)

Gardner supplies a general philosophical foundation for substitute performance after breach. On his continuity thesis, the reasons for an unfulfilled primary obligation may remain and call, all else being equal, for the closest satisfaction still available. The original obligation’s purpose helps identify the appropriate substitute. His discussion of consequential losses likewise connects repair to the reasons underlying the violated norm. A duty to provide reasonable substitute support therefore needs no claim to novelty as a general reparative principle. (Gardner 2011, author eprint, typescript p. 40 and pp. 46–50.)

Legal treatments also recognize the cost of obtaining substitute evidence. In *Thompson*, the majority allowed a preservation-duty claim to proceed; note 7 identified expert and discovery expenses for alternative proof of an alleged cable defect as a possible basis of damages. It did not award those expenses or establish liability. The holding rested on the pleaded special relationship, foreseeable harm, and policy, rather than evidence collection alone. (*Thompson* 1998, §III and nn. 1, 6–7.) The 2015 Rule 37 notes direct initial attention to restoring or replacing lost electronically stored information through discovery and identify expense allocation under other discovery rules as potentially relevant. Replacement efforts should be proportional to the information’s importance; if replacement succeeds, no further measures should follow. The notes separately require prejudice for measures under subdivision (e)(1) and intent to deprive another party of the information’s use for the specified severe measures under (e)(2). Their scope is information subject to preservation for anticipated or ongoing litigation. These are analogies for remedy design, not a general legal duty extending beyond case closure. Mara’s additional problem arises after the first case closes, when another agency misdescribes its outcome. The lost photographs could rebut invented damage without proving contractor responsibility, and assistance could restore a route of defence without resolving that original question.

Nance supplies a closer antecedent concerning the form of adjudication. He separates evidence management from deciding merits and criticizes concealing a remedy for an evidential problem inside an apparently ordinary merits determination. His preferred use of issue preclusion can require facts to be treated as fixed within litigation; he also discusses later malpractice claims. This requires attention to the difference between procedural force and evidence about history, including later uses of a decision. (Nance 2010, pp. 1103–1104, 1110–1114.)

The reception of repair is also an established philosophical concern. Flowerree treats audience uptake and an appropriate reparative response as conditions of successful epistemic reparation. Altanian argues that restorative epistemic reparation must address harmful epistemic practices and recognize victims as contributors, beyond giving them knowledge. These accounts make subsequent reception and testimonial standing central to repair. (Flowerree 2026, §§2–3; Altanian 2024, §4.)

The contribution here is narrower: to apply substitute performance to an established evidential breach, a practically authoritative decision, and a later agency’s error that the claimant remains impaired in resisting. The argument also specifies what assistance must preserve: the decision’s actual grounds, legitimate procedural effects, and room for others’ testimony. It develops existing reparative approaches rather than claiming a new general foundation.

I do not determine the correct monetary remedy. The value of Mara’s lost opportunity, the undertaking, available alternatives, and competing claims on resources all matter. Assume that compensation for the lost reimbursement opportunity has been fairly assessed. This isolates what can remain outstanding after a correct award. The obligation arises from the wrongful impairment, however, not from payment; refusing all redress would not erase it.

## 3. From accurate records to substitute support

The protected function must be specified. Suppose the fund promises to take reasonable care to preserve dated inspection photographs and provide authenticated copies for five years. Its stated purposes include presenting claims and answering later official challenges to them. Residents can reasonably depend on that service instead of arranging duplicate inspections. The undertaking protects their use of the evidence, not merely its internal storage. Closing a payment proceeding does not end the promised access.

This is a substantive premise. A duty to retain material only until an initial decision would not by itself establish the same later obligation. The relevant scope must come from the undertaking or another independent ground, not simply from the fact that assistance would now be useful.

Deletion can damage two interests: Mara’s opportunity to obtain reimbursement and her ability to answer later accounts of the dispute. Compensation for the first need not restore the second. In an easy case, the fund sends a bare denial code that another office predictably reads as a finding that Mara complained falsely. The fund should qualify the record. Yet even a fund that innocently lost the photographs would owe accurate, appropriately qualified communication. This case alone does not establish a specifically reparative duty.

Consider two harder variants. In both, the fund initially sends a clear record: reimbursement was not established, the fund breached its preservation duty, and compensation was awarded for the resulting disadvantage. The first case closes. Two years later, the receiving office migrates its database and drops the explanation. It gives Mara a false-complaint flag, understood as saying that she invented the reported damage, and uses it to reject a new application. The fund neither authorized the alteration nor could reasonably have prevented it. The receiving office owes correction in both variants. What differs is whether the photographs would have helped Mara secure it.

In the first variant, the receiving office offers two routes to removing the flag. An inexpensive factual review accepts authenticated inspection photographs showing that the reported damage existed. Otherwise, Mara must seek formal correction of the imported decision record. That process requires an authenticated account of the original decision and costly procedural assistance; obtaining the certificate alone does not complete the review. These are stipulated features of a hypothetical procedure, not claims about an actual office or a defence of its burdens.

Suppose the lost photographs would have shown the fresh crack Mara reported while leaving open whether the contractor caused it. With the archive available, she could have answered the accusation of invented damage through the inexpensive review. The photographs would not have authenticated what the fund decided. They would have offered a different route to removing the flag. Evidence insufficient to establish contractor responsibility can still rebut the narrower accusation that the reported damage did not exist.

Without the photographs, Mara must take the more expensive route. She cannot afford its additional cost; the fund can provide effective assistance at a cost proportionate to the threatened disadvantage. Funding the additional reasonable work can replace protection once supplied by the photographs, although the replacement concerns the decision record rather than the underlying damage. The fund should certify only what it actually determined. Assistance cannot justify inventing a finding that Mara’s factual account was true.

This differs from obtaining alternative proof of the original allegation, as contemplated in *Thompson*, or replacing information for claims and defences in the litigation addressed by the Rule 37 notes. Here the first proceeding has ended. What needs replacement is a still-protected defensive use of the evidence against another agency’s later error. The substitute need not reproduce the photographs’ content if it restores an effective way to contest that error. Its justification comes from the continuing undertaking, not from extending either legal example into a general rule.

The stipulated content of the photographs makes this a clear positive case. In an actual dispute, destruction alone would not establish that the material was helpful. Its likely relevance would need support from the inspection’s subject, surviving descriptions, and the receiving procedure’s requirements. A genuine lost opportunity can justify assistance without certainty of success. A merely imaginable benefit cannot justify charging every later expense to the custodian.

In the second variant, the office treats the flag strictly as a report of what the fund decided. Correction turns solely on whether the original decision found a false complaint. Only the authenticated decision record answers that question; photographs of a cracked wall do not. Assume that the steps, cost, and prospect of correction would be the same had the photographs survived. The fund still owes appropriate authentication, and the receiving office still owes correction. But deletion supplies no additional reason for the fund to finance this process. Such a reason would require another basis, such as responsibility for the later error or an ordinary duty of assistance.

The comparison holds the receiving office’s mistake fixed and asks what preservation would have changed. It does not ask whether the fund caused the mistake. In the first variant, one institution creates the threat while another has wrongfully removed a protected means of resisting it. In the second, the lost material has no bearing on the available defence. Only the first establishes the further reparative ground at issue.

The principle applied here is that someone who wrongfully impairs another’s protected capacity to resist a foreseeable kind of disadvantage has a presumptive duty to provide a proportionate substitute when that disadvantage materializes and the capacity remains impaired. Gardner’s continuity thesis explains why the original reasons can support such a duty. The purpose of preservation identifies what needs replacing: a usable means of answering official challenges, not merely possession of a correctly labelled document. His account does not itself establish the scope of this particular undertaking; that premise remains necessary.

An accurate decision record may be an adequate substitute in some circumstances and inadequate in others. If it ceases to provide usable protection, its accuracy alone does not settle what repair remains due. Destroying someone’s only means of accessing a service is not adequately answered by offering an unusable replacement and describing it honestly. Similarly, the fund must consider Mara’s continuing impairment, although it need not restore every aspect of her former position. The two variants explain why neither perpetual assistance nor one honest statement provides an adequate general answer.

The temporal and institutional reach is limited. The five-year period has not expired, and answering this official challenge falls within the service’s protected purpose. The particular database error need not have been foreseeable or preventable by the fund. What matters is the foreseeable kind of disadvantage: losing a usable defence against consequential accounts of the claim. This does not establish the same scope for every preservation promise.

The obligation concerns the additional difficulty caused by the breach. If the receiving office supplies effective correction without burdening Mara, no funded substitute is needed. If both offices assist, the same expense cannot be recovered twice. But while neither has supplied it, the receiving office’s responsibility does not make the fund’s repair complete. Dividing costs between them requires further principles. The present argument establishes a claim to proportionate assistance against the fund, not its sole responsibility for every consequence.

Wrongdoing matters to that claim. Compare a fund whose archive was lost in an unforeseeable disaster despite compliance with its undertaking to exercise reasonable care. Both funds may owe inexpensive authentication under ordinary institutional duties. In the first variant, only the fund that breached its undertaking also owes assistance on the reparative ground defended here. That difference can affect correction costs and priority among otherwise comparable demands. An outstanding repair claim should not be treated as merely another application for discretionary help.

This gives Mara no absolute priority over urgent needs or other debts, and the two funds need not perform different acts in every case. It means that ordinary assistance limits must account for the wrongful impairment still awaiting a response. In the second variant, the photographs’ destruction provides no such additional claim for correction costs.

A new administrator inherits the institutional obligation without inheriting personal blame. Replacing employees does not restore Mara’s defence. The relevant continuity lies in the undertaking and outstanding repair, not in the administrator’s state of mind.

## 4. What assistance should preserve

Two requirements follow. In circulating a decision, an institution must take reasonable steps against foreseeable overstatement of its factual authority. Where its prior breach still impairs a person’s ability to contest a consequential misdescription, it may additionally owe proportionate support for correction, including beyond uses it controls. The latter requirement is the specifically reparative extension.

Correction must distinguish the decision’s grounds from a judgment about what everyone can know. An appropriate statement might read: ‘The fund breached its preservation duty. Mara is entitled to compensation for the resulting loss of relevant evidence. The payment proceeding did not determine whether the contractor caused the damage.’ This can coexist with Mara’s testimony, an investigator’s conclusions, or later evidence supporting either side. It cannot require others to withhold all credibility until the fund endorses her account.

The duty has a positive side. The fund should affirm its established breach and Mara’s real entitlement, rather than describing the entire case as uncertain. It should make surviving reasons and appropriate routes for further testimony usable. If another office treats her testimony as officially refuted, authenticating the non-finding can help her speak in her own right without pretending to prove her account.

No universal form follows. Separate descriptions of the breach, practical outcome, and determination’s scope may help, but a short explanation sometimes suffices. A cashier may need only payment authorization; an investigator using the award to assess contractor conduct needs more. Nissenbaum’s contextual-integrity framework already treats context and transformations of data as important to informational norms. The additional ground here is the issuer’s reparative debt for impairing the claimant’s means of contestation. (Nissenbaum 2019, pp. 225–226, 255–256.)

An audience test helps identify misleading historical use: would learning the decision’s actual basis materially weaken the historical inference the recipient is invited to draw? If so, the issuer should preserve that limitation unless a sufficiently strong competing reason prevents it. This test concerns evidence about what happened. It does not require every recipient to reassess the decision’s practical or procedural authority.

## 5. Binding a question without proving its answer

An objection appeals to finality. Institutions settle disputes because parties cannot agree about the facts. If a losing party can always insist that a resolution proves nothing, institutional authority may seem empty. Moreover, some legitimate sanctions require an issue to be treated as fixed in later reasoning, not merely a payment to be recognized as due.

Suppose a fair remedy for withholding evidence precludes the custodian from contesting a factual issue within specified proceedings. A later benefits office might be required to honor that determination. It may properly treat the issue as satisfied without independently believing the historical allegation. The force of this reliance comes from the binding act. It need not supply new historical evidence and is not merely recognition of a monetary entitlement.

We must therefore distinguish three later uses: treating a decision as evidence of history, recognizing an entitlement it creates, and applying an issue it legitimately fixes for procedural purposes. Each is permissible when its own grounds support it. The problem arises when entitlement or procedural force is presented as historical proof.

Normative power can alter what may be challenged, which premises officials must use in a defined proceeding, and which remedies may be demanded. It cannot make a contractor have damaged a house. The institution should preserve both the force of its act and its legitimate scope. Explaining that an issue was fixed as a remedy need not weaken its binding effect. It identifies the authority an official should honor and the historical inference an outsider should not automatically draw. It also avoids extending a sanction against the custodian to parties whom it does not legitimately bind.

This accommodates Nance’s proposal to treat facts as fixed within litigation while retaining his concern about disguising evidence management as fact-finding. The present argument concerns the distinction’s survival through later uses. It does not decide whether any particular preclusion rule is justified. Even legitimate preclusion does not automatically confer unrestricted authority to testify about history.

Institutions can also earn evidential authority through inquiry. Ross argues that criminal convictions should support rational belief in guilt and provide a basis for ending ordinary inquiry; his later work treats convictions as defeasible institutional testimony. Such authority depends on appropriate inquiry, rather than belonging automatically to every institutional resolution. (Ross 2023, §IV; Ross 2026, ‘Conviction as Institutional Testimony.’)

A procedure that must attribute historical guilt to perform its proper function cannot repair inadequate proof by relabelling a conviction. It may have to withhold that verdict while another authorized body supplies relief. Conversely, a legitimate remedial act can create a full entitlement. Challenging Mara’s ownership of the payment solely because contractor responsibility was not established reverses the original mistake: it makes historical proof a condition of an entitlement that rests on a different ground.

Practical finality remains possible. Parties know what is owed, administrators know what to execute, and later officials know which procedural constraints apply. Inquiry can continue through testimony, reassessment, and new documents. Respect for a decision does not require treating the questions it settles as all the questions that matter.

## 6. Evidence, privacy, and limits of repair

Perhaps a remedial award nevertheless provides evidence of the underlying allegation. Authorities may require a credible claim and proof of a material evidential loss. Learning that Mara received compensation might rationally increase confidence that the contractor damaged her home.

The proposal excludes no warranted inference. It requires that inference to reflect the decision actually made. Consider an artificial illustration. Among ordinary merits-based awards, 90 percent concern true underlying claims; among awards for wrongful evidential loss, 40 percent do. Suppose 90 percent of all awards belong to the first category. Someone told only that an award occurred might assign an 85 percent probability to the underlying claim: 0.90 × 0.90 + 0.10 × 0.40 = 0.85. Someone who also knows that it was an evidential-loss award should instead use 40 percent, given only these category frequencies. These figures describe no actual institution.

The second observer may still regard the award as evidence relative to a sufficiently low prior probability. The fault arises when the institution knows the category, foreseeably invites the ordinary inference, and fails to preserve the distinction. Nor does restating an outcome create fresh historical support. If a recipient already knows all relevant grounds and a rule that fully determines the decision, learning that the decision follows that rule adds no confirmation of the original allegation.

Destruction itself can be probative. Selectively burning a file may reveal knowledge that it is damaging. Evidence of such selection can properly be communicated. Mara’s case instead involves indiscriminate deletion for cost savings. Even where destruction supports an allegation, its evidential force remains distinct from its remedial significance. A justified sanction does not establish that the inference from destruction supports a historical finding of equivalent strength.

Disclosure presents a further difficulty. Describing Mara’s award as remedial may stigmatize her as less deserving or disclose matters to people without a legitimate interest. A landlord who needs to know only whether she can pay rent should receive confirmation of her entitlement without an invitation to infer contractor fault. The duty is to prevent misleading use and restore contestation, not to expose the entire file. Where a recipient legitimately investigates the contractor, a careful account should affirm the entitlement while identifying the determination’s limits. If no appropriate account can be given, withholding the misleading proxy may be preferable to disclosing private details.

These duties have different beneficiaries. The preservation undertaking grounds a reparative debt to Mara. The contractor’s protection against unsupported accusations can arise independently from fair treatment and responsible communication. It need not rest on the same debt. If the undertaking also protected contractors, a further directed obligation might arise, but the argument does not require that premise.

This distinction matters for settlement. An informed agreement might replace some individualized assistance with adequate resources or release a claim to assistance. Acceptance of compensation for the lost reimbursement opportunity alone establishes no such release. Mara also cannot release the contractor’s rights or authorize the fund to mislead later recipients. Settling what is owed to her does not grant permission to misrepresent others.

Third-party rights constrain available substitutes. A board that loses examination scripts may owe funded reassessment or compensation. It cannot establish professional competence simply by certifying everyone, or justify transferring the resulting risks to clients who rely on the certificates. A credential’s formal validity does not settle whether issuing it was morally permissible. An entitlement whose costs the responsible institution can fairly bear differs from an assurance that exposes strangers to danger.

The residual duty is not perpetual insurance. An adequate substitute may end the relevant impairment. Costs grossly disproportionate to the remaining disadvantage do not establish a duty to secure complete correction. Where preserved evidence would have left correction equally difficult, the causal basis identified by the two variants is absent. The receiving office remains responsible for its own misconduct. Multiple agents can owe different responses without making one of them responsible for the entire informational environment.

Nor does repair require success at any cost. The fund must support a fair opportunity to challenge the misdescription, not compel a recipient to believe Mara or require her to pursue correction. Assistance should respect her agency. Requiring her to adopt the fund’s preferred narrative would undermine the capacity the assistance is meant to restore.

## 7. What remains after closure

Institutions respond to evidence loss by paying, refusing, certifying, closing files, and issuing reports. These outputs can acquire apparent authority beyond what their grounds support. Even a fair remedy can become an obstacle to explaining what happened.

The response has two parts: preserve the decision’s scope as it circulates and, where the original breach still impairs a protected means of contestation, provide proportionate substitute support. That support can remain due after accurate initial communication and another agency’s error. Its basis and limits lie in the disadvantage the breach created.

Mara can possess a real remedy without a favorable institutional finding about the contractor. She can also know something the fund failed to establish. Her claim against the fund demands neither universal agreement nor universal suspension of judgment. It demands appropriate help in preventing a decision about what she is owed from being mistaken for a determination of what happened.
