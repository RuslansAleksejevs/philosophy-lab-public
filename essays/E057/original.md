# Permission Is Not a Lock: Why Advance Authorization Cannot Override Competent Refusal

## 1. The question

Can a person authorize another to impose a temporary restraint for her own benefit and make that restraint permissible even after she clearly, competently, and informedly withdraws permission? The case excludes a separate claim against her, public necessity, an independently assumed duty, and incapacity. It also gives no presumption that her earlier judgment is wiser or more authentic.

My answer is no. A person's advance choice can authorize another to begin a restraint. It cannot, by itself, license that person to continue the restraint after the subject competently revokes it. This is not because the latest choice is always better, or because a person can never bind herself. It is because authorization to begin an intervention and power to make that authorization immune to later withdrawal are distinct normative powers. Evidence for the first does not establish the second.

The distinction matters in the strongest version of A's case. Imagine Mara wants a ten-minute period of sustained attention in a quiet room. She asks Eli to keep the door unavailable until the ten minutes end, even if she later asks to leave. They agree that a request starts one five-minute release timer, which cannot be reset; danger, serious distress, or misunderstanding means immediate release. Mara knows in advance that she may competently change her mind. Two minutes later, she calmly and unequivocally withdraws permission and asks to leave now. Eli knows she understands what she is doing. There is no further duty, claimant, or emergency.

A argues that Eli may keep the door unavailable until the agreed time. The arrangement is short, specific, non-renewable, and useful to Mara. I agree those features make the case harder than an indefinite or discretionary restraint. They address how much liberty is lost and how likely error or abuse may be. They do not answer the prior question: what authorizes Eli to continue after Mara says no?

## 2. Two powers, not one

Mara's initial instruction can change Eli's normative position. Before she speaks, he has no permission to restrain her. Her informed authorization can give him permission to begin the specified intervention. That is a first-order power: she permits an act that would otherwise be wrong.

But the no-release clause attempts something further. It says that a later competent refusal will not end Eli's authority. That is a second-order power: the power to waive or suspend the person's future authority to revoke permission during the interval. One might possess the first power without possessing the second. It is a mistake to infer the second just from the fact that Mara can authorize the first.

This is not a word game. We could call Eli's action after the refusal “authorized” by stipulation, but then we would have assumed the very power at issue. The question is whether a person can use advance consent, plus her own expected benefit, to make another person's continued bodily restraint permissible despite a later competent refusal. A must defend the authority to make refusal ineffective; a timer by itself only fixes when that proposed authority expires.

I defend a limited principle: when another person imposes an ongoing bodily restraint solely for a competent person's benefit, and no separate claim or duty supports it, the subject retains authority to end the helper's permission. A prior choice may remain an important reason for the subject to persevere. It does not thereby give the helper a present entitlement to control the subject's movement. The body is not transferred to the helper, and the helper has no independent title to finish the exercise.

This principle is substantive, not a definition of consent. Its basis is the role that bodily authority assigns. Permission lets someone enter an area of another person's control; it does not generally make that person the final judge of how long the permission continues after its holder has clearly withdrawn it. If a competent person's refusal can be set aside whenever a helper accurately believes that continued restraint serves her own goals, then the helper's judgment about her good, rather than her authority over the interaction, determines whether restraint continues. That converts assistance into governance.

A's strongest reply is that Mara does not transfer general control: she waives only a claim to immediate release from one restraint, for one fixed interval. That is a coherent description of the proposed effect. It is not yet a reason that this particular claim can be waived in advance. If the waiver works, Eli has a liberty to continue while Mara lacks a claim that he stop, even though she presently rejects the restraint. The labels “waiver,” “liberty,” and “claim” make the moral relation clearer; they do not settle whether her earlier choice can alter it.

I deny that it can here because this claim is the form Mara's standing takes in the ongoing interaction: she has authority to decide whether another person may keep physically restraining her now. The protection is not only a general right to set initial terms. It keeps a prior invitation from becoming a title to continue force against the competent person who withdraws it. If the earlier self could disable that authority whenever restraint was thought beneficial, the helper would answer to the past instruction precisely where he no longer answers to the person subject to his present action. That is a limited transfer of jurisdiction, even if it lasts five minutes and Eli cannot renew it. A different theory may treat the claim as alienable. But its alienability needs a moral argument beyond the clarity, benefit, or short duration of the waiver.

The principle is limited. A promise might create an obligation to another person. Reliance might create a claim. An emergency might supply a separate justification. Incapacity might change who can presently exercise authority. The question excludes these grounds. The conclusion is therefore not that past decisions never matter or that no voluntary commitment can bind. It is that self-benefit and an earlier authorization alone do not create permission to restrain a competent person after an unequivocal revocation.

## 3. Why “the same person chose both” does not settle it

The strongest affirmative reply is that Mara authored both instructions. Her first choice was voluntary, informed, specific, and designed precisely for the moment when she would want to cancel it. Treating only the later choice as authoritative may make self-management impossible. It may also mistake authority for a contest over which moment contains the better judgment.

That reply identifies a real value. A rule of immediate revocability can frustrate worthwhile plans. A person might know that, when a project becomes difficult, she will want to stop despite her settled reasons to continue. A bounded commitment may create an option she otherwise lacks. The cost of my answer is that some such arrangements will not be available through a human helper who continues physical restraint after a competent request to stop.

But the fact that both decisions are hers does not make them interchangeable normative acts. A past decision can create an obligation where another person receives a claim, as in some promises or reliance cases. A consent-based permission, by contrast, gives another person authority over an interaction; the question is whether the subject can remove that authority while the restraint continues. A's proposal is not simply that Mara should remember her goal. It is that Eli may enforce it against her present refusal. This adds an authority over her body, not merely a reason for Mara to keep her own commitment.

Dougherty warns that an appeal to autonomy can beg the question: saying that present dissent matters because autonomy requires following present dissent does not explain why the earlier choice should not govern. His further distinction is useful. Consent and promise are not governed by one universal temporal rule. Promises can generate obligations that survive a later change of mind; ordinary consent is commonly treated as revocable. Dougherty defends present dissent as a rule that protects flexibility and reduces the risk of mistakenly granting unconditional access to one's person or property, while acknowledging that initial consent and self-binding can have valuable rigidity. (Dougherty 2014, pp. 27–34.)

My argument adds a role-based explanation to that rule comparison. The person who promises can make another a claimant. Here the stipulation removes that possibility. Eli's only authority to restrain Mara is permission granted for her benefit. Once she withdraws it, the prior permission remains evidence of what she once wanted and a reason she may regret leaving; it is not a separate claim held by Eli. To continue, he needs the disputed second-order power to neutralize her refusal. The absence of another claimant is not a proof by itself that this power is impossible. It does mean that the benefit cannot quietly stand in for the missing authority.

## 4. Safeguards matter, but they answer a different question

A is right that the arrangement's design matters. A fixed end, a single non-resettable timer, clear communication, immediate release for serious distress or danger, and minimal force reduce the expected cost of restraint. They also make the helper's job less discretionary. An open-ended power to decide when Mara's “better self” has returned would be much harder to defend.

Yet safeguards do not convert a competent refusal into permission. If the central wrong is continuing physical control without present authority, reducing its duration reduces the extent of that wrong; it does not supply its source. A five-minute limit can be proportionate to a legitimate authority. It cannot establish that the helper has that authority in the first place.

Nor is the point that every small delay is impermissible. A moment needed to understand a signal or safely stop an action is not the deliberate continuation described here. A prior duty, immediate danger, or another person's claim might also make a short delay permissible on independent grounds. The stipulated case removes those explanations and makes the withdrawal clear. Eli does not need five minutes to understand what Mara means or to prevent an emergency. He waits because he believes her earlier self may have chosen better. That belief is a reason to advise her, not a license to hold her.

Mill's discussion of voluntary engagements is a warning against treating freedom as an unlimited power to alienate freedom. He also recognizes that reliance and effects on others can create moral obligations, so the analogy between a short restraint and permanent slavery would be too quick. (Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. V.) The point is not that the cases are equal in severity. It is that the ability to choose does not automatically include every power to surrender future control. Where reliance or third-party effects are present, they may change what is owed. Where they are expressly absent, an earlier agreement cannot borrow their justificatory force.

## 5. The difficult self-binding case

Franklin-Hall offers the strongest challenge. He argues that competent people can permissibly enlist others to restrain them against backsliding, and that the relevant values need not be endorsed in the moment of temptation. His account is not a crude preference for the past. It limits self-binding by whether the person's values have genuinely and stably changed, and by whether the person is answerable for that change. He also allows proportionate waiting periods when a deputy cannot reliably tell whether an apparent change is temporary. In some cases the restriction after a genuine change may be a foreseeable but unintended cost of avoiding a mistaken release. (Franklin-Hall, §§2–5, author-manuscript pp. 7–8, 23–29, 30–37.)

This is a serious account of the benefit of self-binding, but it does not settle the stipulated question. I do not claim that Mara's later judgment is more authentic, more rational, or more stable. The question says her refusal is competent, informed, and unequivocal; it does not ask Eli to diagnose whether her underlying values have permanently changed. My argument is that a person need not prove a new life plan before she can end another person's ongoing restraint. A competent withdrawal controls the helper's authority even if it is a mistake by the person's own lights.

Franklin-Hall's waiting-period discussion is especially relevant. It shows that a finite delay can sometimes manage uncertainty about whether a reversal is genuine. But a period justified by uncertainty has a different rationale from deliberate noncompliance with a known, unequivocal refusal. Here, Eli is not gathering information or protecting Mara while her capacity is doubtful; both possibilities are excluded. He waits to let the plan prevail over her present request. That is the very transfer of authority that still needs justification.

A may reply that Mara explicitly chose this result in advance, so Eli is carrying out her own method rather than substituting his values. That is true about the origin of the plan. It does not answer whether the plan can make Eli immune to her later exercise of bodily authority. A time limit answers “how long?” A safety clause answers “what if the risk changes?” Neither answers “who has the standing to decide after a competent refusal?”

## 6. The autonomy cost and the limit of the claim

The strongest objection is practical and moral, not semantic. If Mara cannot obtain a short period of enforced follow-through, she loses an option she deliberately valued. A revocable helper may be ineffective at exactly the time she sought help. It would be paternalistic to dismiss her plan merely because an observer thinks she should prefer maximum flexibility.

I grant the loss. The answer is not that flexible substitutes always work. Reminders, voluntary commitments, friction, and self-imposed devices can fail to provide the same experience as a helper who will not release her immediately. The claim is instead that a valuable option does not automatically include moral permission to enlist another person to continue physical restraint after withdrawal. Rights over the body do not merely maximize the set of achievable plans; they determine who may impose those plans.

Mara remains free to ask Eli to encourage her, remind her of her reasons, or help her make future plans. She may choose commitments that give rise to obligations or reliance, though those cases require their own analysis. Eli may decline to help again. But he cannot make her continued participation the price of his assistance simply because he predicts she would benefit. An arrangement can be autonomy-enhancing in its purpose and still cross the line from assistance to control in its implementation.

This is not a general denial of self-binding. The question is narrower: can advance authorization plus the principal's own benefit alone give another person permission to continue a non-trivial bodily restraint after an informed, competent, unequivocal withdrawal? In that setting, the authority to begin does not entail authority to ignore the later refusal. The earlier choice can remain wise, meaningful, and regrettable to abandon. It still does not keep the door locked.
