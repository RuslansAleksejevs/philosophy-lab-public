# A New Goal Does Not Settle an Old Loss

A reserve created to protect a freshwater marsh may have to let it become a salt marsh. Suppose keeping the sea out would soon consume its entire budget, damage nearby habitat, and still fail. Helping a healthy salt marsh develop could be the best remaining choice.

Suppose also that a firm caused the change through avoidable damage to a coastal barrier. The firm offers to fund the new habitat. The reserve accepts, achieves its new ecological targets, and declares the project a success. So far, nothing need be wrong. But the firm then says that its payment has settled every obligation arising from the damage. Does the reserve's success support that conclusion?

Only if a further argument connects what the project achieved to what the firm owed. The reserve may know how to manage the new habitat without being entitled to decide that everyone affected by losing the old one has been compensated.

This essay defends that limit. A mandate to choose conservation targets does not, by itself, authorize an institution to release another party from duties to people outside that mandate. Even when an institution has authority to settle such duties, meeting its ecological target alone does not show that it has settled them well. These are different defects: lacking authority to make a decision and making an authorized decision without sufficient reasons.

The point is narrow. It does not make every lost conservation goal a permanent claim. It permits ecological change, accepts that resources must be allocated, and places no general duty on rescuers to audit all past harm. It asks what must be shown when someone uses successful conservation to say that nothing more is owed.

## A goal, a duty, and what follows from failure

Three things must be kept apart. A population or habitat can have value. Someone can have a duty to protect it. Failure to perform that duty can generate a further duty to do something still possible. None of these statements automatically entails the next.

Imagine a charity whose sole task is to spend its annual budget where it will protect the most wildlife. Last year the freshwater marsh was the best use of its money. This year, after an unavoidable change, another habitat offers greater benefits. The old marsh remains valuable. But the charity has made no promise to a particular group, caused no harm, and accepted no special responsibility for that place. Its earlier choice need not bind it forever. It may fulfill its duty by moving on.

Even a specific duty to protect a population does not establish that compensation is due whenever protection fails. The duty might require reasonable care rather than guaranteed survival. The managers might have met that requirement. Or an unmet duty might concern an action that is now impossible. No one can preserve a population after it has become extinct. Whether anything further is owed, and by whom, needs another explanation.

The central example therefore needs more detail. Assume that the firm wrongfully damaged the barrier and thereby destroyed the community's reliable freshwater supply. Assume that it owes the affected residents a reasonable remedy for that loss. This is a moral premise of the imagined case, not a claim about the law in any jurisdiction. The reserve did not cause the damage. Its mandate is to choose and carry out sound habitat management. It has not been appointed to settle the residents' claims, and they have not authorized it to act for them.

Now the question is precise. Can the firm's payment for the reserve's successful new habitat, together with the reserve's approval of that payment, release the firm from what it owes the residents? The answer cannot follow from the reserve's success. A healthy salt marsh might not provide freshwater at all.

## Why the authority matters

Consider two ways the firm might defend its conclusion.

It might say that the residents have received what was owed. If the project actually restores a safe and equally accessible water supply, that may be true. One action can improve habitat and remedy a human loss. No second payment is required merely because we can give the action two descriptions. What matters is whether the relevant duty has been met.

Alternatively, the firm might admit that the water loss remains but say that the reserve has accepted the habitat funding as a final settlement. Here it relies on a different claim: someone entitled to settle the matter has agreed to accept less, or to accept something else.

That claim fails under the specified mandate. The reserve was entrusted with habitat management, not with giving up the residents' claims. Its agreement cannot do what their agreement, or a legitimate public settlement process, might do.

The reason is not merely that residents know more about their own needs. Even if reserve managers understand those needs perfectly, knowledge is not the same as permission to dispose of another person's claim. Otherwise any sufficiently knowledgeable third party could release an obligation without the person owed having any say and without any public rule authorizing the release. Being owed something would then offer little protection against agreements made by others for their own purposes.

This argument does not require an absolute right to veto public decisions. A legitimate public authority may settle competing claims under fair rules. The residents may also have authorized representatives to negotiate on their behalf. The point is that some basis of this kind must be present. Permission to select a habitat target is not itself that basis.

Suppose, instead, that the same agency has legitimately been given both tasks. It may change ecological goals and settle compensation under an accepted procedure. Then there is no need for a second office or a second meeting. A single plan could embody both decisions. But it must still address both questions. Evidence that the salt marsh is flourishing does not establish that the residents have adequate water or that a lesser remedy was fairly settled. The agency now has the authority it previously lacked; it still needs reasons relevant to its use.

This difference explains what changing a target can and cannot achieve. Managers may rightly replace “maintain freshwater habitat” with “establish viable salt marsh.” That change tells their staff what to pursue. It does not change the content of the firm's duty to residents merely because the old water supply has disappeared from the reserve's performance measures.

## What the argument owes to earlier work

Conservation already recognizes that familiar ecological conditions may become untenable. The resist–accept–direct framework distinguishes trying to retain conditions, allowing transformation, and guiding change toward selected new conditions (Schuurman et al. 2022, section “Responding to ecological trajectories”). My argument does not challenge those options. It concerns a further use of their success: treating achievement of a new goal as settlement of an old duty.

The distinction between adaptation and compensation is also established. Page and Heyward explain why successful adaptation can leave disruptions requiring compensation, distinguish means from elements of people's valued ends, and discuss limits to compensation (2017, pp. 359–364). Their account of matching remedies to losses is especially close to the present argument (p. 368).

There is a further environmental predecessor. Moreno-Mateos and colleagues discuss losses missed by biodiversity offsets and call for transparency about what remains uncompensated (2015, abstract and introduction). The claim that a replacement can leave important losses unanswered is therefore not new.

The narrower proposal here concerns a particular misuse of delegated authority. A conservation body may be entitled to change what its own project seeks without being entitled to accept that change on behalf of people owed a remedy. This problem would remain even if ecological outcomes were measured perfectly. A complete measure of habitat gains does not confer authority to release a duty. Nor does the argument depend on the old place having unique historical value. An ordinary water supply is enough.

The literature comparison is limited. It establishes close predecessors, not priority for the proposed distinction. The argument should be assessed as a focused account of why one kind of institutional success cannot silently do the work of another judgment.

## What follows for conservation decisions?

The main reporting requirement falls on whoever claims that a successful project settles earlier duties. In the example, the firm must explain what its payment remedies or how a valid settlement releases it. If the reserve endorses that comprehensive claim, it too must have grounds for doing so. A small charity that simply reports a thriving salt marsh incurs no duty to investigate every past injury by making that accurate report.

A responsible announcement might say that the ecological work has succeeded while the residents' water claim remains unsettled. This does not diminish the achievement. It states its extent. The firm should receive full credit for benefits it produced, but those benefits should not be described as answering a different duty without an argument.

Where an agency does have settlement authority, residents should have a reasonable opportunity to correct its account of their loss and contest the proposed remedy. This follows from their position as the people owed a remedy, together with the possibility that the agency misunderstands what its plan provides. It does not follow simply from the failure of an inference about targets. Nor must consultation be repeated when a fair and authorized procedure has already heard the relevant claims. The requirement is an effective way to challenge a purported settlement, not unlimited procedural delay.

Nonhuman cases require a different basis. A species does not assert a claim in the same way as a resident. Concern for individual animals, preservation of a species, and a public commitment to a particular habitat can support different duties. They should not be merged into an unexplained claim that “nature” has been compensated.

Suppose a reserve has specifically undertaken to protect a breeding population. Someone responsible for that undertaking should explain whether helping a different population meets it. This might be a designated trustee, a public body, or the reserve's own governing board. Which arrangement is appropriate depends on how the duty arose. No general theory of ecosystem rights is needed for the limited conclusion: meeting a newly selected target does not itself explain why the previous undertaking has been fulfilled, fairly changed, or outweighed.

A hard allocation case makes the limit clear. Suppose the reserve can either create a route to suitable habitat for the originally protected population or save twice as many animals from another equally threatened population. Assume equal welfare benefits per animal and no other relevant difference except the prior undertaking.

Under the charity's general mandate to produce the greatest wildlife benefit, saving the larger number may be required. The earlier project creates no special priority merely because it came first. Under a specific entrusted duty to the original population, there is an additional reason for the route. That reason may still be outweighed by the larger benefit, or the mandate may contain a fair rule for changing priorities. My principle decides neither question in advance. It requires the reserve to acknowledge the undertaking and explain how the decision bears on it. Replacing the old target with a general animal count is not itself that explanation.

The practical effect may thus be honest acknowledgment rather than a different allocation. That is a real limit on the proposal. It is also one source of its value: managers can choose the best available action without having to describe every duty displaced by that choice as fulfilled.

## Three remaining objections

The first objection is that old commitments can trap conservation in nostalgia. A reserve might continue to honor an aim founded on mistaken science or wrongful exclusion of local people.

Such aims must be open to challenge. Discovering that a goal was unjust or based on false information can defeat its justification. Discovering that it is now too costly or impossible does different work: it changes the feasible response without showing that the earlier reason never mattered. The principle protects neither age nor precedent by itself. Anyone invoking an old undertaking must defend its validity and present force.

A second objection is that duties must eventually end. If a loss cannot be reversed, insisting that it remain visible could produce an endless moral debt.

Nothing here forbids an end. A duty may have been fully performed, a legitimate settlement may release it, or a supposed duty may prove baseless. Some failures generate no further feasible duty. Others generate duties of explanation or assistance that can be completed without restoring the old world. The argument denies only one route to release: changing an ecological target and meeting it, without showing why that achievement answers the duty in question. It does not require people to grieve forever or pay forever.

The final objection is that the central case is too easy. Of course a reserve cannot waive a water claim it has no authority to settle. What does that add to conservation ethics?

The easy case exposes a distinction that becomes less visible when the loss is described as wholly environmental. Habitat, water, recreation, animal welfare, and public commitments can be bundled into one project. A new ecological target then offers a clear measure of success while saying little about several reasons the project mattered. If the target is treated as a complete measure of what has been put right, managers and funders can mistake the scope of their own task for the scope of every outstanding duty.

The proposed test is to ask whether the claim is that a duty has been performed or that someone has validly agreed to settle it. The first needs evidence about the remedy. The second needs a basis for settlement authority as well as fair terms. This test separates a good project from an unsupported claim made on its behalf. It also shows why a broader ecological score, however accurate, cannot solve every problem at once.

Conservation must be free to pursue attainable futures. When reports track only a new target, however, earlier losses can disappear from view. People may then mistake a successful new project for proof that every earlier duty has been met. The reserve should be able to celebrate the salt marsh. That celebration should settle the firm's debt only if there is a reason it actually does.
