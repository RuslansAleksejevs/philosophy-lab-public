# A Lower Complaint Count Is Not Yet Better Deliberation

## Abstract

A public body can change what it calls an objection, how people may report one, and how reports are counted. If fewer complaints appear afterward, the decline may reflect fewer concerns, changed classification, barriers to reporting, disengagement, or earlier responses that resolved concerns. Measurement-validity research already warns that an indicator must remain interpretable in relation to the concept it is meant to measure; deliberative-democracy research likewise connects judgments of deliberative quality to the indicators and communication paths used to assess it. This essay claims no new general principle of measurement or democratic inclusion. It offers a narrower distinction: evidence that successive counts remain comparable is different from evidence that deliberation improved. A count may be a useful indicator only when its target and reporting path are examined, including who does not report. A claim about deliberative quality also needs process evidence that relevant reasons received fair consideration. These requirements do not preserve old wording, make every complaint relevant, or give objectors a veto.

## 1. The quiet record

Imagine an agency that distributes a monthly benefit. It once accepted complaints under a broad category called “unfair treatment.” After a policy change, every eligible recipient receives the same cash amount. The agency replaces its form with one that records only payment errors. It then reports fewer complaints and says that deliberation has improved: participants apparently object less and accept the new rule.

That conclusion does not follow from the count alone. The policy might have resolved a genuine source of disagreement. But recipients may also have lost a way to report unequal access or unusually high costs. The form now counts a narrower set of events. The recorded decline is compatible with both a changed policy and a changed instrument.

Now suppose the form still has an open-text box, but the agency has repeatedly dismissed earlier submissions. People know the channel exists but expect no response and stop using it. Here the category has not changed and the formal route remains open. The count still cannot show that the underlying concerns or deliberative quality improved. The missing reports may reflect diminished trust or perceived standing.

There is also a different possibility. Perhaps the agency began responding to concerns before recipients needed to file formal complaints. The decline could accompany improvement, but the count does not establish why it fell. The evidence for improvement would lie in the response history and participants’ accounts, not in the reduced tally by itself.

These possibilities separate three claims: the policy produced better results; the deliberative process gave reasons fair consideration; and the complaint count is evidence for either claim. A lower count may be relevant to an evaluation, but it does not collapse the three. The central thesis is modest: when an evaluator uses changing complaint counts to support a claim about deliberative improvement, the evaluator needs evidence both that the counts track a sufficiently stable target and that the process considered relevant reasons. Even then, the count is one indicator among others, not a verdict.

## 2. Concepts, forms, and reports are different things

The problem is sometimes described as a change in concept, but that description can obscure several mechanisms.

A **conceptual change** alters the criteria or practical meaning associated with a concept such as fairness. Sally Haslanger’s purpose-sensitive account of conceptual revision asks what work a concept should do in context; it does not require retaining every familiar meaning (Haslanger 2000). A reformed concept can therefore be better suited to its purpose. Conceptual disagreements may also carry disputes about the things a term classifies: Plunkett and Sundell argue that metalinguistic negotiation can involve object-level issues (Plunkett and Sundell 2021 [first published online 2019]). That is one reason to ask what happens to the underlying reason when a word or definition changes.

An **administrative classification change** alters how a form, database, or official treats statements as cases of fairness, access, payment error, or some other category. That change may accompany conceptual revision, but need not. An agency could keep the same public meaning of “fairness” while narrowing its form to payment errors. Conversely, it could adopt a new account of fairness while preserving a broad way to record recipients’ reasons.

A **reporting change** occurs when people become more or less able or willing to use the route. Knowledge of the form, translation, time costs, trust, fear of retaliation, expectations of response, and beliefs about whether one’s concern counts can all affect whether a concern is filed. These are not identical to classification. A form may be formally available yet functionally unused.

Finally, **deliberative treatment** concerns what happens after a reason reaches the process. It may be recorded but ignored, understood but judged weak, or fairly considered and rejected. Mary Scudder’s account of uptake emphasizes fair consideration, not agreement or getting one’s way (Scudder 2020). A recorded objection is therefore not proof of uptake, just as a rejected objection is not necessarily a deliberative failure.

A complaint count is produced by a path: a person has a concern, encounters or learns about a route, expresses the concern, reaches an institution, has the report recognized and classified, and has it included in a tally. The stages can change independently. The term “expressive closure” is unnecessary for this analysis: what matters is to identify whether the change occurs in the concept, the administrative map, access and reporting, or subsequent consideration.

## 3. What measurement validity already tells us

The general measurement point is established. Adcock and Collier distinguish disputes about a background concept from the question whether an indicator validly represents a specified concept. Scores are meaningful only insofar as the indicator captures what the concept is meant to contain (Adcock and Collier 2026). Köhler’s account of conceptual engineering also treats classificatory devices and their practical effects as important, not merely word meanings (Köhler 2025).

Work on deliberative measurement applies related requirements in a setting where the target is itself a democratic process. Fleuß, Helbig, and Schaal identify the choice of democratic theory, concept of deliberation, site, and aggregation rule as parameters of measurement (Fleuß, Helbig, and Schaal 2018). Fleuß’s later account likewise connects macro-level assessment to an explicit conception of deliberativeness, appropriate indicators, and evidence of communication between sites (Fleuß 2022). These frameworks already rule out the assumption that a number measures a political value just because an institution labels it an indicator.

Broader accounts of communicative exclusion also explain why formal access may fail to make a concern intelligible or valued. Young criticizes deliberative standards that can silence or devalue some speakers, while Fricker analyzes disadvantage arising from unequal shared interpretive resources (Young 1996; Fricker 2017). These are background reasons to inspect how a route functions; the present argument does not infer that an older word must be retained.

The present argument does not add a general standard to this literature. Its narrower purpose is to distinguish two inferences that can otherwise be conflated in the complaint case.

First, **measurement comparability** asks whether the old and new counts refer to a sufficiently similar target and whether the channel captures that target in a sufficiently similar way. This concerns the relation between reports and what is counted.

Second, **deliberative assessment** asks whether relevant reasons entered the process and received fair consideration. This concerns what happens to reasons, not simply how many reports reach a form.

Evidence for the first question is necessary if counts are to be compared as counts of the same kind. It is not sufficient for the second. Even a stable reporting route can yield fewer complaints because people disengage, because the affected population changed, because problems were resolved before formal complaint, or because the process has become less trusted. Conversely, a changed classification may improve the measure while making the deliberation no worse. The distinction is useful because it keeps indicator validity from standing in for democratic quality.

The essay’s possible contribution is therefore a focused diagnostic application of measurement validity and uptake to institutional complaint records: evaluators should state which inference they want the count to support and test the relevant links. Whether this is more than a familiar application remains open. No general originality claim follows.

## 4. Three questions for interpreting a changing count

An evaluator should begin by stating the claim and its target. Is the institution claiming fewer filed complaints, fewer underlying concerns among a defined group, more successful prior resolution, or better deliberation? Those claims require different evidence. An absolute count can fall because fewer people were eligible, fewer were exposed to the policy, or the period observed was shorter. Where the inference concerns affected people, counts need a clear denominator and case description.

The next question is whether the old and new measures remain comparable. A count’s target should be specified before interpreting its movement. If the agency now counts only payment errors, it cannot simply compare that number with an earlier tally that included unequal access. A bridge might recode a sample of old and new reports under a shared analytic scheme; compare what is placed in “other” or left uncoded; and examine whether participants can still submit reasons without selecting the institution’s preferred label. These checks can reveal changes in classification. They cannot identify every person who never reports.

For that reason, evaluators also need evidence about **non-use**. They can ask affected people whether they knew of the route, understood it, considered it safe, and expected a response. Accessible interviews, confidential channels outside the disputed process, or surveys of a defined affected group may reveal barriers that the administrative record cannot show. The result remains partial: nonresponse and distrust can affect these methods too. The point is not to guarantee perfect access but to avoid treating the institution’s own record as a complete picture of who could speak.

Even if the count is comparable and reporting appears stable, a conclusion about deliberative improvement needs evidence about treatment. Did relevant reasons reach a forum with authority to consider them? Were they answered with reasons? Could participants learn how they affected the decision, even if they did not determine it? Did the process make corrections or explain why it rejected the objection? Records of handling, participant assessment, and follow-up can inform these questions. They should be interpreted together, since formal response does not by itself establish fair consideration.

A count may help locate a question, reveal a pattern, or prompt further investigation. It becomes stronger evidence only when the evaluator shows what it measures and relates it to evidence about the process. There is no universal checklist: the needed checks depend on the population, the institution’s claim, the kinds of reports that may be lost, and what participants can safely disclose. When the evidence is absent, the honest conclusion is that the count does not settle the matter.

## 5. Which objections count as relevant?

A process cannot be expected to consider every imaginable complaint. Yet the institution cannot define relevance solely by the new concept and then cite the absence of excluded reports as proof that the reform succeeded. The boundary needs a basis that is public and open to challenge.

Four sources can inform that boundary. First, identify the reasons the institution gave for adopting the reform before evaluating its effects. An objection that bears on those reasons is relevant to the claim that the reform achieved its purpose. Second, use reasonably available evidence about foreseeable effects, including costs that could count against the stated purpose. Third, treat prior complaint patterns as prompts for inquiry, not as an exhaustive list: an old record may omit concerns precisely because an earlier category failed to recognize them. Fourth, invite testimony from affected people in accessible forms that do not require them to use the disputed label. Their testimony is evidence about what matters and what may be missing, not an automatic veto or an infallible definition of the issue.

These sources can conflict. An institution may have to set limits for time, jurisdiction, or available evidence. It should state those limits and explain how they bear on the public reasons at stake. A concern need not be accepted as true to deserve consideration; its relevance is a reason to examine it, not a presumption that it wins. New evidence may warrant reopening a boundary, but no single objection creates an endless duty to restart a decision.

This approach also separates the fact that a complaint is outside an agency’s remit from the inference that participants accept the exclusion. The institution may have good grounds for a bounded decision. But if it excludes an issue, the resulting absence of complaints about that issue cannot also be treated as evidence that deliberation about it improved.

## 6. Four versions of the benefit case

In the first version, the agency replaces the broad fairness category with a payment-error form. Historical reports included unequal access, while the new form excludes it. The two counts do not share a target. The decline may reflect reclassification, so the count cannot by itself support an inference about fewer access concerns or better deliberation.

In the second version, the form retains an open-text field and staff continue to code the same kinds of concerns. Yet participants report that officials ignored earlier submissions, and many expect no response. This is not a coding discontinuity. It is a change in reporting behavior and possibly in trust. A formally comparable route does not repair the inference. The institution would need evidence that the lower count does not arise from non-use and evidence about how reasons were considered.

In the third version, the agency reviews concerns through the same route but begins resolving access problems before recipients file a formal complaint. The count falls. If records show timely responses, corrections, and explanations, and affected recipients confirm that the earlier problem no longer requires formal escalation, those facts may support an improvement in process. The count alone cannot distinguish successful early resolution from disengagement; the additional process evidence does the work.

In the fourth version, the agency changes the form to eliminate duplicate or misdirected reports while preserving an accessible route for concerns bearing on its stated reasons. A sample of free-text submissions and a common coding review show that the new measure captures the same relevant class more consistently. The lower count may then be evidence of fewer reports within a comparable target. It still does not establish fair consideration or better deliberation without evidence about what happened to the reasons received.

These variations show why the thesis is conditional in two different ways. A changed concept or administrative category may undermine comparability; but restoring comparability does not turn the count into a measure of democratic quality. Policy outcomes, complaint frequency, reporting behavior, and deliberative treatment need separate support.

## 7. Objections and replies

### 7.1 “This asks reformers to preserve obsolete concepts”

It does not. A new concept can improve classification, expose a false assumption, or make policy easier to justify. Nothing here requires an agency to retain an old label. The demand is about the evidence it uses to support a separate claim about deliberation. A reform can proceed while its effects on reporting and consideration remain uncertain, provided that uncertainty is not presented as proof of improved deliberative quality.

### 7.2 “The test demands impossible evidence”

No empirical design reveals every unexpressed concern or guarantees trust. A bounded review may use a common coding sample, accessible participant feedback, records of decisions, and a clear account of who was included. Each method has blind spots. The strength of the conclusion should match the evidence: a limited audit might support a limited claim, while a broad claim about affected participants requires broader evidence. If only administrative counts exist, evaluators should say what the counts establish and leave the rest unresolved.

### 7.3 “Complaint numbers are still useful indicators”

Agreed. The argument does not discard them. A rising count can identify emerging problems; a falling count can be consistent with improvement. Measurement validity does not demand that indicators be perfect. It demands that their relation to the stated target be examined. In this case, the count is best treated as one input into an assessment, especially when the category, route, or population changes.

### 7.4 “Scudder’s account of uptake already makes this point”

Scudder supplies the key democratic standard: inclusion alone is inadequate, uptake concerns fair consideration, and evidence such as participant perceptions or complaints is informative but inconclusive (Scudder 2020). The measurement literature supplies the general warning about matching indicators to concepts and testing them in context. Those accounts establish much of the normative and methodological ground. The present distinction between count comparability and deliberative assessment may be only an application of that work. Its limited aim is to make explicit why evidence that an indicator remains stable does not show that reasons received fair consideration, and why lower counts may arise from non-use even when labels stay the same. If this clarification is not a sufficiently distinct contribution, the essay should be read as an illustration rather than a novel result.

### 7.5 “A fair process can reject a relevant objection”

Yes. Fair consideration is not agreement, influence, or control over the outcome. A process may reject an objection as unsupported, outweighed, or outside a publicly explained remit. The question is whether the reason was available for consideration and received an adequate response, not whether it prevailed. This is why evidence of policy success cannot substitute for evidence about deliberative treatment, and why the argument gives no complainant a veto.

## 8. Limits and conclusion

This essay supplies no empirical estimate of how often institutional reform changes complaint counts or reporting behavior. The benefit agency is hypothetical and illustrates possibilities, not a documented case. The proposed checks do not guarantee access, trust, or uptake; they are ways of making assumptions visible and testing a claim in context. The argument also does not establish that its focused synthesis is original.

When an institution changes a concept, a form, a coding rule, or its response practice, those changes can affect different parts of the complaint path. A lower count may reflect fewer concerns, altered classification, reduced participation, successful early resolution, or several changes together. Measurement comparability can make counts more interpretable, but it cannot by itself show that deliberation improved. That further claim needs evidence that relevant reasons entered the process and received fair consideration.

The conclusion is not that reform must preserve old language or that every complaint should shape policy. It is that institutions should not use a quieter record as straightforward evidence of better deliberation without showing what the record counts, who could contribute to it, and how reasons were treated. The general measurement principle is familiar; this essay offers a bounded deliberative application whose independent originality remains unsettled.
