# What a Checked Proof Has Not Yet Told Us

A proof assistant reports success. Its kernel is sound, its assumptions are acceptable, and its proof object really establishes the formula displayed. A mathematician nevertheless asks whether the announced theorem has been proved. This need not express distrust of computers. It may express uncertainty about which mathematical claim the formula represents. Another successful execution of the same checker can answer a question about reproducibility while leaving this question untouched.

The familiar lesson is that formal verification requires attention to formalization. A further distinction matters: establishing that a formula exactly captures an intended concept is different from establishing that it supports a particular mathematical use. I argue that the appropriate response to a translation challenge depends on which of these claims is being defended. A mismatch can invalidate a claimed translation while leaving a proposed inference secure. Conversely, impeccable proof checking can leave that inference unsupported. The aim should be to establish the implication a use requires, not invariably to establish identity of meaning or to accumulate successful checks.

This is a conditional epistemic norm, not a demand that all mathematics acquire a new ritual. It neither makes examples into proofs nor requires every user of a theorem to understand its proof. Its point is to explain when a particular request for more evidence is rational, what kind of evidence would answer it, and why impressive evidence of another kind may not.

## Three claims behind one announcement

Consider the announcement, “This development proves that a sequence converges uniformly.” It can compress at least three claims. First, a formal formula follows from the development's assumptions by accepted rules. Second, the formula and its definitions express uniform convergence in the intended sense. Third, the result therefore licenses the ordinary uses of uniform convergence, such as selecting a single approximation threshold that works throughout the specified domain. The first claim concerns derivation. The second concerns exact correspondence. The third concerns a particular consequence. These are distinct achievements: exact correspondence can support the consequence, but a justified implication from a stronger or differently formulated result may suffice.

The distinctions do not make the three claims unrelated. Reading a definition may establish the second claim straightforwardly; the first and second can then support the third. Nor is the first claim merely a fact about meaningless marks. Mathematicians can understand the formal formula perfectly well. The issue is the additional identification expressed when a result is announced under a familiar mathematical description.

Adams explicitly distinguishes an incorrect formal statement, including errors in its supporting definitions, from unsound inference. His proposed proof audit combines replay with review of the final statement and its dependencies. The need to check correspondence is therefore no new discovery here (Adams 2016, §3.1.2 and §4.3). Avigad likewise treats the recognition of logical and mathematical structure as part of understanding assertions, rather than treating a successful derivation as a complete account of understanding (Avigad 2010, §3.1).

My concern begins after these distinctions have been accepted. How much correspondence must be defended for a given use, and when does additional checking address a challenge to that use? These questions are connected. Unless the proposed inference is identified, one cannot tell whether an apparent translation error matters or what evidence would settle the issue.

## A theorem that passes under the wrong name

Let a sequence of real-valued functions f_n on a domain D have proposed limit f. Pointwise convergence requires that, for every x in D and every positive error tolerance ε, there be a threshold N such that every n at least N satisfies |f_n(x) − f(x)| < ε. The threshold may depend on x. Uniform convergence requires that, for every positive ε, there be a single N that works for every x in D and every n at least N. The difference lies in the position of the quantifier over x relative to the choice of N.

Imagine a development whose predicate called “uniform convergence” accidentally uses the pointwise quantifier order. This is not a supposition that the kernel secretly ignores quantifiers. The kernel respects exactly the predicate it receives. A long, valid proof about the misnamed predicate can therefore be checked without detecting the error.

Take f_n(x) = x^n on D = [0,1), with candidate limit zero. For each fixed x in this interval, x^n tends to zero. Thus the sequence satisfies the erroneous predicate. But the convergence is not uniform. Set ε = 1/2. Whatever threshold N is proposed, choose a positive integer n at least N, and put x = (3/4)^(1/n). This x belongs to [0,1), while x^n = 3/4. No common threshold controls every point. The formal result under the mistaken predicate is true; the announcement of uniform convergence is false.

Now narrow the domain to [0,1/2]. Here 0 ≤ x^n ≤ (1/2)^n, so a threshold making (1/2)^n less than ε works for all x. Both the correct and mistaken predicates accept the narrowed case. Many checks made only on this smaller domain would therefore be compatible with either predicate. They would show that these instances behave as expected, but they would not test the missing uniformity.

The larger domain supplies a discriminating contrast. The investigator asks whether the formal predicate accepts the sequence on [0,1). If it does, while the intended concept requires the common threshold just shown impossible, the purported identification fails. A definition review can reveal the same failure more directly by inspecting the quantifiers. The contrast is valuable because it gives the formal difference a visible mathematical consequence. It explains what the quantifier order permits and prevents.

Nothing in this example is a report of an actual defective library or a completed machine experiment. It is a deliberately small mathematical model of a possible error. Its simplicity lets us grant complete confidence in proof checking while holding the representational question open. Once the formula is inspected competently, of course, there are no two equally correct readings of its quantifier order. The uncertainty belongs to people assessing the relation between the formula and the announcement, not to the formal semantics itself.

## Correct use without exact correspondence

Suppose a second predicate, also called “uniform convergence,” requires the stronger bound |f_n(x) − f(x)| ≤ 2^(−n) for all n and x. It differs from ordinary uniform convergence. For example, the constant functions f_n(x) = 1/(n+1) converge uniformly to zero on every nonempty domain, but fail this bound for n = 2. Nevertheless, proving the stronger predicate warrants the ordinary uniform conclusion: given ε, choose N so that 2^(−N) < ε. Every n at least N then satisfies the required bound throughout the domain. An objection establishing nonequivalence would defeat a claim of exact translation, while leaving this inference intact.

Even the mistaken pointwise predicate can suffice in a restricted setting. If D is a nonempty finite set and pointwise convergence holds, choose, for each x in D, a threshold N_x for ε. The maximum of these finitely many thresholds works everywhere in D. For the empty domain the condition is vacuous. Thus the quantifier-order difference that defeats the inference on [0,1) does not defeat it on a finite domain. One need not pretend that the predicates are identical. One must supply the missing implication under the actual domain assumption.

There are consequently three different situations: a faithful translation supports a use; a non-equivalent formulation supports it through a justified implication; or the conclusion happens to be true without being supported by the reason offered. On [0,1/2], pointwise convergence of x^n and uniform convergence are both true. Merely observing this agreement does not justify the general inference from pointwise to uniform convergence. The geometric bound does justify the particular conclusion. What matters is the available reason, not merely the truth values of its endpoints.

This changes how a challenge should be assessed. “Your definitions have different extensions” is sufficient against an assertion of equivalence. Against a particular application, it needs a further step: “Here is why that difference threatens the inference you are making in this setting.” A response can establish the needed implication without repairing every discrepancy in terminology. Correcting the name may remain desirable for future readers, but the current inference need not wait for a complete conceptual identification.

## What another successful check contributes

Now suppose that the proposed inference really is threatened. Does repeating the proof check help? It is essential to distinguish a success compatible with two possibilities from one equally informative under them. Compatibility alone says little. A demanding development may succeed more often when produced by a careful team; evidence of care may also reduce reasonable concern about mistranslation. A successful audit may include an operator's inspection of the quantifiers. Both can support confidence in the intended use.

The clean limiting case is narrower. Fix the formal proof object, the formula, the checker, and relevant information about the production process. An assessor is uncertain whether the proved formula licenses the intended inference. Replaying exactly that object produces the same derivability verdict regardless of the assessor's interpretation. Once the derivability result and reproducibility are accepted, a further identical verdict introduces no additional distinction bearing on the contested implication. Any further support would have to come from something else learned during the operation.

This does not describe two possible mathematical worlds with different quantifier semantics. It describes an assessor who knows one fact—the submitted formula has been proved—but has not settled another—the formula supports this use. A deterministic replay can supply additional psychological reassurance without supplying a new reason for that second judgment. Replaying may still be rational if there is uncertainty about files or execution. Removing that uncertainty can increase confidence overall without establishing the missing implication.

The restricted norm is therefore a norm of responsible assurance claims: do not report that a specific threat to an inference has been checked merely because an operation confirmed derivability, if the operation contains no evidence addressing that threat. This is stronger than advice to perform more tests and weaker than a necessary condition for all mathematical knowledge. Warranted reliance need not deductively eliminate every alternative. Process reliability and testimony can make an error sufficiently implausible to permit reliance, even when nobody has conducted the particular check. “We have good reason to rely on this result” and “we checked that these quantifiers support this use” then make different claims.

## When a challenge calls for an answer

An unlimited demand to answer possible mistranslations would make routine mathematics impossible. Merely naming a conceivable typo cannot reopen every settled theorem. A challenge deserves a response when it identifies a plausible mismatch in the actual statement or its dependencies, explains which asserted equivalence or proposed inference it threatens, and points to an issue not already answered by evidence the claimant has adequate reason to trust.

Each condition serves a purpose. The first connects the challenge to the artifact rather than unrestricted skepticism: a displayed quantifier order, an unreviewed new definition with an ambiguous convention, or a concrete conflicting example can supply that connection. The second blocks objections irrelevant to the use, as in the exponential bound. The third allows a response to cite existing review rather than repeat it. A published equivalence theorem or a competent inspection of the very definition in question can close the issue without a new experiment.

Why impose this burden at all? Someone defending a use presents reasons sufficient for that use. A concrete challenge showing an unaddressed way those reasons could fail undermines that defense, unless the challenge itself is answered or the claimed support is narrowed. The claimant need not accept every critic's diagnosis, but cannot restore the same defense simply by repeating the premise whose sufficiency is disputed. This is a constraint on giving reasons to others, not a claim that public debate alone constitutes knowledge.

The stopping condition is similarly local. Once the relevant implication is established, or trustworthy existing evidence explains why the suspected mismatch does not occur, the challenge is answered for this use. Further scrutiny requires a new reason to distrust that evidence or a different proposed use. “Plausible” and “adequate reason” inevitably involve judgment; the rule is no decision procedure for refereeing. But it specifies what that judgment concerns and prevents a perpetual demand for full semantic reconstruction when a limited bridge has already been secured.

## Why contrasts are evidence about mathematics

A contrast is useful only if its expected outcome has a warrant that does not inherit the disputed error. If both the theorem and its test are generated by expanding the same mistaken predicate, their agreement is no surprise. This is a dependence problem even when different people, programs, or proof assistants perform the work. Separation of workers may help, but the relevant separation is between the reasons fixing the intended outcome and the assumption under challenge.

In the convergence example, the intended outcome is anchored by the demand for one threshold and the explicit construction defeating every proposed threshold. That reasoning is open to criticism independently of the name attached to the formal predicate. It can also be formalized. Nothing requires the discriminating evidence to remain informal or to be produced by a human. What matters is that the bridge from the intended question to the test has not simply been assumed in constructing the answer.

Positive contrasts have a role too. The narrowed interval demonstrates that the test is not rejecting convergence indiscriminately. More generally, a useful challenge specifies both what should remain possible and what should become impossible when a mathematically relevant condition changes. Nevertheless, no universal prescription requires paired tests. Sometimes a single definition inspection, an equivalence theorem to an accepted formulation, or a counterexample to a proposed identification is decisive enough for the question at hand.

There is also an asymmetry between success and failure. Finding a case where the formal predicate and the intended condition diverge defeats their claimed equivalence. Agreement on finitely many cases usually cannot establish equivalence over an infinite domain. Successful contrast checks therefore support correspondence defeasibly; they do not replace a general account of the definitions. Their epistemic role is often to remove a particular reason for doubt, rather than to provide an exhaustive semantic foundation.

This distinction prevents a misleading response to powerful proof automation. One cannot repair an uncertain specification by demanding an arbitrarily large collection of certified consequences drawn from it. All may faithfully reflect the same mistaken premise about meaning. The needed gain is evidence for the disputed implication or correspondence. Further elaboration is useful when it supplies that evidence.

These practices are not new inventions. The math-commons validation document already recommends independently grounded instances, consequences outside the initial design targets, and counterexamples exposing inadequate characterizations. It also distinguishes an object's characterization from a usable collection of consequences (math-commons, “The validation ladder” and “Operational vs categorical specifications”). The present proposal concerns a different assessment question: even where exact characterization remains unsettled, what is enough to warrant this particular inference? No historical priority for the general idea of relevant evidence is claimed.

## Three objections

First, is this merely good software engineering? A mistake in a predicate resembles a mistake in a program specification, and the proposed contrasts resemble tests. The resemblance is welcome. But the argument concerns when a mathematical claim is warranted, not only how to reduce development costs. In the example, a fully correct artifact is insufficient for the announced mathematical conclusion because the evidence supports another proposition. Efficiently producing the artifact and being entitled to use it are different questions. Engineering methods can address an epistemic requirement without exhausting its philosophical explanation.

The philosophical consequence is that neither proof-checking rigor nor completeness of conceptual identification alone orders the warrant for particular uses. A fully characterized predicate can be irrelevant to the needed conclusion; a non-equivalent stronger predicate can establish it. In the finite-domain case, a short threshold argument settles the use without establishing the false claim that pointwise and uniform convergence are equivalent on arbitrary domains. Assurance is therefore properly assessed relative to the conclusion and background assumptions being defended.

Second, does the norm overburden testimony? A mathematician routinely uses theorems without checking their definitions from first principles. Must everyone devise a contrast before knowing a formalized result? No. Competent testimony that the statement has been reviewed can support the relevant connection. Familiar definitions in a well-understood setting can make a suggested mistranslation too remote to deserve further work. The duty arises under the concrete challenge conditions just defended. Even then, a user may reasonably rely on another person's answer. The account identifies what the evidential chain must address; it does not insist that each person reconstruct the chain privately.

Third, does formalizing mathematics eliminate the representational gap by making the formal statement the theorem itself? It can eliminate one intended task. A community may decide that its object of study is exactly the explicitly given formal predicate, whatever its suggestive name. Then a correct proof establishes a result about that object, and no prior informal target needs to be recovered. But this does not license importing the consequences of another concept through the name. A new predicate with pointwise quantifiers may be perfectly legitimate; calling its satisfaction “uniform convergence” does not yield a common threshold. The need for correspondence reappears at any boundary where the result is advertised or used in that stronger sense.

These responses mark a limit to the thesis. It is not an account of all mathematical understanding. A person might know that a formal statement faithfully represents a theorem without understanding why the theorem holds, why it matters, or how to generalize it. Conversely, someone might understand the central proof idea while misreading a formal encoding. The relevant competence here is narrower: recognizing enough of the claim's structure to justify the proposed passage from one representation to another.

## The remaining question after success

Formal proof changes the distribution of mathematical uncertainty. It can make enormous chains of inference dependable without making every part of the surrounding announcement equally secure. Recognizing this achievement requires saying exactly which uncertainty has been removed.

An interpretive challenge should therefore be formulated as a mathematical difference with consequences. Does the threshold depend on the point? Does the result cover the advertised domain? Would the proposed representation admit a case the announced concept excludes? The answer may come from direct reading, a checked equivalence, a contrast, or justified testimony about such work. The method can vary. What cannot vary is the need for the answer to bear on the difference being challenged.

A checked proof deserves its authority. That authority does not spread automatically across every identification surrounding it. When a remaining doubt concerns which theorem was checked, another success matters only insofar as it helps answer that remaining question.
