# What Introspection Preserves

You direct attention to a faint bodily sensation and now discern pressure with a definite spatial boundary. Was that bounded pressure already part of your experience? An affirmative answer is tempting: attention apparently revealed what was there. But another possibility is that attention helped produce the definite phenomenal character you now report. The report may be entirely correct about the present without settling the past. Conversely, a sensation that disappears under scrutiny need not have been absent before scrutiny began.

This difficulty raises a selective question: what can we learn from introspection without assuming that it leaves experience untouched? An intervention can change its target while preserving enough about it to support a particular conclusion. We need not establish that introspection leaves experience unchanged. We need to establish that it does not make the specific kind of change that would invalidate the inference we want to draw.

I defend an account that distinguishes three achievements: a conclusive transfer to the past, an increase in a claim’s probability, and enough confidence for warranted belief. For conclusive transfer, a positive report requires excluding the production of the feature from absence, while a negative report requires excluding its elimination. These two exclusions are independent. Mere probabilistic confirmation, however, follows a different pattern: in a simple binary model, both polarities depend on the same comparison of retention with production. Confusing these standards misidentifies what introspective interference defeats. The distinction explains why repeated introspective agreement may improve confidence in a present report while leaving its retrospective application unsupported. It also shows how a locally limited form of first-person authority can survive interference without deciding the wider question of introspection's reliability.

## 1. Accurate about what?

Consider three claims about the pressure example:

1. There is pressure with a definite spatial boundary in my experience now.
2. There was pressure with a definite spatial boundary in my experience just before I attended to it.
3. The intervening transition preserved whether there was pressure with a definite spatial boundary.

The first claim concerns the attended experience. The second concerns an earlier experience. The third concerns their relation. Even granting the first claim maximal authority does not grant the third. Nor does the first entail the second. A theorist who treats the subject as an impeccable observer of her current experience can still question what her observation establishes about the experience preceding scrutiny.

This is not the discovery that introspection can interfere with experience. Spener distinguishes introspective access from the method in which it is employed, and explains why reports of experience altered by attention cannot automatically characterize experience outside that setting. Her discussion supplies the starting point here, particularly the distinction between natural and forced episodes (Spener 2022, §§1, 2.2–3). The proposed advance is a more selective account of the support needed to cross that boundary: preserving every feature is unnecessary, and even preserving the truth value of one feature may demand more than the intended inference requires.

The target must first be specified accurately. In the opening example it is spatially bounded pressure, not pressure of any kind. “Pressure” might mean any felt pressure, a pressure above a specified apparent intensity, or a pressure with a determinate spatial boundary. An intervention can preserve the first while creating the third. It is therefore unhelpful to ask simply whether attention changes the sensation. Likewise, “before introspection” must designate an interval rather than an indefinitely extended background. The difference between an experience immediately before an attentional shift and the experience of the previous hour is not just an increase in difficulty; the relevant transitions differ.

My argument concerns an inference from a report of a present attended condition to a claim about an earlier condition. It does not assume that every introspective judgment works this way. Someone may remember an experience directly, or register an ongoing experience without deliberately attending to it. If a report is genuinely about the past from the outset, its evidential status depends on that retrospective capacity. It should not be reconstructed as a present report merely to make it vulnerable to the argument.

## 2. Two histories, one truthful report

Let F be a specified phenomenal feature. The transition includes everything between the designated times: attention, elapsed time, stimulus changes, bodily fluctuations, and memory dynamics where relevant. Knowing that attention itself cannot create F does not exclude a bodily change creating F during the interval. Suppose a subject accurately reports F after directing attention toward her experience. Two histories can produce this situation. In the first, F was present and the transition retained it. In the second, F was absent and the transition produced it. If the present report is equally expected under both histories, the report alone does not discriminate between them.

The point is epistemic, not a claim that both histories are always equally plausible. Prior knowledge may overwhelmingly favor preservation. A familiar constant sensation might have a well-supported history of surviving comparable attentional changes. But this support comes from more than the accuracy of the present report. One cannot establish preservation simply by insisting that the subject is right about what she now experiences. The two histories grant precisely that correctness.

It helps to separate observation from transition. Write F-before and F-after for the presence of the same specified feature at the two times. A nonproduction condition must hold across the relevant alternative histories of this context, not merely as a material implication about the one actual pair of times. Across those alternatives:

If F-after, then F-before.

Call this the nonproduction condition. Its justification must do more than restate the observed positive outcome; it may draw on the same overall evidence, but cannot simply assume the retrospective conclusion. It does not say that nothing changes. It allows the transition to remove F, to alter other features, or to increase the intensity of F while keeping it within the category under consideration. What it excludes is a transition from the absence of F to its presence. Given an accurate positive report and this condition, a positive retrospective inference follows.

The condition needed for a negative retrospective inference runs the other way:

If F-before, then F-after.

Call this the nonelimination condition. If the subject accurately reports that F is absent afterward, and the transition could not have removed it, F was absent beforehand. This condition permits production, alteration of other features, and any changes that leave F present. It excludes only a transition from presence to absence.

These elementary implications matter because ordinary assurances about attention often point in just one direction. Suppose someone argues that directing attention to a feature can enhance it but cannot suppress it. Even if this were established, it would support a negative retrospective inference, not the positive inference commonly associated with attention's revealing power. Failure to find the feature would count against its prior presence, provided failure to report is an accurate report of absence. Finding the feature would remain compatible with its having been produced. Conversely, if attention can suppress a feature but cannot produce it, finding it would support its prior presence, whereas failing to find it would be inconclusive.

There is no paradox. Turning on a device that can only add a signal makes a remaining absence informative about the earlier absence. It does not make a resulting presence informative about the earlier presence. What is distinctive about introspection is not the logic but the temptation to bypass it by appealing to first-person privilege. Privilege over the output does not determine the direction of the transformation.

Full preservation combines nonproduction and nonelimination. It licenses both positive and negative transfers. But asking for full preservation when only one transfer is at issue sets an unnecessarily demanding standard. Equally, citing a change in experience without identifying its direction can be an insufficient objection. The relevant question is whether the change opens the particular alternative history that would defeat the conclusion.

These formulations concern conclusive transfer given the stated premises. They do not by themselves characterize ordinary evidential support. That difference yields a further result.

## 3. Confirmation without preservation

Assume a binary feature, a fixed context, and an accurate observation of its presence or absence afterward. Let r be the probability of later presence conditional on earlier presence: the retention probability. Let c be the probability of later presence conditional on earlier absence: the production probability. These quantities describe the whole transition, conditional on the background information already available; they are not asserted empirical values.

An observed presence favors earlier presence over earlier absence exactly when r is greater than c, assuming both earlier possibilities have nonzero prior probability and the observation is possible. An observed absence favors earlier absence exactly when 1−c is greater than 1−r. That is the very same ordering, r greater than c. If r equals c, neither observation discriminates the two earlier conditions. If r is smaller than c, both usual interpretations reverse: presence favors earlier absence, and absence favors earlier presence.

Thus the two strict exclusion conditions are independent, but the directions of binary incremental confirmation are linked. Nonproduction corresponds to c=0; nonelimination to r=1. Neither is needed for ordinary confirmation. Nor is a low production probability sufficient for it. What matters is production relative to retention. This is a consequence of the elementary likelihood comparison, not a substantive discovery about human attention.

Two hypothetical cases show why it changes the philosophical assessment. Suppose earlier presence and absence are equally probable, r=0.9, and c=0.8. A positive observation raises the probability of earlier presence to 0.9/(0.9+0.8), approximately 0.529. Production is common, so deduction fails; nonetheless the report supplies positive evidence. A negative observation raises earlier absence to 0.2/(0.2+0.1), approximately 0.667. The two polarities agree in direction but differ in strength. A critic who establishes frequent creation of the phenomenal feature has not thereby shown that present introspection is evidentially useless about its past.

Now let r=0.001 and c=0.01, retaining equal priors. Production is rare, but retention is rarer. A positive observation lowers earlier presence to approximately 0.091. An assurance that the procedure almost never creates the feature would therefore be a misleading defense of positive retrospective use. These numbers stipulate possible transition models; they are not measurements of sensations, attention, or reporting.

For arbitrary prior probability p of earlier presence, the posterior after a positive observation is pr/[pr+(1−p)c]. The likelihood ordering determines whether the probability rises; the prior helps determine how high it ends up. A report can confirm a hypothesis that remains highly improbable. Whether the resulting confidence warrants belief raises further epistemic questions; it cannot be read directly from the fact of confirmation. The claim here is only that these assessments must be separated.

This refinement prevents two opposite mistakes. Possibility of production defeats a guarantee, but not necessarily confirmation. Rarity of production encourages confidence, but does not establish even the direction of confirmation without a retention comparison. The selective question about preservation therefore develops into a comparative question about transition behavior. First-person authority over the observed outcome supplies neither answer by itself.

## 4. Why looking again can miss the problem

Repeated scrutiny seems an obvious remedy for introspective uncertainty. A subject attends, reports a determinate pressure, attends again, and gives the same report. This can strengthen confidence that she is not simply misspeaking or making a transient classificatory mistake. Yet it need not strengthen the inference about what preceded the first act of attention.

Imagine a process that turns an initially diffuse sensation into a determinate one. Once established, the determinate sensation persists under further attention. Every subsequent report is accurate and stable. The initial transition nevertheless produced the feature being investigated. Repetition samples the persistence of the transformed condition. It does not repeat the transition from the original condition.

Now compare a process that merely preserves an already determinate sensation. After the first attentional shift, these two processes may yield the same entire sequence of reports. If the two candidate models assign the same conditional probability to every further report under this procedure, those further reports leave their relative odds unchanged. Even unlimited agreement within that specified procedure then cannot, by itself, identify which initial transition occurred. This is not the claim that repetition never helps. It helps if the competing histories make different predictions about later scrutiny. The point is that persistence after a first intervention and preservation through that intervention are different propositions.

There are consequently two distinct forms of calibration. We may calibrate the judgment: does the subject discriminate and describe the experience she has while reporting? Or we may calibrate the transfer: does that reporting procedure preserve the feature relevant to claims about the prior condition? Success at the former does not constitute success at the latter. This remains true if discrimination becomes extremely fine or subjects acquire a shared vocabulary. Such achievements may make reports of transformed experiences extraordinarily accurate.

Training can also supply transfer evidence. Perhaps variations in the timing or kind of attentional demand produce patterns that one transition model predicts and another does not. But the evidential gain then comes from discriminating the models, not from agreement as such. Similarly, returning repeatedly to a genuinely comparable initial condition differs from continuing to inspect an already modified condition. The distinction should guide interpretation even when neither form of evidence is easy to obtain.

This argument also limits a familiar skeptical response. Schwitzgebel emphasizes both error and indecision in introspection and describes the instability of experiences under reflection (2008, §§XII–XIII). The present concern can remain even after those difficulties are provisionally removed. A perfectly stable, correctly classified experience can be the wrong temporal target. Conversely, temporal transfer can be warranted despite instability when the instability does not threaten the feature or direction at issue. Reliability of observation and legitimacy of retrospective use should therefore receive separate verdicts.

## 5. What a cue establishes

A dispute about reports of briefly presented arrays illustrates the stakes. A subject may be able to identify selected elements after receiving a cue even though she cannot report all the elements together. One interpretation is that the later report retrieves phenomenal detail already present. Another allows the cue to help generate a more determinate experience from information that was previously represented without that phenomenal detail. Sergent and Rees explicitly propose attentional modification as an alternative to pure readout, while distinguishing access to coarse attributes from detailed reports (2007, p. 524).

The directional account specifies the extra burden involved in moving from successful cued identification to prior phenomenal detail. The relevant feature must be the experienced identity or detail in question, rather than the mere presence of stimulus information somewhere in the system. Evidence that information survived the display does not, without further premises, establish that its phenomenal form survived unchanged. A process can preserve information while altering whether or how that information is experienced.

Assume for argument that the subject's cued report accurately describes the phenomenal detail she then has. To infer conclusively that the same detail was experienced before cueing, one must exclude its production during the intervening process. To claim mere confirmation, one instead needs a favorable comparison between the likelihood of the observed detail given its earlier presence and given its earlier absence. It is insufficient to say that the cue could not insert the correct letter's identity because the cue did not contain that identity. The production hypothesis need not say that the cue supplies the information. The cue might alter the use of information already stored. Producing a phenomenal feature and producing the underlying information are different possibilities.

This is not a refutation of phenomenal overflow, the thesis that phenomenal consciousness can exceed cognitive access. A larger body of evidence may support the relevant nonproduction assumption or a theory under which prior phenomenal detail is the best explanation. Nor does the possibility of production establish that the earlier representation was unconscious. The earlier experience might contain coarse features while lacking the later determinate feature. The conclusion is instead a constraint on one evidential step: correctness after cueing cannot do all the work required to identify experience before cueing.

The same constraint applies to the opposing inference. If a subject cannot report a feature after a demanding introspective task, earlier absence does not follow unless the procedure preserved that feature and the reporting process was sufficiently sensitive. Those who invoke the constructive effects of attention against positive reports cannot simply forget its potentially suppressive effects when interpreting negative ones. There is no uniform burden favoring a richer or sparser account of consciousness. For strict transfer the burden follows each polarity; for incremental confirmation the two polarities share a likelihood ordering. An objection must identify which standard it defeats.

A nearby argument also marks the limits of this diagnosis. Giustina and Kriegel’s argument from attention concerns whether conscious experiences can be attended to, and their response to destructive-attention objections requires the possibility of nondestructive attention. They also discuss experience individuation and a reformulation involving successors (2024, §§4, 5.3–5.4). A requirement to certify every actual transition would therefore demand something their modal premise does not assert.

For the present problem, however, neither possible preservation nor token continuity suffices. The possibility of a transition preserving F does not establish that this transition did so. And one continuing headache may cross an intensity threshold while remaining the same headache: preserving the episode would not preserve the truth of “the pain exceeds that threshold.” Conversely, two numerically distinct episodes could both have F. The account needs stable specification of the feature, not a settled metaphysics of experience identity. This shows precisely where an argument about attendability and an inference about a past feature can diverge without either being simply mistaken.

## 6. Is the requirement too demanding?

One objection says that ordinary knowledge never requires an explicit theory of how observation preserves its object. I can know that a cup was on the table before I looked without establishing a formal nonproduction condition. Why should introspection face a stricter test?

It should not. The conditions above describe what supports an inference, not what every knower must articulate. Ordinary background knowledge often excludes the relevant production history well enough. Nor does merely imagining that attention creates a feature defeat everyday knowledge. A live concern requires some reason to take that transition seriously: the nature of the task, evidence of attentional alteration, a credible competing explanation, or a conclusion that explicitly concerns the very dimension attention is being used to sharpen. In a dispute where that concern is already at issue, repeating the current report leaves the dispute untouched. The account locates a burden of response without imposing universal prior certification.

A second objection appeals to direct acquaintance. Perhaps the phenomenal feature is itself involved in the introspective judgment, leaving no room for error about it. Grant this for the sake of argument. It secures a relation between the judgment and its present constituent or object. It does not establish that the same kind of feature occurred before the judgment-generating activity began. A theory could secure direct access to an earlier experience as well, but that would be an additional account of retrospection. The directional argument is compatible with very strong current authority; that compatibility is part of its significance.

A third objection asks whether calibration is circular. To learn that attention does not produce F, must we not compare experience before attention with experience afterward, thus attending to the supposedly unattended experience? Some proposed calibrations will indeed reproduce the original difficulty. The answer is not to declare any agreement sufficient. It is to distinguish observations that depend on the disputed transition from constraints that discriminate between transition models. Retrospective judgments, spontaneous reports, task variations, and independently supported mechanisms may contribute, but none is automatically decisive merely because it bears a different label. Their evidential dependencies must be examined.

The account allows an uncomfortable result: for some features in some conditions, available evidence may not discriminate between production and preservation. That is a limit on knowledge of the earlier feature, not proof that it was absent, proof that it was present, or proof that present introspection fails. Recognizing that local indeterminacy is preferable to allowing confidence in one question to answer another.

Finally, one might object that the proposed framework only restates the obvious fact that inferences need premises. In one sense it does: its logic is deliberately elementary. But the philosophical use is more precise than a demand for “further evidence.” It identifies two independent exclusions for strict transfer, shows why ordinary binary confirmation instead rests on a shared likelihood comparison, and explains why an apparently stronger introspective procedure can leave the relevant transition uncertainty untouched. It thereby changes what counts as a relevant defense or criticism. Demonstrating some modulation is not always a sufficient criticism; demonstrating repeated accuracy is not always a sufficient defense.

## 7. The scope of first-person authority

The same accurate introspective report may settle whether a feature is present during scrutiny, establish that the feature can occur, weakly confirm that it occurred before scrutiny, and fail to warrant belief in that historical claim. These are compatible assessments of different achievements.

An intervention need not preserve an entire experience to preserve something worth knowing. The exclusion of production can secure a positive retrospective transfer while leaving a negative one unsupported; excluding elimination reverses that asymmetry. When neither exclusion holds, a favorable retention-to-production comparison may still yield evidence. But stable reporting about the resulting experience does not by itself establish that comparison or either exclusion.

A useful criticism of introspection must therefore identify the conclusion, the relevant feature, and the standard of support at stake. A useful defense must do the same. Truthful reporting, confirmation, warranted belief, and preservation of an earlier target are distinct achievements. The task is to determine which has actually been secured.
