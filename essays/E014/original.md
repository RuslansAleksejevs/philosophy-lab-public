# Borrowing Without Replenishing: The Ecology of Moral Deference

## 1. A debate conducted in the first person singular

For twenty-five years the philosophical argument about moral testimony has been organised around one question: what is wrong with *me* when I take your word for it that eating animals is wrong? Pessimists have answered that something is. I may know that it is wrong without understanding why, and understanding is what good character and morally worthy action require (Hills 2009). My conviction is not properly mine; I have outsourced something it was my business to do (Nickel 2001; and, at the limit, the autonomy tradition descending from Wolff 1970). Optimists have answered that nothing is. Moral testimony is no worse than testimony about anything else (Sliwa 2012); deferring can be morally required, as the least risky response to my own uncertainty (Enoch 2014); and when deference takes the form of advice rather than bare verdict, adviser and advised can form a joint agent that manages autonomy, understanding and moral worth between them (Wiland 2021).

I think the optimists are right, and I shall not try to rescue any pessimist argument. That concession is meant to be complete: I claim that no individual deferrer is criticisable simply for deferring. What I want to argue is that the shared framing of the debate — what does deference cost *the deferrer*? — has concealed the cost that actually exists. It is not borne by the deferrer. It is borne by the community whose moral norms the deferrer is relying on, and it takes the form of a slow depletion of something that community needs and cannot buy.

The thesis, stated compactly, is this. Moral norms are not self-applying. Keeping them in working order — extending them to unforeseen cases, noticing when an accepted formulation has quietly gone wrong — requires a stock of *applicative competence* distributed across many people. That stock is produced by the very activity that deference replaces: the activity of working out, oneself, what a norm requires here. So moral deference draws on a stock it does not replenish. Each act of drawing is innocent. The aggregate is corrosive. And because the cost is structural rather than personal, the remedies are institutional rather than accusatory.

I call this the *ecological* objection to moral deference, to mark that it concerns the conditions under which a moral practice sustains itself, not the standing of any individual within it. Section 2 isolates the competence at issue and distinguishes it from moral understanding as the pessimists conceive it. Section 3 argues, with help from Hart's account of what it takes for a rule to exist, that morality is unusually dependent on the dispersal of that competence. Section 4 describes the peculiar economic structure of the good. Section 5 defends the crucial disanalogy with medicine and science. Section 6 disarms the suspicion that I am running a bad generalisation argument. Sections 7 and 8 state a criterion and show what it explains. Section 9 answers objections.

## 2. Two competences

Distinguish two things a person might have with respect to a moral norm.

**Verdictive competence** is the capacity to arrive at the correct verdict in the cases one actually confronts. It is what matters if what we care about is that people act rightly.

**Applicative competence** is something else: the capacity to bring a norm to bear on a case that has not been settled in advance — to see that this situation is a case of *that*, to see when two accepted norms pull apart, and, crucially, to register that an accepted verdict does not fit the case and to be able to say what the misfit is. It includes a dispositional element. Someone has applicative competence with respect to a norm only if they stand ready to treat the norm as answerable to their own judgment in application, and to voice the result when their judgment and the received view diverge.

Testimony transmits verdictive competence efficiently and applicative competence hardly at all. That is the familiar observation on which pessimism was built. But pessimists drew an individual moral from it: the deferrer lacks something that makes her action worthy, or her belief properly held. That moral is what the optimists have successfully contested. Sliwa's case is representative: since moral knowledge does not generally require grasping the grounds of one's belief, and since we do not think the layperson's medically informed decisions are defective, the deferrer's belief is in good order and her action can be morally worthy.

My claim requires none of this to be false. Applicative competence might be worth nothing at all to its possessor — no contribution to her character, no enhancement of her moral worth, no improvement in her hit rate — and the ecological argument would go through unchanged. What matters for my argument is not what applicative competence does for the person who has it, but what its *distribution across a population* does for that population's capacity to keep its moral norms in repair. That is a different property of a different bearer. It is why the ecological objection is insulated from the entire individual-level dispute: the optimists can win every round and the objection stands.

One clarification before proceeding. Applicative competence is content-specific and earned case by case. It is not a general faculty of moral reasoning that, once acquired, applies everywhere. To be positioned to notice that the received understanding of consent mishandles some class of situations, one must have worked through cases of consent — not cases of promising or of fairness. This matters later: it means the good can be plentiful in one region of morality and nearly absent in another, and it means the losses from deference are local to the domains in which people defer.

## 3. Norms need maintainers, and morality has no officials

Why should a community need applicative competence spread widely, rather than concentrated in a few reliable heads?

Start with a structurally similar case where the answer is known to be subtle. On Hart's account, a legal system rests at bottom not on force and not on a presupposed norm but on a social rule — the rule of recognition — which, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it, is "a social rule that exists only because it is actually practiced, that is, used to guide conduct" ("Legal Positivism", §2). What it is for that rule to be practised is for the relevant people to take toward it what Hart calls the internal point of view: they "use it as a standard for guiding and evaluating their own and others' behavior, and this use is displayed in their conduct and speech, including the resort to various forms of social pressure to support the rule and the ready application of normative terms such as 'duty' and 'obligation' when invoking it" (ibid.).

Now the instructive part. Hart's view is that the rule of recognition is an *official* custom. Law is a technical enterprise with a division of labour, and so, in the encyclopedia's summary, "[o]rdinary subjects' contribution to the existence of law may therefore amount to no more than passive compliance, and in an extreme case perhaps less than this" (ibid.). A legal system can exist, by Hart's criteria, while almost nobody outside the courts and legislature takes the internal point of view toward its foundations. And Hart does not treat this as a neutral fact: "this division of labor is not a normatively neutral fact about law; it is politically charged, for it sets up the possibility of law becoming remote from the life of a society, a hazard to which Hart is acutely alert" (ibid., citing Hart 1961 [2012: 117]).

Two lessons follow, and the second is the one I need.

The first is that the internal point of view is not the same as agreement or compliance. A person can sincerely assert that cruelty is wrong, hold others to it, feel indignation at violations — and still be, with respect to that norm, a consumer rather than a maintainer, because when asked *why this counts as cruelty* she has nothing to offer but the citation of an authority. Deference occupies an awkward intermediate position that the pessimists sensed and mislabelled. It is behaviourally and even emotionally internal, and deliberatively external. Nothing in that observation convicts the deferrer of anything. It does locate what she is not contributing.

The second lesson is the disanalogy. Law can tolerate mass passivity because it has officials: an institutionally defined group who are professionally required to take the internal point of view, whose disagreements are staged in public, whose reasons must be written down, and whose errors are correctable by identifiable procedures. Even so, Hart flags the hazard of remoteness. Morality has no officials. There is no body whose acceptance of a moral norm constitutes its existence, no procedure that makes one reading of "respect" authoritative rather than another, no appellate structure. The people who play, for morality, the role that judges play for law simply *are* the ordinary participants, considered severally. The maintenance of moral norms has no institutional redundancy to fall back on when participation thins out. When ordinary subjects of the moral law go passive, nobody is left holding the internal point of view on their behalf.

This is why the standard consolation — that the experts still understand, so nothing is lost — is weaker in ethics than it looks. In law it would be a decent consolation, and Hart still thought it carried a political cost. In morality there is no expert body constituted as such, and the nearest approximations (professional ethicists, religious authorities, advice columnists, compliance officers) have no claim to constitute the norms they interpret.

## 4. The structure of the good

What sort of good is a population's stock of applicative competence? It does not fit the standard typology cleanly, and seeing exactly how it fails to fit is what makes the case distinctive.

Take the features catalogued by Cullity's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on the free rider problem (§3.3). Public goods are characterised by non-excludability together with features such as "[n]onrivalness: one person's enjoyment of the good does not diminish the benefits available to anyone else from its enjoyment". Common-pool goods, following Ostrom and Ostrom (1977), are non-excludable but rival — fish stocks are the standard case. Club goods, following Buchanan (1965), are excludable but non-rival.

A community's stock of applicative competence is non-excludable in the way that matters: anyone can draw on it by asking, and the benefits of having norms that are in good repair fall on everyone, including those who have never thought a moral thought through. It is non-rival in consumption at a time: my asking you does not use you up, and the benefit I take does not reduce what is available to others.

So far this looks like a plain public good, and if that were all, the free-rider structure would be familiar and my thesis unoriginal. But it has a further feature that the standard typology does not name. The good is *produced* by exactly the activity for which consuming it substitutes. To consume the good is to take a verdict from someone else rather than work it out; to produce it is to work it out. For each agent, on each occasion, consumption and production are alternatives in a single choice. Call a good with this feature **substitution-coupled**: its consumption technology competes with its production technology at the level of the individual decision.

Substitution-coupling matters because it defeats the usual reasons for optimism about public goods. We normally expect the supply of a public good to be robust because some people have idiosyncratic reasons to produce it, or because production and consumption are independent activities that can both expand. Here they cannot both expand in the same person at the same time. A rise in consumption is, by the same token, a fall in production. The good therefore erodes not through overuse — nothing is worn out — but through *displacement*.

Notice also, following Cullity's entry, that this need not be a pure public good for the problem to arise: "Free rider problems can arise when goods are collectively produced, whether those goods are themselves public or private. All that is needed is the perception that one can receive the good without having contributed to producing it" (§3.3). Applicative competence satisfies that minimal condition conspicuously. Nothing in my argument requires settling whether the stock is best classified as a public good or a common-pool resource.

There are non-moral instances of substitution-coupling, and they are worth having in view because they show that the structure is real and that its consequences vary. Navigational skill in a city and turn-by-turn directions; mental arithmetic and calculators; the mutual intelligibility of a dialect and the option of switching to the prestige language. In each, the convenient substitute does displace the competence. But in the first two cases most of us are untroubled, and for a reason that will become the criterion of section 7: the substitute is maintained by an external system that is *independently* error-corrected. The satellites do not need me to know the way.

## 5. Why ethics is not medicine

The optimists' best card is the parallel with non-moral expertise. You defer to cardiologists, structural engineers and historians of the Reformation without embarrassment, and nobody thinks the practice of medicine is degraded by patients who cannot read an echocardiogram. Why should ethics differ?

I think there are two differences, and together they carry the argument.

**(i) Morality has no exogenous error signal.** Medicine is checked by something that is not medical opinion. Patients recover or they do not; the bridge stands or falls; the predicted isotope ratio is found or it is not. This check is available to the expert community whether or not laypeople possess any competence at all, which is precisely why the division of epistemic labour in the sciences is not self-undermining. The stock of lay scientific competence can go to zero and the error-correcting mechanism keeps running.

There is nothing that plays this role for the application of moral norms. No experiment tells a community that it has over-extended "consent", or drawn "desert" too narrowly, or built an exception into "honesty" that serves the powerful. The signal that an accepted moral formulation has gone wrong is, characteristically, that someone meets a case, finds that the received verdict does not sit right, and can articulate the misfit well enough for others to feel it too. That is not a supplement to moral error-correction; in the domain of application it is very nearly the whole of it. Which means that in ethics, unlike in medicine, the distributed lay competence *is* the error-correcting mechanism. Deference in medicine leaves the corrective apparatus intact. Deference in ethics consumes it.

I should say precisely how strong this claim is. I am not claiming that moral truths depend on us, nor that there is no such thing as moral expertise, nor even that moral beliefs are unconstrained by evidence — plainly many moral questions turn on empirical sub-questions that the world can settle. The claim is about the *application* of accepted norms to cases: about the layer at which a community works out what its norms come to. At that layer the feedback is internal to the practice, and it runs through people's responses to cases.

**(ii) Distributed judgment decorrelates error.** Suppose someone grants (i) but replies that a small expert body could do the applying, and do it better, since it would think harder and more consistently than a dispersed public. This reply mistakes what dispersal is for.

The value of having applicative competence spread widely is not primarily that it multiplies the number of thoughts. It is that it *decorrelates* the failures. The blind spots of a small, institutionally defined, socially similar group are similar to one another; the same background assumptions that make a received formulation feel obviously right to one member make it feel obviously right to the rest. A dispersed population's blind spots are much less alike, because people occupy different positions and the norms that misfire misfire on different people. Error-detection by a body whose errors are correlated is close to worthless, however competent the body. Error-detection by a dispersed population is powerful even if each member is individually mediocre, provided the members are positioned differently.

This is why the historical record cuts against the expert-body reply rather than for it. When a community has revised a badly drawn moral norm, the decisive pressure has usually come from those whom the received formulation handled worst — people positioned to feel the misfit, whose competence about *that* norm was purchased by having to live under it. It is hard to think of a case in which a settled moral formulation was overturned because a professional body of moral appliers noticed, from the inside, that it was wrong. This suggests that the applicative competence most worth having in a population is precisely the competence of those with the least institutional authority: the ones whose deference is, on the standard picture, the most reasonable thing in the world.

## 6. Am I just saying "what if everybody did that?"

The objection to face now is that this is a familiar bad argument in new clothing. Generalisation arguments — it would be disastrous if everyone did it, therefore you must not — are notoriously unreliable, and the literature on aggregation has taught us to be suspicious of moving from a bad collective outcome to an individual verdict, whether through mistakes in moral mathematics (Parfit 1984) or through the difficulty of saying when one imperceptible contribution makes a difference (Kagan 2011).

Three replies, in ascending order of importance.

First, I am not drawing the individual conclusion. The generalisation arguments that have been refuted all terminate in a verdict about what a particular agent may do. Mine terminates in a description of a structural feature of a practice, and in recommendations addressed to advisers and institutions. A collective action problem, on Cullity's definition, is a situation in which "it is better for every member of a group that everyone does A than that no one does A, but conflicts in what the parties regard as being in their own interest risk producing an outcome that is worse for every member than everyone's doing A would be" (§1). Identifying such a structure is not yet blaming anybody, and the standard replies to generalisation arguments leave the identification untouched.

Second, where there is a marginal contribution here, it is not imperceptible in the way pollution is. Applicative competence is lumpy and individually located: a person either has worked through cases of this kind or has not. There is no continuum along which a contribution might vanish below a threshold of detectability. When someone acquires competence about consent, the community contains one more node at which a misapplication of the consent norm could be caught, and this is a discrete fact about a discrete person.

Third, and most important: the correct unit of assessment is not the act but the *policy*. One act of deference no more depletes a person's applicative competence than one missed run depletes an athlete. The depletion claim concerns standing dispositions — what someone characteristically does when a moral question arises in a domain — and standing dispositions are efficacious in exactly the way single acts are not. If I adopt the policy of settling questions about my professional obligations by consulting the compliance handbook, it is not a matter of chance or of thresholds whether I develop judgment about those obligations: I do not. The aggregation puzzles that trouble generalisation arguments arise because single acts make vanishingly small differences to a collective outcome. Policies make unmysterious differences to whether *one person* has a competence, and the collective stock is nothing more than the count of such persons and the diversity of their positions.

So the ecological argument is not a generalisation argument. It is closer to an argument about a training regime than an argument about pollution.

## 7. The Replenishment Conditions

When is deference ecologically cheap? Three conditions, each scalar, and deference is innocent to the extent that one of them holds.

**R1. Codifiability.** Where a norm's correct application in a domain is stable and statable in rules, applicative competence adds little, because there is little applying left to do. Do not take more than your share of the shared supplies; do not park in the disabled bay. Here the informational content of the norm survives transmission almost intact, and there is nothing for a dispersed population to notice that a rule-book would miss.

**R2. External check.** Where the domain has an error signal that does not run through the community's own moral responses — typically because the contested questions are substantially empirical — deference does not remove the corrective apparatus. Much applied ethics has this character in part: whether a policy in fact harms the people it is meant to help is not settled by anyone's moral sensibility.

**R3. Transmission.** Where the deference itself builds applicative competence, it produces the good rather than merely consuming it. Advice that gives reasons, teaching, apprenticeship, an exemplar whose example is legible enough to be learned from rather than merely copied: in these cases the substitution-coupling is broken, because the consuming act is also a producing act.

Where all three are weak — a contested, uncodifiable domain, with no external check, and deference in the form of bare verdicts — deference is at its most costly. And this, notably, is the profile of precisely the cases the pessimists chose as their examples. The literature's stock cases involve exactly the questions on which reasonable people differ, on which no evidence is decisive, and on which the deferrer takes a verdict without reasons.

To these three conditions add one structural remedy, addressed not to the individual but to the design of advisory institutions: **plurality**. Deference distributed across many mutually contesting advisers preserves decorrelation far better than deference to a single authority, even a more reliable one. This gives a principled reason to prefer a noisy plurality of moral advisers to a single excellent one — a preference that is unintelligible if the only thing that matters is the accuracy of the verdicts received. It also connects the present argument to Ostrom's empirical finding that self-organising groups can solve common-pool problems without either state regulation or privatisation when conditions including reliable information, communication among members, informal monitoring, and prior social capital are satisfied (Ostrom 2010, as reported in Cullity §4). The moral case is not a case for regulation. It is a case for the conditions under which a practice polices its own stock.

## 8. What the view explains

A theory earns its place partly by explaining data its rivals leave unexplained. The ecological view explains four things.

*Why unease about moral deference varies with the domain.* It is hard to feel troubled by someone who takes another's word about an obscure point of research ethics, and easy to feel troubled by someone who takes another's word about whether their own behaviour toward a colleague was demeaning. Individual-level accounts must say that the same epistemic or aretaic defect is present in both and merely feels different. The ecological view says the cases differ in their R1 and R2 scores, and that the unease is tracking a real variable.

*Why advice seems better than bare testimony.* Wiland's observation that advice is different in kind from testimony is, I think, correct, and his explanation — that adviser and advised form a joint agent — is one way to account for it. The ecological view gives another, which does not require joint agency: advice typically carries reasons, and reasons transmit applicative competence. Advice can satisfy R3; "just take my word for it" cannot.

*Why deference to a system feels worse than deference to a friend, even when the system is more reliable.* On any purely individual and purely accuracy-based account this should be backwards. On the ecological view it is predicted: the friend gives reasons and is one of many, so R3 and plurality are in play; a single authoritative system gives outputs and is one of one.

*Why the debate has had the shape it has.* This is the payoff I care about most. For two decades, pessimists have reported a clear sense that something is wrong with moral deference, and every attempt to locate that wrongness in the deferrer has proved either too strong (condemning benign cases) or too weak (yielding a pro tanto cost easily outweighed). That pattern is exactly what we should expect if the cost is real but misattributed — if a structural feature of the practice has been repeatedly translated into the vocabulary of personal defect, which is the only vocabulary the debate's framing made available. The ecological view explains not just the intuitions but the persistent failure to systematise them.

## 9. Objections

**"This presupposes anti-realism."** It does not. Nothing in the argument concerns the truth-makers of moral claims, which may be as mind-independent as you like. The argument concerns a community's *capacity to apply* norms correctly and to detect when it has applied them incorrectly, and that capacity is plainly a contingent feature of populations. The arithmetical truths are eternal; the quantity of arithmetical competence in a society is not, and can fall. A realist should find the ecological point especially congenial, since realism makes room for the thought that a community's received moral formulations can be *wrong*, and hence raises, more urgently than anti-realism does, the question of what mechanism is supposed to detect that.

(A stronger version of the argument is available to those with weaker metaethical scruples. If some morally significant facts — what counts as an insult, what a fair share is, what consent requires in a given practice — are partly constituted by shared understandings, then a community that outsources application does not merely lose track of the answer; there ceases to be an answer of the relevant kind. I set this aside. The argument does not need it.)

**"This is elitist, or overdemanding."** The opposite, on both counts. The pessimist tradition told ordinary people that their deference made their actions less worthy and their beliefs less properly their own. I say it does not. What I say instead is that a moral culture which has arranged things so that most people rarely exercise judgment has a problem, and that the problem belongs to the culture. The corresponding duties fall most heavily not on those who defer but on those who are deferred *to*. If applicative competence is the scarce good, then anyone in a position to transmit it — a teacher, an adviser, an institution issuing guidance, a person asked for their view — has strong reason to answer in the way that builds it, which normally means giving reasons rather than verdicts. That relocation of the normative burden is, I think, the most useful practical consequence of the view, and it is precisely the reverse of pessimism's.

**"The empirical claim is unverified."** Partly true, and I want to be exact about where. The argument has one empirical hostage: that applicative competence is acquired mainly by exercise, so that policies of deference in a domain leave a population with less of it in that domain. I think this is as well supported as any claim about skill, but it is not established here, and I can say what would tell against it. If it turned out that people who habitually defer on some class of questions were nonetheless just as good at spotting misapplications when presented with hard cases, the mechanism would fail and the thesis with it. Everything else in the argument — the substitution-coupling, the absence of an exogenous check, the decorrelation value of dispersal — is conceptual or historical rather than hostage to that finding.

**"Mill said this."** He said something adjacent, and the difference is instructive. Mill argued that a true opinion not "fully, frequently, and fearlessly discussed" is "held as a dead dogma, not a living truth", that a belief which "abides as a prejudice, a belief independent of, and proof against, argument" is "not knowing the truth", and that a creed "received passively, not actively" ends up "as it were outside the mind, encrusting and petrifying it" (*On Liberty*, ch. 2). He even canvassed the division-of-labour reply in a form that anticipates the optimists — that "simple minds" may "repose in the assurance" that the difficulties "have been or can be answered, by those who are specially trained to the task" — and rejected it.

But notice what Mill's rejection turns on: that the specially trained answerers themselves need free discussion, and that suppression starves them of the objections they must answer. His topic is the suppression of opinion; his diagnosis of the passive believer is individual ("this is not knowing the truth"); and his social claim is essentially an aggregate of individual losses of conviction's "vital effect" on conduct.

The present argument differs on all three counts. It concerns voluntary deference under conditions of perfect liberty, where nothing is suppressed and the objectors are simply absent because too few people are in a position to raise objections. Its diagnosis of the deferrer is that she is fine. And the good it identifies is not an aggregate of individual understandings but a functional property — error-detection capacity — that depends on the *number and positional diversity* of competent appliers, and so can be low even where every individual understanding is of high quality. Mill's free-discussion remedy does not address it: you may have the freest discussion imaginable and still have almost nobody able to contribute a case-based objection. Mill is the ancestor; the child is not the parent.

**"Does this argument apply to itself?"** It does, and comfortably. I have given reasons rather than asked for trust, which is what R3 requires. A reader who accepts the conclusion because I asserted it would be a small instance of the phenomenon described, and, consistently with everything above, would not thereby be doing anything wrong.

## 10. A distributive corollary, and a coda

One consequence deserves to be stated on its own, because it turns an epistemological point into a political one.

If applicative competence is produced by the exercise of judgment, then it is produced by an activity that costs time, attention and a certain security — the security of being able to afford to be wrong. These are unequally distributed. So the question of *who in a society gets to be a maintainer of moral norms, and who is left a consumer of them* is not settled by anything like merit. It is settled substantially by the distribution of leisure and standing. And section 5 gave us the reason this matters beyond fairness: the applicative competence most valuable to a community is that of the people its norms handle worst, since theirs are the positions from which misfits are visible. A society that arranges for those people, in particular, to be consumers rather than maintainers has disabled the part of its error-correcting apparatus that was doing the most work.

This is also the condition under which moral authority is captured. Where few can apply a norm independently, whoever is authoritative about its application effectively determines its content, and does so without the friction that dispersed competence would generate. The ecological objection to moral deference is thus continuous with a familiar worry about domination, and gives that worry an epistemic mechanism.

Finally, a coda I have deliberately kept out of the argument. The account was developed without reference to automated moral advice and stands or falls independently of it. But it does yield a test. A cheap, universally available, highly reliable source of moral verdicts is the limiting case of a substitution-coupled deference technology: it scores superbly on accuracy, badly on R3 if it delivers verdicts rather than reasons, badly on plurality to the extent that it is one system consulted by everyone, and it supplies no external check of the kind that makes deference to medicine safe. On the individual-level framing, such a technology raises no question beyond whether its outputs are true. On the ecological framing, the design questions are different and sharper: does consulting it build or bypass applicative competence, are its verdicts contestable by their recipients, and are there enough independent sources that the community's errors remain uncorrelated? Those are questions about institutions, which is where, if I am right, the moral testimony debate should have been all along.
