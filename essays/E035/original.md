# What a Selection Establishes: Allocation Choice and Whole-Life Comparison

## Abstract

A decision about who receives a scarce benefit can be described as if it ranked the recipients’ lives. That inference needs an additional premise. I defend a limited claim: the selected allocation, together with a rule stated in terms of claims to a particular resource, does not by itself entail a complete weak-betterness ordering of the recipients’ lives as wholes. A clinic lottery illustrates the point only conditionally. Equal chances can be a fair way to handle genuinely equal claims to an indivisible good, but the fairness of that procedure is a separate normative premise, not a result of the draw. Sen’s work on choice under incomplete rankings is a close formal predecessor, while Broome and Vong directly address equal-claim lotteries. These sources limit any novelty claim and also help state the example more carefully. A short “bridge check” can expose an unsupported move from an allocation reason to a whole-life judgment. I offer it as a heuristic synthesis, not a new theory of allocation or a finding about actual clinical practice.

## 1. A selection is not yet a life comparison

Imagine a clinic with one indivisible treatment slot left for the day. Two patients, Ada and Ben, meet its eligibility requirements. For this deliberately narrow example, the relevant evidence supports the same expected benefit and urgency for each. Neither has a different right, promise, or relevant treatment history. The clinic’s policy treats their claims as equal. It cannot divide the treatment or obtain another slot, and its procedure gives each claimant an equal chance through a public draw. Ada’s name is drawn.

The draw settles who receives the slot. It does not by itself tell us that Ada’s life is better than Ben’s, that Ben’s is worse, or that the two lives are equally good. It does not say which patient is more worthy or whose life matters more. It records the result of a specified allocation procedure. To infer a ranking of complete lives, we need a premise connecting that result to whole-life well-being.

The claim defended here is correspondingly narrow: **a selected allocation, under a rule framed in terms of claims to a particular resource, does not by itself entail a complete weak-betterness ordering of the recipients’ lives as wholes.** A complete weak-betterness ordering is reflexive and transitive and compares every relevant pair of lives: for any two, it says that one is at least as good as the other, or that they are equally good. This is a claim about comparative well-being, not equal moral worth. It does not assert that no such ordering exists, that every justified allocation can be defended without any whole-life facts, or that a real policy never relies on such facts. It says that the selection and the allocation rule, as stated, do not settle the full comparison unless a further bridge is supplied.

“Justified” also needs a limit. The draw is not justified merely because it happened. Its use depends on a defensible account of which claims count, whether they really are equal, and whether equal chances are fair in this kind of conflict. The essay will give a modest conditional defense of that procedure while leaving open whether it is all-things-considered required. A critic may reject the fairness premise. If so, this clinic case cannot show that the actual clinic made a justified allocation. The logical point survives only as a conditional one: if an allocation rule is independently defensible, its selection need not encode a complete comparison of whole lives.

The opening claim is not that institutions commonly make a specific mistake. A decision can be described as though choosing one person established that person’s life as superior. Whether a particular policy makes that inference is a question for the policy’s actual reasons and language. The present example gives a controlled way to ask what a selection establishes.

## 2. Three questions with different objects

Consider three questions that can be connected but should not be conflated.

First, which feasible action should an allocator take? The options may be to assign treatment to Ada, assign it to Ben, or use a procedure that determines which assignment occurs. This is a question about actions or allocation outcomes.

Second, whose claim has priority with respect to this resource, at this time, under these circumstances? Priority might turn on urgency, a right, expected benefit, a promise, or a history of exclusion. This question concerns claims and action-guiding reasons. Its answer depends on which reasons the policy recognizes and how they interact.

Third, how do Ada’s and Ben’s complete lives compare in well-being? That question concerns each life as a whole, not only the consequences of receiving this treatment or the strength of a claim to it. A society might affirm equal moral worth while judging one life better in welfare, or it might leave the welfare comparison unsettled.

The relations can overlap. A policy might explicitly rank expected lifetime benefits or give priority to people worse off over their lives. Such a policy brings whole-life facts into its justification. A rights-based policy might instead honor a prior promise. An equal-access policy might focus on whether the claimants have equal standing for this resource. These reasons require separate defenses. Calling a reason “local” or “procedural” does not show that it is sound, and calling it “welfare” does not show that it ranks complete lives. The decision-maker must specify what is being compared and why that comparison matters.

This distinction also clarifies what “does not entail” means. It is not a claim that a chooser has no beliefs about the recipients’ lives. Nor is it a claim that the allocation rule’s full justification could not rely on such beliefs. It is a claim about logical sufficiency: the selected option does not, without an added premise, establish a complete relation among the beneficiaries’ lives. If a policy does contain a premise such as “priority goes to the person whose complete life would be better,” then the bridge is present and the example no longer applies in that form.

## 3. A conditional countermodel

Let the available options be treatment for Ada and treatment for Ben. Suppose an independently defensible rule says that each has an equal claim to this indivisible slot and that an equal-chance procedure is fair when no further relevant reason distinguishes them. The public draw selects Ada. Keep those allocation facts and that draw fixed. The complete welfare comparison could still be that Ada’s life is better, that Ben’s is better, that they are equally good, or that the comparison is incomplete. The same result of the draw is compatible with each possibility unless the rule adds a further premise connecting the result to whole-life betterness.

This shows what the countermodel can and cannot establish. It shows that a selection under an equal-claims rule does not determine one complete life ordering. It does not prove that the rule is independently justified in every setting, or that its justification never draws on facts about the claimants’ lives. Those are substantive matters. The countermodel’s work is only to separate the conclusion about the selection from the stronger conclusion about whole-life comparison.

The formal distinction between choice and ordering is familiar. Sen’s discussion of collective choice explains that consistent choice need not always presuppose a complete ordering, while also showing that an incomplete relation can leave some menus unresolved (Sen 1970, 47–48). His later “Maximization and the Act of Choice” is closer still: Sen says that a decision may have to be made even when the ranking is incomplete (1997, 746–47). That paper addresses choice behavior and the structure of maximization; it does not itself supply a normative defense of a clinic’s procedure. This boundary matters. A fact about how choices can be made is not yet a claim about which allocation is fair.

The present distinction should not be mistaken for a new formal result. Sen’s work is a close predecessor for the point that choice and complete ordering are different tasks. The more modest question here is what a choice licenses us to infer about a different object: the comparative welfare of the people receiving or missing out on a resource. Even there, the argument is not a field-wide originality claim. It is a reminder to state the bridge premise when moving from an allocation reason to a comparison of whole lives.

An acyclic strict-priority relation over a finite, nonempty set of options has at least one maximal option. A unique maximum can be selected by that relation. Several maximal options remain tied under it and require some additional selection rule. A cycle may leave no maximal option. This formal observation diagnoses what a relation can select; it does not justify the priority relation. A complete ordering of allocations, even if a rationality theory requires one, would not automatically become an ordering of recipients’ complete lives. The mapping between those objects would itself require an argument.

## 4. What can make an equal-claims lottery fair?

The clinic’s draw needs a defense that is more than “there was a tie.” Broome’s discussion of fairness is directly relevant. He treats a lottery as a way of distributing scarce goods among equal or nearly equal claims, and describes lottery chances as a kind of surrogate satisfaction of claims. He writes that “if claims are close to equality, holding a lottery will be fairer than not” (Broome 1990, 99). But he also distinguishes fairness from what is all-things-considered required and warns that merely calling a lottery a tie-breaker does not explain its fairness.

That warning is right. A tie-breaker label reports what a procedure does; it does not defend why the procedure treats claimants fairly. The modest defense available in the clinic case is this: if the policy has already established that the two claims are equal on the relevant measure, if the benefit is indivisible, and if no right, history, urgency, or other reason favors either claimant, then equal chances are one way to give each claim the same opportunity of satisfaction. The draw also prevents officials from converting discretion into an unexplained advantage. Its fairness rests on the accepted principle that equal claims to this good should receive equal chances when they cannot both be met. This is a normative premise. It is not proved by the lottery itself, and someone may reject it.

The principle has a limited force. It gives a reason to regard the lottery as a fair procedure under stipulated conditions; it does not establish that the lottery is the only fair procedure, that it is always required, or that it overrides every other consideration. If the public draw is biased, if officials manipulate the pool, if a claimant has a prior right, or if equal claims are a mistaken description, the defense fails. Repeated losses may also make a sequence of individually equal draws unfair unless the policy considers cumulative access. Procedural fairness must be assessed in the actual institutional context.

Vong’s analysis makes the boundaries especially clear. He identifies equal-conflict cases in which people have equally strong claims to an equal benefit and the good is scarce and indivisible; he also distinguishes procedural fairness from the all-things-considered moral requirement to use a given lottery (Vong 2020, 320–24). The two-patient example is a simple, non-overlap case of that familiar allocation problem. Vong’s work is a close precedent for the lottery setting, not a source from which this essay can claim a new lottery theory. Cases where claimants’ claims overlap or lotteries distribute chances unevenly raise further problems; nothing here settles them.

Taurek’s proposal for equal chances in a one-versus-five case provides a boundary rather than support for generalizing this clinic example (Taurek 1977, 303). There, the number of people affected makes the claims unequal in a way that the stipulated two-person case avoids. The controversy about equal concern and numbers cannot be resolved by simply repeating that everyone matters. The current argument therefore covers neither unequal numbers nor unequal claims. It explains what a draw among stipulated equal claims does not establish about complete lives.

## 5. Why the non-entailment remains limited

The lottery is one possible illustration, but the logical point does not depend on lotteries. An allocation may be selected because someone has a stronger claim under a defensible policy, such as a right to a promised service. That reason may concern a duty owed in the allocation, not a ranking of the recipient’s life as a whole. A critic may argue that the duty’s ultimate justification depends on a wider theory of value. That is possible. The important question is whether the policy has supplied a premise that ranks complete lives. If it has, its result may bear on that ranking. If it has not, the mere selection does not fill the gap.

Nor should the argument use “incomparable” as a shortcut. Chang argues that better, worse, and equally good do not exhaust every evaluative relation: items may be on a par without being either better, worse, or equally good (Chang 2002, 659–67). She also emphasizes that a comparison depends on a covering consideration—what is being compared and under which value or duty. Her account cautions against inferring incomparability whenever a complete ranking has not been established. The clinic’s equal-claims comparison may concern fair access, while the whole-life comparison remains open. That does not show that the lives are incomparable; there might instead be parity, an unknown fact, a borderline comparison, or a determinate ranking not settled by the allocation.

The claim is likewise not that decisions can ignore history. Parfit’s discussion of equality and priority stresses that past disadvantage, current need, and the way a person’s situation came about can provide different grounds for priority (Parfit 2000, 83, 101–03). The clinic example holds such facts fixed only to isolate the inference. In a real policy, an omitted history may make the claims unequal. A count of previous treatments could serve as an administrative proxy, but it would need a defense: different procedures may not represent equivalent benefits, and a count may miss need, severity, effectiveness, or lifetime disadvantage. Coherence in a proxy rule does not establish justice.

This is why the scope is logical rather than a general ethical conclusion. A prioritarian or aggregative policy may expressly rely on whole-life welfare and make it decisive. A rights-based policy may rely on facts about a promise and a past wrong. An equal-access rule may rely on a procedural account of claims. The merits differ. The selection alone cannot tell us which rationale the policy actually uses; we have to inspect its premises.

## 6. A small bridge check, applied

A short set of questions can help inspect an inference from an allocation to a whole-life judgment. It is a heuristic synthesis, not a new theory.

1. **What are the objects?** Does the rule compare complete lives, expected treatment outcomes, claims to a resource, needs, rights, or feasible actions?
2. **What is the stated reason?** Is priority based on urgency, a chance of benefit, fair access, an obligation, lifetime disadvantage, or something else?
3. **What premise connects that reason to whole-life betterness?** Is the rule explicitly ranking total welfare, or is the comparison being inferred from a local selection?
4. **What facts could defeat the rule?** Could a prior right, unequal need, omitted history, bias in the procedure, repeated access, or a cycle in priorities unsettle its result?

Apply the questions to the clinic. The objects are two claims to one slot, stipulated equal under the clinic’s accepted measure. The stated reason for the draw is equal opportunity when neither claim has priority. The result is that Ada receives the slot. Unless the policy adds a bridge such as “the selected patient’s complete life is better,” the draw gives no premise for that conclusion. If an official nevertheless announces that the clinic has judged Ada’s life superior, the check reveals the missing step: a fair selection of one claimant does not supply a whole-life welfare comparison. If Ben had a prior right or a materially different need, the equal-claims premise would fail and the draw might no longer be fair.

This example shows what the check contributes without claiming that it is novel: it makes the objects, reason, and missing bridge explicit in one case. It can also show when the bridge is present. If the policy states that the person with the greater expected lifetime welfare should receive the slot, the rule has introduced a whole-life premise; the critic should then assess that premise rather than accuse the policy of deriving a ranking from selection alone. The check does not determine which allocation is just. It points to the premise that must be defended.

The essay offers no evidence that institutions commonly make the error illustrated here, and it does not analyze a real policy document. Its motivating case is hypothetical. The check is a compact way to ask a question that formal choice theory and the lottery literature leave for the specific justification: which comparison supports this allocation, and does that comparison rank lives as wholes? Its value, if any, is clarificatory and pedagogical.

## 7. Objections

### A justified choice requires a complete ranking

An objector may argue that rational or morally justified choice requires a complete ordering of all options. Sen’s work does not settle that normative dispute. His 1997 discussion concerns choice behavior and incomplete rankings; it shows that choice can occur without a complete ranking but does not establish that a particular procedure is morally justified. A theorist may add a normative requirement of completeness. Even then, a complete ordering over allocation options does not automatically rank recipients’ whole lives. The objector needs an additional premise identifying these relations or showing how one determines the other.

### The fairness premise hides the whole-life comparison

Perhaps equal claims can be established only by comparing full life prospects. That may be true under some policies, and this essay does not rule it out. The clinic’s example stipulates equal claims under a specified allocation measure; it does not claim that no evidence about the patients’ lives enters the measure. The relevant distinction is between using some life facts to assess a claim and establishing a complete ordering of each person’s life as a whole. If the fairness defense depends on whole-life rankings, then the example cannot support the stronger independence claim. But the outcome still does not create the ranking by itself; the policy’s independent premise does that work.

### Equal chances treat the lives as equally valuable

Equal chances are probabilities of access under a rule. Equal welfare is a comparison of lives. One can hold the former without asserting the latter. The fair-chance premise in this example recognizes equal claims for one resource under one policy; it does not assert that the complete lives are equal in welfare or interchangeable in all respects. If the claims are unequal, or if numbers, rights, or cumulative disadvantage matter, equal chances may be unfair.

### Without a complete comparison, the result is underdetermined

Sometimes it is. If the accepted reasons leave several options maximal and no tie procedure is defended, the policy has not established what to do. A cycle may likewise leave no maximal option. The clinic resolves its deliberately narrow tie by a separately defended equal-chance procedure. If a reader rejects that principle, the case remains a conditional countermodel rather than a recommendation. Nothing in the thesis promises that every allocation problem has a determinate or justified answer.

## 8. Conclusion

What the draw establishes is that Ada was selected under a procedure for allocating a scarce treatment among claims stipulated to be equal. It does not establish that her life is better than Ben’s, that his is worse, that their lives are equally good, or that they are incomparable. Any of those conclusions would require further premises about the relevant comparison.

The equal-claims lottery is a familiar and contested allocation method. Broome and Vong provide direct discussions of its fairness and limits; Sen provides a close formal account of choice when rankings are incomplete. Those precedents constrain what can be claimed here. The contribution is a limited restatement of a logical distinction, together with a small heuristic for locating the bridge from an allocation reason to a whole-life judgment. I have not shown that the confusion is prevalent or that the heuristic adds a new theory. Nor have I proved that the clinic’s procedure is required or suitable for any real institution.

The defensible demand is modest: say what the allocation compares, defend the reason for priority, and do not treat the selected recipient as proof of a whole-life ranking unless an additional argument supports that conclusion.
