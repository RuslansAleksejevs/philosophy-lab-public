# Repairing Uncertainty Without Inventing Title

A museum cannot establish whether it bought a painting or borrowed it. Its former managers deliberately destroyed the acquisition records to reduce storage costs, violating an obligation to preserve them. They destroyed the records without inspecting them, so their conduct supplies no special evidence that this acquisition was improper. The present trustees were uninvolved. They have disclosed the destruction and exhausted reasonable recovery efforts. A family presents credible but inconclusive evidence that the painting was a loan. The trustees reply that the family cannot produce the documentation required by the museum's return procedure.

Suppose that this procedure's justification depended on an assurance that the museum would preserve and supply the relevant documentation. The family reasonably relied on that assurance. The trustees cannot adequately answer its claim by invoking the obstacle their institution created. Yet they need not believe the family's history. A restriction on demanding proof can survive an innocent change of personnel without delivering the missing information.

Now suppose a second family has an equally credible, incompatible claim. A presumption against the museum might help each family in a separate dispute. It cannot by itself determine which family should receive the painting. Nor is it enough to say that the museum should give the painting to whichever family suffered greater evidential damage. The museum's debt of repair does not establish that this disputed object is available for paying that debt.

This essay examines that conversion problem: when may a remedy owed by an evidence custodian become a reason for allocating an object the custodian possesses but may not own? I argue for three distinctions. A historical claim, a claim to repair, and a present entitlement created by a settlement or adjudication require different grounds. An evidential breach can defeat a custodian's reason for refusing accommodation while supplying neither historical knowledge nor authority to dispose of the object. These distinctions support a constrained institutional consequence: a custodian that disabled an assessment procedure on which claimants were entitled to rely must provide an effective route to assessment that does not recreate the same obstacle. They do not establish that every such institution must surrender every decision-making power.

## 1. What is already established

The separation of remedy from historical certainty is not a discovery. Porat and Stein's theory of evidential damage defends liability for wrongfully impaired evidence. It explicitly distinguishes procedural remedies against a party to the underlying dispute from a separate tort remedy where a stranger destroys the evidence (1999, introduction, p. 7). Its structure already treats the responsible party's relation to the dispute as relevant to the remedy. Friedman likewise argues that different evidential deficiencies call for different responses (1997, introductory discussion).

A concrete precedent appears in *Smith v. Atkinson*. The Alabama Supreme Court considered an evidence custodian's liability for the destruction of a vehicle needed for a potential products-liability action. Subject to specified conditions, it adopted a rebuttable presumption that the plaintiff would have succeeded in the underlying action. That presumption operated in determining the custodian's liability; it did not thereby impose liability on the manufacturer. The court distinguished the remedy from those available when the party to the underlying dispute destroys evidence (2000, §III(C), pp. 434–436; §IV, p. 438). The opinion is relevant here as an existing structure of reasoning, irrespective of its subsequent legal treatment.

Neither source needs to be read as requiring belief in whatever a claimant asserts. A practical presumption can guide a decision within a specified relation. The further problem arises when the custodian is also a claimant to, and controller of, a single disputed object. Awarding money against a separate custodian can leave an underlying dispute unresolved. Transferring the object itself necessarily alters some claimant's prospects of obtaining it. A theory needs to explain that additional effect.

Personal innocence is another background distinction. Rosen's discussion of derivative responsibility relates culpable ignorance to earlier failures to take required precautions (1999, §III). Harman challenges expansive claims that moral ignorance exculpates (2011, §§1–2). The present argument does not settle their disagreement. It stipulates trustees who neither caused nor endorsed the evidential breach and who now investigate properly. They can nonetheless have duties in administering a continuing institution. Reiff explicitly distinguishes institutional and successor obligations from guilt assigned to current members (2024, §§2.4–2.5). What requires explanation here is which institutional constraints persist when the underlying title remains unknown.

## 2. A requirement and the assurance that supports it

Consider an organization that requires customers to use its register as the authoritative record of deposits and repayments. The organization assures them that it will preserve and provide that register, and reasonably induces reliance on this arrangement. It then wrongfully destroys the register. A customer establishes that she deposited money, but whether she was repaid is unclear. The organization insists that she produce the stipulated proof that repayment remains outstanding.

Her objection is stronger than the observation that its misconduct made litigation harder. The assurance of access helped justify requiring her to establish her claim in this particular way. The organization invokes one side of an arrangement while disregarding a connected obligation that made imposing it acceptable. It cannot remove its own performance and assume that the customer's unchanged burden remains justified.

The relevant connection is justificatory, not merely causal. Suppose instead that a historical archive independently owes preservation to the public. Its breach destroys material relevant to a dispute between two people. That can ground a claim against the archive. It does not follow that one person's burden in the underlying dispute has lost its justification. The archive's performance may never have been part of what made that burden acceptable. A preservation duty, a causal loss, and a damaged claim are therefore insufficient, by themselves, for the particular argument developed here.

The narrower principle is:

**Where the justification for an institution's proof requirement depends on an evidential opportunity that it undertook to maintain, its wrongful impairment of that opportunity defeats its entitlement to treat the resulting noncompliance as sufficient for rejection, pending adequate accommodation or an independent justification for applying the requirement.**

The opportunity need not have guaranteed success. A hearing can be owed to somebody whose case would fail. What matters is that the institution made a protected route to assessment part of the justification for imposing a disadvantageous requirement. Breaching that assurance changes what can justify its response; it need not change the probability that the customer's story is true.

There are determinate applications. A token hearing followed by rejection solely because the destroyed register is absent supplies no accommodation. It simply restates the obstacle. Access to an independently reliable duplicate at the institution's expense normally does supply an adequate substitute: the promised opportunity has effectively been restored. Where no duplicate exists, a procedure that considers alternative evidence and openly assesses the distinct loss of the promised opportunity can be adequate even if it ultimately rejects the underlying claim. Its adequacy depends on offering a real response to the impairment, rather than silently returning the whole loss to the claimant.

Accommodation need not always alter the ultimate standard for historical fact-finding. A separate award for the destroyed opportunity may sometimes be appropriate. But merely announcing such an award is insufficient where the institution continues to impose an unjustified documentation requirement and retains a contested object by exploiting it. One must ask whether the proposed remedy responds to the actual dependency. Paying a small sum for inconvenience while leaving the disabled procedure as the only route to a painting does not restore that route or justify dispensing with it.

Wrongfulness matters through the terms of the assurance. If the institution guarantees access, an unavoidable fire may leave it owing a substitute too. If it promises reasonable care and fairly allocates residual accident risk, appropriate precautions may distinguish a fire from negligent destruction. Fault identifies a failure outside the accepted allocation of risk. It is not an additional source of information about the deposit or the painting.

## 3. What successors inherit

Replacing every employee responsible for the destruction does not restore the customer's opportunity. Nor does it change the institutional arrangement in which the organization imposed its requirement. If replacement alone restored the right to reject the claim on that basis, the institution could recover a lost justification without remedying what defeated it.

The restriction survives for two connected reasons. The assurance ran to the customer: its point was to protect her practical opportunity, rather than merely to regulate a clerk's conduct. And the successor continues to exercise the institutional power supported by that assurance. She cannot rely on the arrangement's authority while treating the obligation that qualified it as somebody else's private moral history. This is ordinary continuity of an office's responsibilities, not transmission of personal guilt.

The argument is strongest for a continuing institution whose administrators change. It does not supply a general account of obligations across corporate dissolution, state succession, or remote ancestry. Those cases require further arguments about continuity. The narrow conclusion already matters: conscientious present officers may lack an adequate institutional reason for a refusal even when their factual uncertainty and personal innocence are beyond criticism.

That conclusion concerns a reason, not the whole of their authority. Competent trustees might repair the procedure themselves. They might disclose the breach, admit alternative evidence, give a fair hearing, explain the distinct questions of ownership and repair, and provide accessible review. If that process is adequate, institutional affiliation alone does not invalidate it. Losing a particular justification for closure is not identical to losing the power to reach a justified decision.

It is also essential to distinguish possession, negotiation, and adjudication. Possessing a painting gives the museum practical control. It can refuse a requested voluntary transfer. Neither fact automatically gives it legitimate final jurisdiction over another person's title. Where a court already offers effective adjudication, the museum need not newly surrender jurisdiction it never possessed. Where its own procedure is the only practically available route, however, restoring access to assessment can require changing its control over that route. The additional argument for that consequence appears below.

## 4. Two claimants and three kinds of standing

Call the two families Alder and Birch. Assume three serious historical possibilities: the museum acquired valid title, borrowed the painting from Alder, or borrowed it from Birch. Neither family participated in the destruction. Their surviving evidence is equally strong; their protected opportunities were equally impaired; their relevant interests and position within the procedure are alike. There has been no later settlement or authoritative transfer.

A serious defender of burden shifting can grant all this without accepting inconsistent histories. She can propose a practical presumption for each family in its relation to the museum, followed by a joint proceeding to settle the conflict. There is no contradiction in that proposal. It does, however, leave the essential allocation question open.

The result in this symmetric case is modest but precise: an equally applicable reason to repair Alder and Birch cannot, by itself, select Alder over Birch. Interchanging the families' names preserves every specified ground of the decision. A rule selecting whichever family appears first would need a justification for the priority of arrival; the museum's equal breach supplies none. A fair lottery might be justified, but its justification would concern allocating prospects under symmetry, not reconstructing the missing history.

To see what further reasoning is needed, distinguish three kinds of standing. **Historical standing** concerns the evidence that a family owned the painting before its transfer to the museum. **Remedial standing** concerns what the family is now owed because its evidential opportunity was impaired. **Present title** concerns an entitlement currently governing possession and exclusion, including one newly created by an authorized settlement or adjudication. These can diverge.

Alder might have a substantial claim to repair even if Birch was historically the owner. Both families might voluntarily agree that a lottery will determine future title. If Alder wins under a valid agreement, Alder can acquire present title that excludes Birch, without thereby proving that Alder owned the painting before. An accurate description must distinguish a constitutive decision—one that creates an entitlement—from an evidential finding about an earlier entitlement.

This distinction prevents an overbroad prohibition. A reparative decision can properly affect an innocent person's present rights. What it needs is an account of the authority for that effect. The museum's responsibility for lost evidence does not supply such an account merely because the desired remedy involves an object in its possession.

## 5. Unequal damage does not end the problem

Change the case. The museum destroyed Alder's uniquely useful archive in breach of a specific assurance, while Birch lost only a largely redundant fragment. Their surviving historical claims are still equally credible. Alder plausibly has a stronger claim against the museum for evidential repair. May that stronger claim justify giving Alder priority to the painting?

It may supply a relevant consideration, but a step is missing. A duty to compensate Alder does not establish that every object the museum controls is available for satisfying that duty. If the museum knowingly held a violin belonging to an unrelated person, its debt to Alder would not authorize giving her that violin. The owner's innocence would not be the only point. The object would lie outside the museum's authority to use as payment.

In the painting case, availability is uncertain rather than known to be absent. That uncertainty does not disappear because the debt to Alder is clearer. We now know more about one relation—the museum's obligation to Alder—without knowing more about another—the museum's authority over an object possibly belonging to Birch. The stronger repair claim does not itself connect the two.

The required condition is this:

**Using a disputed object to discharge an evidential repair obligation requires a justification for making that object available for this remedial purpose; possession and the repair obligation alone do not supply it.**

This is not a general ban on comparative reparative priority. The stronger impairment may justify a larger payment from the museum's undisputed resources. It may justify devoting more investigation to Alder, provided Birch's opportunity to contest ownership is preserved. It may also justify greater weight in an agreed allocation procedure if the families authorize treating their repair claims that way. A defensible public rule could conceivably make those claims relevant to a binding allocation without unanimous agreement. But such a rule must justify why a disputed object's possible owner can be displaced by another person's claim against its custodian. It cannot merely repeat that the custodian owes repair.

The constraint identifies a particular failure of reasoning, rather than excluding every outcome with unequal effects. A stronger remedial claim does not become stronger historical evidence. Neither does it automatically become a claim against an asset that may be another claimant's property. Converting it into allocation priority needs grounds connecting the repair obligation to the asset and grounds authorizing the resulting decision.

One might object that this favors Birch's possible property right over Alder's certain right to repair. But requiring a justification for that conversion does not rank all uncertain property claims above all certain remedial claims. It refuses to treat certainty about a debt as certainty about which resources can satisfy it. A claimant cannot generally improve her claim to a specific asset merely by accumulating unrelated debts against its custodian. Where a special connection makes that improvement appropriate, the connection must be shown. Here the painting is related to the evidential injury, but that causal relation still leaves the competing owner's position to be justified.

The museum also cannot exploit this constraint as a new excuse for inaction. Birch's claim may block an immediate transfer to Alder while leaving intact the museum's duties to both. Additional compensation, costs of assessment, interim preservation, and changes to the obstructive procedure remain available. The inability to repair through one contested asset does not cancel the obligation to repair by permissible means.

## 6. Access to assessment without automatic displacement

Consider a museum that controls the only practically available procedure for assessing return requests. An outside adjudicator is prohibitively costly for these families. The museum's procedure depends on the promised archive and ordinarily rejects undocumented claims. The archive has been wrongfully destroyed. Continuing that policy would convert an assurance of access into an institutionally created dead end.

The duty to provide an effective alternative follows under an additional premise: where an institution undertakes to maintain a person's practical route to assessment, its breach does not discharge that undertaking merely by making the original route impossible. Within reasonable limits, performance must be replaced by an accessible substitute or by an adequately justified response to the lost opportunity. Otherwise an assurance meant to protect access would become least demanding precisely when its breach made access most difficult.

That premise, together with the control of the only effective route, rules out a procedure the museum can defeat simply by reimposing its disabled documentation condition. It can require funding outside assessment or providing enforceable review. It does not necessarily require a wholesale transfer of authority. An internal process can satisfy the obligation if claimants have a real opportunity to present their case, contest how the breach is addressed, and obtain a reasoned disposition under standards that genuinely accommodate it. If the museum can unilaterally nullify every accommodation by returning to its old rule, the offered route is not effective.

The limits are important. Not every allegation triggers expensive proceedings. A claimant must establish an independently credible connection to the acquisition, the relevant assurance and breach, and impairment of the promised route. These requirements cannot themselves demand the very record that was destroyed. They may be supported by surviving correspondence, testimony, undisputed dealings, and evidence identifying an affected class of transactions. Nor does the obligation require endless investigation. Reasonable recovery efforts can end, and a justified procedure can reach practical finality.

Suppose conscientious trustees offer an accessible hearing, consider surviving evidence under appropriate standards, acknowledge Alder's greater evidential injury, make a reasoned award against the museum for that injury, and reject Alder's ownership claim on independently sufficient evidence supporting Birch. That response can be adequate. The museum need not give Alder a painting that the remaining evidence adequately attributes to Birch, and external adjudication is not required merely to condemn the predecessors more visibly.

By contrast, suppose the trustees offer the same hearing but reject Alder solely because the museum cannot produce the lost instrument, while refusing to consider either alternative evidence or the consequences of its broken assurance. A hearing label has changed nothing. The original defect still determines the result. This response fails even if the trustees are individually kind and sincerely uncertain.

What if the historical facts remain irrecoverable and a binding allocation is necessary? A justified procedure can still create a present entitlement. It might select shared access, transfer, or sale under a defensible rule; the appropriate choice depends on the interests, institutional arrangements and available alternatives. The arguments here constrain those choices without uniquely determining one. In particular, a procedure cannot justify preference for Alder solely by an equal breach against both families, or by treating the larger debt owed to Alder as self-executing authority over the painting.

Boult emphasizes that epistemic repair must attend to the relations and resources through which people are heard, rather than treating those resources as neutral (2026, §§2–3). The present application is narrower: an institution must not make its own disabled procedure the conclusive judge of whether an accommodation is necessary. That conclusion depends on the assurance, breach and control specified here. It is not a general consequence of every epistemic wrong.

## 7. Keeping the grounds of a decision intact

These distinctions place a final demand on the description of outcomes. Suppose a valid agreement gives Alder present title after an allocation procedure. The museum should plainly acknowledge the new title. But if the procedure did not establish earlier ownership, it should not announce that the award vindicates Alder's historical account. The entitlement created by a decision is a new fact; it is not evidence that the corresponding old fact obtained.

Equally, if the museum compensates Alder for evidential impairment, that payment must not silently become a finding against Birch in a later proceeding. Its basis was the museum's duty to Alder. A later decision-maker needs to know that basis before giving the payment evidential weight. Where the manner of destruction independently supports an inference about ownership, that evidence should of course be considered. A manager's selective destruction of unfavorable documents differs from the indiscriminate disposal stipulated here. The point is to preserve the distinction between an inference supported by conduct and a remedy justified by responsibility for that conduct.

This requirement is not satisfied by describing every outcome as unresolved. Some questions may be authoritatively settled: who now possesses the object, which payments are due, or what procedure binds the parties. Other questions may remain unanswered. Accurate institutional speech must locate finality at the level actually justified. Otherwise a legitimate decision about present arrangements gains an illegitimate second life as a verdict about the past.

The contribution is therefore a constraint on combining familiar remedies. Institutional duties survive innocent succession; presumptions can operate within particular disputes; procedures can create new entitlements. But these facts do not compose automatically into authority to allocate a disputed object. Where the custodian owes repair to several claimants, one must separately justify the move from liability to priority and from priority to title. Sometimes that justification will be available. Where it is not, the institution must seek other means of repair while admitting the remaining loss. Responsibility for uncertainty can change what an institution owes without telling it what happened or giving it everything it would need to impose a solution.
