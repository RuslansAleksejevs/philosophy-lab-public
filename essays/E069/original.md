# When Old Parts Become a Second Table

A wooden table has been repaired many times. Each worn part was replaced while the rest remained assembled. On Monday the last old part is removed. The table stands in the kitchen, and every piece of its original wood lies in a shed. On Tuesday someone decides what to do with that wood. They might burn it. They might put it back together as a second table.

Can Tuesday's choice determine whether the table in the kitchen on Monday was the table bought years earlier?

A theory of ordinary repair should hesitate before answering yes. Suppose everything through Monday is the same: what happened to the table, what the workers intended, and the practices under which they handled it. If the theory says that this table survived in the history where the old wood is burned, it owes us an explanation for saying otherwise when the wood is later assembled. The mere appearance of a rival does not supply that explanation.

This is a challenge to an account of repair, not a proof that future events cannot matter to identity. I will show exactly which claims conflict, defend a reason to prefer an answer based on the completed replacement process, and examine two coherent ways of resisting that preference. The result is a narrower claim: the exception “unless the old parts are later reassembled” cannot do its work without further support.

## What is at issue

Being the same table means being one particular thing at different times. It does not mean looking the same, serving the same purpose, or containing the same wood. Each of those facts may help us identify a table, but each can also mislead. Two tables can look alike. One table can acquire a new purpose. A pile of wood can contain all the material of a table without presently being a table.

The classic puzzle makes these considerations compete. Hobbes considers a ship whose planks are gradually replaced and another assembled from the removed planks. Both cannot be the original ship if they are two distinct ships. His challenge is to the combination of the claims, rather than merely to the possibility of replacement. See [*De Corpore*, II, XI, excerpt p. 1](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil1020/Hobbes.pdf).

A recent defence of the reconstructed object supplies a direct target. Lundgren argues that complete reconstruction from the old parts preserves the original, whereas gradual replacement can preserve it when those parts are destroyed. He also explicitly considers delayed reconstruction. His explanation treats them as different changes through damage: disassembly and assembly in one case, replacement in the other. See [Lundgren 2025, §§3–5](https://doi.org/10.1111/theo.70013).

The mere introduction of a delay is therefore not an objection he has overlooked. My objection concerns where the histories first differ. A discovery after eighty years might reveal an earlier theft or substitution. That would change our evidence about what happened. But suppose we already know everything relevant about the earlier handling of the table and its parts. We then vary only a later choice. An appeal to discovery no longer explains the different verdicts. Nor does calling both processes forms of damage yet explain why the later use of the wood determines what underwent the earlier damage. That is the point at which I question his explanation.

## Two histories with one past

Make the example precise. No single table part has a special status, such as the irreplaceable surface of a signed artwork. The replacements occur over years. The table remains usable throughout. Removed pieces are labelled and stored, without any settled plan to rebuild them. On Monday, after the last replacement, the workers leave. Through Monday, the two histories are alike in every relevant physical and social detail.

In the first history, a decision on Tuesday leads to the destruction of the stored wood. The kitchen table receives no further repair or alteration. In the second, a decision on Tuesday leads to the wood's careful assembly elsewhere. The kitchen table again receives no further repair or alteration. We ask about the kitchen table on Monday, before either decision. We are not comparing the two tables on Tuesday and asking which deserves a family name.

Three claims now pull apart.

First, this particular table survives through Monday in the history where the stored parts are burned on Tuesday. This is a premise of the argument, not something established by calling the work a repair. It applies the targeted account’s favourable verdict about replacement followed by destruction to this case. Second, assembly of all the old wood in its old arrangement must recover the original table, even while the kitchen table remains standing. Third, changing only events after Monday cannot alter which earlier table was present in the kitchen on Monday.

Suppose the table survived in the history where the old wood is burned. If events after Monday cannot make a difference to its survival through Monday, it also survived through Monday in the reconstruction history. In that history, the claim about reconstruction says that the table assembled on Tuesday is the original too. Add the plain assumption that the kitchen table persists through Tuesday while nothing happens to it. The result is that the two separate tables on Tuesday are one table. That is the conflict.

This argument reveals a choice. We can deny the particular survival verdict, deny that reconstruction always recovers the original, allow future-dependent verdicts, or deny that the kitchen table persists while standing undisturbed. There is no contradiction in choosing one of these exits. The question is which choice has the best support. In particular, nothing here shows that a defender of reconstruction must allow the future to determine the past. They can instead say that the kitchen table failed to survive in both histories.

The last option is especially unattractive. On that account, assembling wood across town destroys the kitchen table and brings a different table into existence exactly where it stood. No change occurs in the kitchen, in its occupants' practices, or in any causal process maintaining the furniture. The alleged destruction does all its work in the account of identity. It needs a reason beyond the desire to reserve the original table for the other site.

## What needs explaining

It is tempting to say that Tuesday cannot affect Monday because causes run forward. That would be too quick. Dependence need not be causal. Whether a Monday meeting was the last meeting of a committee depends on what happens later. No backward cause is involved. A defender of future-dependent identity can make the same reply about the table.

The objection concerns different answers in two possible histories. It does not accuse the defender of saying that an identity was true on Monday and became false on Tuesday. On their view, it was false throughout one history and true throughout the other.

We should therefore ask whether being the original table is relevantly like being the last meeting. The latter description openly selects an event by its place in a sequence. The meeting can take place, be attended, and end before it is settled that no later meeting occurs. None of those facts requires it to have been the last.

With the table, we are trying to explain whether a particular replacement process preserved a thing. An answer based on its whole history says that what happened during that process does not settle which table was there. The answer also depends on what later happened to the removed wood. That is a possible account. Why prefer another?

Consider the reasons for accepting ordinary repair in the first place. The assembled item remains supported and usable. Each replacement is fitted to the structure already there. People continue to handle it as furniture they are maintaining. None of this proves survival, but these are the features that make repair a plausible case of survival despite material change. They concern how the assembled item is treated and sustained during replacement.

All these reasons apply equally in the two histories. So do the facts about removal and storage. If they support a survival verdict in one, a reason to reverse that verdict in the other must explain what the later assembly contributes. It cannot simply repeat that old material is important. The targeted account already allows that the table survives without that material in the destruction history.

A more definite reply says that the old material becomes a restored whole, and that such a whole has priority over continuity through replacement. But that rule is the very claim requiring support. The ordinary example of restoring a disassembled table supports it when no assembled item has continued through replacement. Extending it to the competing case changes the evidence. We should not treat the extension as already established by that ordinary example.

This gives a comparative reason to prefer equal verdicts about Monday. Equal verdicts let the same reasons concerning the same completed events carry the same weight. Unequal verdicts add a priority rule whose relevant application remains contested. This is a methodological advantage within an approach grounded in ordinary repair and restoration. It is not a general proof that all facts about identity must depend only on past events.

There is a related consequence for knowledge, but it should not be exaggerated. On the future-dependent account, even complete information through Monday cannot guarantee survival. That does not rule out ordinary, fallible knowledge. People may reasonably ignore unusual possibilities. The primary issue is what makes the survival claim true, not whether people must wait for certainty before speaking.

Wiggins distinguishes the grounds of identity from evidence for it, while also allowing later practices to refine our conception of an artefact. See [Wiggins 2012, §§4 and 16](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0378.2012.00516.x). His latter point matters here. Past practice may fail to settle this unusual case. I will return to that possibility. The proposed preference is not a ban on social or outside facts. It is a request for a reason why a later event should decide between otherwise equally supported answers about the completed replacement.

## What reconstruction can show

A defender of reconstruction may reply that a table can survive disassembly. If its original parts are stored and later rejoined, surely we can get the table back. Why should concurrent replacement change that?

I agree that disassembly need not destroy a table. But the familiar case and the present case differ. In the familiar case, people take a table apart, preserve its components as the components of an item in storage, and later assemble it. There is no continuing assembled object competing for its history. Our ordinary acceptance of that case does not establish that the same verdict is mandatory when the parts have been removed through years of ordinary repair.

The question is not whether the wood is authentic. It is whether every route by which the wood becomes separated must count as the table's disassembly. Removing a leg from a table and retaining it as a spare does not by itself place the table into storage. After another part is removed, it is still an open question whether the workers have begun moving a table. Putting the old wood together later does not by itself show that they were moving the original table piece by piece. This concerns what their actions accomplished, not only what they intended.

Someone may insist that they were such stages, although no one knew this. That returns us to the original dispute. It offers a description of the completed history, not an independent reason why that history must determine the earlier object.

There is also a practical source of confusion. A museum may prefer the assembled old wood because it carries tool marks, stains and traces of its maker. Those are real reasons to value and exhibit it. A family may prefer the continuously repaired table because meals were eaten around it for fifty years. Those too are real historical connections. Neither preference establishes numerical identity. Calling the preferred item “the original” can express authenticity or inheritance without settling whether it is literally the earlier table.

This distinction allows us to take the old material seriously without giving it unlimited authority. Even if the reconstruction is a new table, it can be the best surviving collection of evidence about the old one. Even if the kitchen table is the old table, it can contain none of that evidence. Survival and evidential value can come apart.

## Two coherent replies

The strongest defender of reconstruction need not accept unequal answers about Monday. They can say that the stored wood already constituted the original table in a disassembled state. This is true in both histories. The kitchen item is a different table in both. Tuesday either destroys the stored original or restores it to use. Nothing about Monday depends on that choice.

This is coherent. It rejects the first premise of the argument. It also means that the defender must distinguish replacement while retaining the parts from replacement while destroying them as they are removed. They may accept ordinary survival in the second case and deny it in the first. The difference is already present during replacement, so the shared-past objection does not touch it.

The remaining question is why retaining every removed part makes the assembled item a new table. Perhaps the manner of storage matters: keeping numbered components as one item is different from keeping miscellaneous spare wood. Perhaps material continuity matters even when nobody treats the stored pieces as a table. These are real proposals about what survives. They need defence, but they are better answers to the present challenge than letting Tuesday’s choice settle Monday’s verdict. My argument leaves this material-priority view open. It does not show that the repaired item must be the original.

A second reply denies that established practice gives a definite answer. People know how to repair tables and how to store disassembled furniture. They may not have settled what to say when all the replaced parts are kept and later assembled. Even complete agreement about past practices would then leave this case unresolved.

I accept that possibility. Fixing past conventions does not guarantee that they cover every new combination of events. If the answer is unresolved in both histories, the argument has no definite survival verdict with which to begin. Later practice might then refine the relevant concept, rather than uncover or alter an already settled answer. Because social practice can matter to artefacts, we should not dismiss this possibility as mere naming.

But this reply also changes the proposed solution. It no longer gives a definite survival verdict from replacement followed by destruction and a definite contrary verdict from reconstruction as though both were already supported by settled ordinary practice. It offers an account of how that practice develops. Such an account would need to explain who settles the case, how the settlement applies to earlier events, and why rebuilding the pieces rather than some other practice produces that result.

Someone might go further and say that a table is its entire history, from making to destruction. Two histories could then agree through Monday yet concern different tables because their later events differ. This also resists the preference I have defended. Still, taking complete histories as the objects does not by itself explain why the path through the old wood has priority. That requires an additional account of which complete history counts as a table. The objection survives as a request for that account, not as a refutation of this conception of objects.

## What the argument leaves open

No claim here says that gradual change always preserves an object. A table might slowly be rebuilt into a furnace. Small steps do not prove survival through the whole process. Anyone defending survival through replacement must still explain what makes the sequence a continued table rather than its gradual destruction.

Nor does the argument decide every case of branching. When a machine is physically divided into two working machines, the division changes its organisation. Facts about both results may matter to survival through that event. Here the question concerns an interval that has ended before reconstruction begins elsewhere.

Finally, shared appearances are insufficient. A secret substitution and a public repair may look alike but involve different purposes and practices. The example holds those details fixed. That isolates the proposed role of the later choice; it does not declare earlier intentions decisive or irrelevant in every case.

The conclusion is conditional but useful. If we accept that this particular replacement preserved the table in the destruction history, and give reconstruction unconditional priority, we must explain why later events make a difference to Monday’s answer. Describing one complete history as repair and the other as disassembly does not yet explain that difference. It states the disputed verdict in the language of processes.

We have several honest alternatives. We can deny survival in both histories, limit the claims of reconstruction, or treat the case as unresolved. We can also defend a theory in which the entire future helps identify the object. What we cannot do is let the reconstruction exception pass as a minor qualification to an otherwise ordinary account of repair. It carries a substantial claim about what makes a thing persist, and that claim needs its own argument.
