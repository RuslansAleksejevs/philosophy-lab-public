# Keeping a Restoration Without Believing It

A museum owns a damaged installation called *Exchange*. Two recorded voices alternate. The equipment that once controlled the interval between them has failed, and the surviving instructions do not say how long the silence should last. A short interval makes the voices sound responsive; a long interval makes each voice sound abandoned. Photographs cannot resolve the issue. Witnesses disagree. Neither a lost score nor a conveniently authoritative artist is available.

The museum chooses the short interval. That choice is reasonable: the work must have some timing to be heard, the decision is reversible, and this version makes a valuable encounter possible. During the following thirty years, audiences form attachments to it. Other artists answer it. Critics develop subtle readings of its hesitant conversational rhythm. Eventually someone proposes the long interval. The museum replies: the familiar rhythm is now integral to the work; replacing it requires a compelling justification.

Perhaps that reply is right. But would the museum also be right to demand new historical evidence before a critic could continue to regard the long interval as an equally credible account of the installation's earlier state?

I argue that it would not. A conservation decision can acquire artistic authority without acquiring retrospective evidential authority. The history generated by a speculative realization may strengthen reasons to preserve that realization while leaving untouched the historical standing of its rejected alternative. This creates a legitimate asymmetry: changing the work may come to require more justification even though questioning the historical attribution does not.

This is not simply a warning against misleading restorations. Assume that the original uncertainty has been perfectly documented and that everyone in the dispute knows it. The problem survives. It concerns what we may infer from a restoration's acknowledged success, not whether its intervention has been concealed.

## 1. A narrower question than authenticity

The view that conservation merely protects an independently fixed material object has already faced substantial criticism. Lisa Giombini treats artworks as social objects whose continuity cannot be reduced to the persistence of their physical components. She connects restoration with interpretation and readability, while including changes of function and context in the discussion of authenticity. Her argument makes room for the thought that *Exchange* can gain, and not merely lose, artistically significant features as its history continues. [Giombini 2018, especially sections 4–6.](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/Lebenswelt/article/download/10364/9731)

Brian Castriota goes further in examining how manifestations and conservation documentation participate in producing the identities they appear to describe. His account challenges the idea that a conservator simply discovers a work's determinate essence from a position outside it. The feedback between representations and subsequent enactments is therefore not a new phenomenon introduced here. [Castriota 2024, especially sections 3 and 5.](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-42357-4_4)

Nor is the duty to record uncertainty new. The London Charter requires computer-based heritage visualizations to disclose factual uncertainty, interpretive decisions, and significant hypothetical dependencies. The Venice Charter combines protection against falsifying artistic or historical evidence with respect for the valid contributions of successive periods. These principles already resist the simple opposition between an untouched past and an illegitimate present. [London Charter, principle 4;](https://www.london-charter.org/documentation.html) [Venice Charter, articles 11–12.](https://www.icomos.org/images/DOCUMENTS/Charters/venice_e.pdf)

My question begins where these insights meet. Suppose an intervention is recognized as interpretive, its uncertainty is disclosed, and the resulting version becomes artistically important. What kind of authority does this development confer? To answer that question, we must distinguish three propositions:

* **Historical:** Before its controller failed, *Exchange* used the short interval.
* **Artistic:** The short interval is important to the work as it is now encountered and understood.
* **Practical:** The museum should retain the short interval in its next exhibition.

None of these propositions is equivalent to either of the others. The historical claim might be true while the short interval has become aesthetically unsuccessful in a new acoustic setting. The current artistic claim might be true even if the historical claim is false. And retaining the interval might be justified by obligations to preserve a later artistic achievement, despite uncertainty about both its origins and its superiority to another version.

The distinctions are elementary. Their joint consequence is less comfortable: a museum can be right to defend a restoration, right to regard it as artistically integral, and wrong to treat a historically dissenting critic as now bearing a heavier evidential burden.

## 2. Two histories that cannot both discover the past

Consider two possible continuations of the same initial situation. They share every surviving fact about the earlier installation, every credible witness report, and every initial artistic reason. The available evidence does not favor either interval.

In the first continuation, the museum chooses the short interval because the replacement controller it already owns can produce it reliably. In the second, it chooses the long interval because a different replacement controller happens to be available. Neither choice is presented as a discovery. Both are acceptable provisional decisions under the circumstances.

Thirty years later, the short version in the first continuation has inspired serious artistic responses. Its rhythm has become a familiar reference point. In the second continuation, the long version has inspired different, equally substantial responses. Both communities now have good reasons to resist casually dismantling the form around which those relationships developed.

It does not follow that all preferences created by repetition deserve protection. The example stipulates considered appreciation and valuable subsequent work, rather than bare familiarity. Such developments can create goods that were absent at the moment of choice. An initially dispensable feature can become the means through which later artists and audiences sustain a worthwhile relationship to a work.

Yet the two histories cannot, simply by diverging in these ways, make opposite earlier timings more probable for people who know how that divergence occurred. The earlier event is held fixed. The different later outcomes arise from controller availability and the subsequent activities it makes possible. In the stipulated case, the resulting traditions do not track which interval the earlier installation actually used.

The point does not depend on requiring evidence to predate a hypothesis. New evidence can obviously arrive later. It depends on the route by which the supposed evidence was produced. We deliberately varied a later practical choice while holding the earlier facts and their evidential traces constant. The fact that each selected interval then became artistically fruitful gives us information about the possibilities of those intervals and their later reception. Without another premise, it does not discriminate between the competing historical claims.

Someone might object that one community's later experience could reveal a hidden fit with the surviving work. That is possible, but it changes the case. It adds an outcome connected to the earlier work in a way that may discriminate between the hypotheses. I examine that possibility below. The conclusion at this point is conditional but exact: where the new reasons arise entirely from the selected realization's subsequent career, their force is prospective or present-directed, not automatically retrospective.

Now notice what happens to burdens of justification. At the initial decision, either interval could have been selected. Thirty years later, replacing the short interval may destroy or obscure something valuable in the first continuation. A person demanding replacement must address that loss. The historical critic, however, proposes no destruction. She says only that the long interval remains equally compatible with what is known about the earlier installation. Requiring her to produce new evidence solely because the short version has become important makes one claim pay for the consequences of another.

The distinction can be expressed without claiming exact equality of probabilities. Whatever relative support the historical hypotheses initially possessed, subsequent value that does not discriminate between them cannot by itself improve the favored hypothesis's evidential position. Genuine discoveries may change that position. Mere selection, followed by the development of a valuable tradition, need not.

## 3. Acquired value must support its own claim

The museum's position need not therefore collapse. It can answer the critic honestly: we do not know that our interval is the earlier one; we nevertheless have reasons to keep it.

That answer may seem like a retreat from conservation into creative production. But the charge assumes what is at issue: that a conservator can care only for properties fixed at a privileged origin. Works sometimes survive through activities that add significance, stabilize previously open details, or change the ways in which they matter. One need not accept every such change as legitimate to accept that some are.

The relevant limit is different. If a later artistic achievement is the reason for keeping a feature, it must be defended as a later achievement. Its defenders cannot borrow the appearance of historical necessity to avoid justifying the change on its own terms.

Why does this matter aesthetically? Because the two defenses protect different achievements. Suppose the earlier installation was an exploration of failed communication, whereas its restored form has become an influential model of hesitant mutual attention. Those can both be artistically serious accomplishments. Treating the latter's success as proof of the former's intention erases the difference between them. It makes the earlier work answer for choices introduced later, while denying the later makers and audiences recognition for what their activities helped produce.

Even where authorship is diffuse and no precise credit allocation is possible, the difference matters to appreciation. An audience can admire how a community transformed an uncertain inheritance. That is a different object of admiration from the artist's supposed foresight in designing every feature of the familiar experience. A historical error can survive beneath completely accurate praise of the present object.

This gives a constraint on the use of authenticity language. When calling a disputed feature authentic, we must be prepared to say whether we mean that it is supported as an earlier feature, that it belongs to a legitimate later life of the work, or that it should be preserved now. We need not choose one meaning for all art. We must avoid allowing a reason that supports one claim to pass silently as support for another.

Material reversibility does not resolve this problem. The controller may still be changed in minutes, while thirty years of valuable relationships make changing it costly. Conversely, impeccable conservation records do not resolve it either. A defender can know that the interval was speculative and still reason that its artistic centrality vindicates the original historical guess. The error lies in that inference.

The distinction also prevents an overly severe response. Recognizing the gap does not require stripping away every uncertain addition. Historical uncertainty is not itself a reason to destroy acquired artistic value. What is required is a willingness to keep the addition without claiming that keeping it confirms its fidelity.

## 4. When realization teaches us something

A strong objection threatens to make this account useless. Reconstructing a work can reveal things we could not learn from examining its fragments. We may need to hear both voices together to discover what their timing permits. If realization is always suspect because a hypothesis helped produce it, conservation loses an important method of inquiry.

But the argument condemns neither experiments nor dependence on hypotheses. It concerns which part of the result does the evidential work.

Suppose a restorer sets the voices thirty seconds apart, then observes that they are thirty seconds apart. The observation confirms successful implementation, not the historical hypothesis. Suppose instead the restorer sets the same interval and discovers that an independently preserved lighting sequence repeatedly switches off halfway through each utterance. If there is independent reason to think the lighting and speech originally formed a coordinated sequence, that result counts against this reconstruction. The model was built using the hypothesis, but the relevant mismatch was not stipulated by it.

The valuable distinction is between a result that merely displays the choice and a result that tests the choice against something with discriminatory force. Three questions help expose the difference. What feature was imposed? What consequence was discovered rather than imposed? And what justifies connecting that consequence to the historical claim?

The last question matters especially in aesthetics. A reconstruction's beauty is sometimes treated as though it certified its fidelity. Yet the inference requires a bridge: some reason to expect the earlier work to possess this kind of beauty, more strongly under this reconstruction than under its rivals. A critic cannot simply assume that an earlier artist must have chosen whichever version a present audience prefers. Works can be awkward, abrasive, unsuccessful, or successful according to unfamiliar standards.

Aesthetic judgment need not be excluded from historical inference. Consider a hypothetical damaged sequence of panels. Two orders are compatible with the surviving mounts. In one order, details distributed across the panels reveal a carefully repeated pattern; in the other, those relationships disappear. Evidence from the artist's independently preserved work might make such a pattern historically significant. Viewing the reconstruction could then enable a genuine discovery. But the grounds include the surviving relations and the independently supported account of the artist's practice. They do not consist simply in the museum having selected this order or audiences having grown attached to it.

Nor must every relevant test be wholly independent of everything the investigator believes. The modest demand is that the hypothesis not obtain its apparent advantage merely because the disputed feature was inserted, privileged in display, and then admired under that privilege. We need a reason why the observed consequence bears differently on the alternatives after accounting for how the observation was generated.

Thus reconstruction may increase historical warrant. It does so by revealing discriminating consequences, not by turning practical authority into evidence. This exception protects experimental learning without opening the inference that the paired histories discredit.

## 5. Does later practice change what the earlier work was?

A more radical objection challenges the separation of historical and artistic claims. If a work's identity is constituted through an ongoing social practice, perhaps a later stabilization genuinely determines which earlier events count as manifestations of the work. There may be no complete, institution-independent essence waiting to be recovered.

The objection is important. My argument does not require such an essence. It does require us to identify the question being answered.

The physical question of how long an earlier controller paused is one clear case. A community may change what counts as an authorized manifestation of *Exchange* today without changing that earlier duration. An interpretation of why the work matters is more open to later development. It can be true that *Exchange* has become an exploration of mutual attention without being true that this was a determinate demand of its first manifestation.

There are harder intermediate cases. Perhaps later practice establishes that an earlier long-interval performance was a rehearsal, not an instance of the completed work. If the relevant artistic category permits such retrospective classifications, the practice may genuinely affect the correct classification. But it still does not show that the earlier event possessed the short interval. A retrospective rule and a historical discovery are different achievements, even when both are legitimate.

The proposed constraint therefore applies to claims presented as discoveries about a temporally specified earlier condition, requirement, or commitment. It does not forbid later reinterpretation, revised classifications, or artistic development. It asks their advocates to own the contribution made by the later practice. Where they insist there was no earlier fact to discover, they should not also present their decision as the successful recovery of that fact.

This does not settle the metaphysics of every artwork. It establishes a point on which quite different metaphysical views can converge. A practice may have the authority to make something count without thereby possessing evidence that it already counted that way. How far its constitutive authority extends is a further question, not an automatic answer to the evidential one.

## 6. Three limits

First, the argument grants no unconditional authority to the existing institution. A speculative restoration may have been imposed coercively, may exclude a living community's relationship to the work, or may rest on aesthetic habits worth abandoning. Acquired value supplies possible reasons for retention, not a guarantee that retention is right. The paired histories show that such reasons can arise; they do not show that every settled practice deserves them.

Second, historical dissent and practical intervention often occur together. A critic may argue for the long interval precisely because she wants it exhibited. The museum can then ask how her proposal would affect the later work. What it cannot do is count an inadequate answer to that practical question as a refutation of her historical claim. Someone can be right about the past and wrong about what to do next.

Third, a museum cannot preserve every alternative equally. The account does not require endless parallel installations or the equal presentation of every imaginable conjecture. Alternatives must earn their standing through the available evidence. The claim concerns an alternative that already had such standing: institutional repetition alone does not revoke it. Whether to spend scarce resources displaying it is a distinct decision, with its own reasons.

These limits make the asymmetry useful rather than merely verbal. They allow a museum to resist damaging changes, refuse implausible reconstructions, and sustain a valuable later version. At the same time, they prevent that power from deciding historical questions by default. A practical settlement need not become an epistemic settlement.

Return to *Exchange*. After thirty years, the short interval may deserve to survive. Its defenders need not apologize for all the later art and attention that make it worth keeping. But the critic who still hears a historically possible long silence has not necessarily failed to supply the evidence now required of her. The evidential situation may not have changed at all. What has changed is the value of what people did while that question remained open.

A responsible defense of conservation can hold both thoughts together: this is worth keeping, and we still do not know whether it was there before.
