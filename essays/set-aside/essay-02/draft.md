# Forgetting Is Not Permission: The Evidential Limits of Binding a Future Self

Mara wants to forget an ordeal. Imagine a procedure that can gradually remove her episodic memories of it. Before starting, she understands the risks and authorizes three sessions. She expects to become frightened between sessions, so she also asks the team not to treat every later refusal as a cancellation. Her instruction nevertheless contains a condition: stop if a session involves the particular humiliating treatment she has expressly forbidden. Some distress is an accepted cost; that treatment is not.

After the first session, Mara refuses the second. She feels that something went wrong but cannot explain what. The team replies that precisely this sort of unsupported refusal was anticipated. The written authorization remains. The trouble is that the first session also removed her memory of how she was treated. No independent record was kept.

The team need not be malicious. It may sincerely believe that nothing prohibited occurred. Nor need Mara have become a different person. Her values, character and plans can remain intact. Nevertheless, something has gone wrong with an appeal to her earlier authority. The procedure has removed the evidence she would need to distinguish an anticipated objection from an objection her earlier instruction expressly protected.

My thesis is a limited one. When the implementation of an advance authorization foreseeably disables a material route for detecting an agreed exception, that implementation cannot also justify treating the absence of a substantiated exception as a reason to continue. Those invoking the authorization must either supply an adequate independent basis for its applicability or suspend reliance on it. Agreement to lose memories does not, by itself, constitute agreement that the resulting ignorance should count against the person who loses them.

This is a claim about the justification of continued action, not a claim that forgetting cancels consent. It leaves open whether any particular form of coercive precommitment is acceptable. Even granting that some are, the permission to disregard specified future refusals has evidential limits.

## What an earlier decision can authorize

An advance decision can do at least three different things. It can authorize an intervention; it can authorize disregard of a certain class of later objections; and it can specify how uncertainty about its continued applicability should be resolved. These powers are easily run together.

Consider an ordinary instruction to a friend: 'Keep my phone until tomorrow, even if I ask for it back tonight, unless my child has an emergency.' This authorizes disregarding requests prompted by impatience. It does not authorize hiding messages from the hospital and then concluding that no emergency has been reported. The latter behavior changes how the exception operates. In effect, it replaces 'unless there is an emergency' with 'unless an emergency becomes provable despite our chosen information barriers.' Those are different instructions.

A person can explicitly choose a crude decision procedure. She might say that her friend should release the phone only when a particular emergency contact calls. Such a rule accepts that some emergencies will go undetected. The point is not that every conditional permission secretly demands perfect information. The point is that acceptance of one limitation does not automatically authorize every further limitation, especially one produced by the way the permission is exercised.

Memory creates a distinctive temptation to slide between these powers. If the person deliberately agreed to lose the information, it can appear that she agreed to whatever decisional disadvantage follows from its absence. Yet a desired psychological effect and a rule governing future authority are different objects of choice. Mara may want not to relive the ordeal while insisting that the team must not humiliate her. Nothing in that combination is contradictory. Whether a feasible procedure can respect it is a separate question.

The device imagined here is speculative. No claim depends on a present ability to erase selected human autobiographical memories so neatly. An ordinary counterpart is available when a person agrees to sedation, disorientation or isolation that impairs later testimony. These cases illustrate a structure of authorization; they do not provide clinical guidance.

## The existing debate and the missing inference

The possibility of rationally revising a precommitment is well established. Ryan Spellecy's defense of Ulysses contracts appeals to plans and rational reconsideration; his discussion assigns a role to evidence that a patient has genuinely reconsidered the plan. Harriet Standing and Rob Lawlor likewise defend a presumption favoring prior wishes in specified circumstances while discussing evidence of changed mind, independent assessment and override clauses. The problem is therefore not simply that earlier decisions must remain defeasible. [Spellecy 2003; Standing and Lawlor 2019.]

Memory ethics also supplies important precedents. Junjie Yang recommends dynamic reassessment and opportunities to withdraw during continuing memory intervention. Przemysław Zawadzki goes further than a generic concern about forgetting: he explains how losing autobiographical material can deprive someone of the resources needed to defend a changed self-understanding against others. Muriel Leuenberger distinguishes the process by which a person changes from the preservation of particular characteristics. Neither memory-supported resistance nor attention to a change's causal history is a new discovery. [Yang 2024; Zawadzki 2023; Leuenberger 2022.]

My proposed addition concerns the use of evidential defaults inside conditional advance authority. A review can be formally independent and repeatedly offered while remaining unable to detect the very exceptions it is supposed to assess. The problem can occur even when nobody resists a changed identity, and even when the person's preferences have not changed. An exception can obtain without the person ever reconsidering her plan: she may still want exactly what she wanted before, including its original limits.

Thus the relevant question is not always whether Mara has produced evidence of a new decision. It may be whether those acting under her unchanged decision have adequate grounds for saying that this action falls within it. Requiring Mara to demonstrate rational reconsideration then asks the wrong question. She need not revise an instruction to be entitled to its proper application.

## Why the burden moves

The argument rests on four steps.

First, a conditional authorization supplies a reason for action only within its scope. An instruction to continue unless prohibited treatment occurs does not become an unconditional instruction merely because discovering the prohibited treatment is difficult.

Second, uncertainty about scope must be evaluated using the evidence actually available and the processes that produced it. Failure to report an exception can support confidence that none occurred when a reasonably reliable reporting route was open. That support diminishes when the route was disabled. The same verbal answer, 'I cannot identify a prohibited event,' can therefore have different evidential significance in different histories.

Third, granting an agent authority to undertake an intervention does not ordinarily grant that agent unilateral authority to expand its scope. If the implementation removes the practical operation of an agreed exception while preserving the power to override refusal, the agent has gained something beyond the original permission. This is so even without an intention to gain it.

Fourth, when an implementer foreseeably controls a material source of uncertainty, the implementer bears a special responsibility to avoid exploiting that uncertainty as a default in its own favor. Otherwise, the less discriminating the implementation becomes, the easier it becomes to invoke the authorization. A procedure that cannot distinguish permitted from prohibited continuations would acquire a practical advantage over one that can. That reverses the relationship between having authority and showing that one has it.

These premises do not prove that prohibited treatment occurred. They remove one purported justification for continuing: Mara has not established that it occurred. The team may possess another adequate basis. It might have reliable observations covering the session, an independent witness, or a monitoring arrangement that would detect the specific condition. The relevant independence is from the disabled evidential route, not merely from the team's institutional affiliation.

Imagine two otherwise similar procedures. In the first, a trusted observer keeps a protected record of whether the expressly prohibited treatment occurs. Mara need not see the record or recover the ordeal in order for the condition to be assessed. In the second, only Mara could have supplied that information, and the intervention predictably removes it before assessment. In both cases she makes the same unsupported refusal. In the first, continuation may still be justified under the advance authorization, if the surviving evidence supports applicability. In the second, her inability to explain the refusal cannot perform that work.

This difference does not show that the first procedure is permissible overall. Nor does it require the second team to establish the absence of every imaginable exception. The burden concerns a material, live question about a condition actually belonging to the authorization. What evidence is adequate depends on the condition, the stakes and reasonable alternatives. A minute uncertainty about a harmless inconvenience and substantial uncertainty about a forbidden invasion need not receive the same response.

The resulting principle has both prospective and retrospective force. Before the procedure, there is a reason to preserve a suitable route for evaluating its limits or to explain honestly that this cannot be done. Afterwards, if the relevant route has been lost, there is a reason to withhold a favorable inference from the resulting silence and to seek another basis before proceeding. The prospective demand follows from a responsibility to make authorized action possible, rather than from an unlimited duty to preserve information.

## Permission to forget and permission to take a risk

The strongest objection is that Mara knowingly accepted the memory loss. Why should she retain a power to resist its foreseeable consequences? People routinely make decisions that close off later knowledge. A traveller who chooses not to bring a camera cannot later demand photographic evidence of every event. An operation can be permissible despite unavoidable uncertainty about its effects.

The answer requires care. Mere foreseeability of a consequence is not the same as authorizing someone else to use that consequence as a rule of decision against you. Mara can accept that she will not remember whether she was humiliated while requiring someone else to monitor the condition. The information may disappear from her consciousness without disappearing from the grounds on which the team's authority is assessed.

Suppose instead that no substitute is available and Mara is explicitly told this. She replies: 'Continue even if we cannot determine whether the condition was breached. I accept that uncertainty, provided there is no positive evidence of a breach.' That is a different directive. Subject to the broader moral limits on what people may authorize, it may settle the evidential question. My argument does not establish that this choice is invalid. It establishes that the earlier, simpler agreement to memory loss did not already settle it.

This concession prevents an inflated interpretation of the principle. There is no absolute right to perfect future contestability being smuggled into every act of self-binding. There is a distinction between waiving memory, retaining a substantive exception, and accepting a rule for handling uncertainty about that exception. A defender of strong precedent autonomy should welcome the distinction: it helps identify what the earlier self actually authorized.

An express uncertainty rule is not a magical cure. Its meaning, informed adoption and permissible scope can themselves be contested. But the rule cannot be supplied retrospectively by the party whose conduct made uncertainty harder to resolve. The point is conceptual and justificatory before it is procedural: different permissions must not be treated as interchangeable.

## Must any missing evidence stop the process?

A second objection charges that the account makes precommitment too fragile. Memory is always incomplete. People authorize action because they expect to become less able to assess it. If any loss of evidence defeats reliance on an earlier decision, much useful planning becomes impossible.

The objection would defeat a rule requiring complete retention of every possible reason for refusal. It does not defeat the narrower principle. The question is whether a material route for testing an agreed limit has been disabled, and whether adequate independent grounds remain. A self-binding arrangement can deliberately reduce a person's practical ability to act on a temptation while preserving the means to detect danger. The point of precommitment does not generally require making those two functions fail together.

Nor does naturally occurring amnesia make missing reports reliable. The epistemic point applies whatever caused the loss. What foreseeable control adds is a special duty in designing and defending the arrangement. If a storm destroys a record, the fact that nobody can now substantiate a breach still acquires no evidential strength from that destruction. Yet a team that chose a record-destroying procedure bears a responsibility to have addressed the risk that a team surprised by a storm may not bear. Causal responsibility affects duties; it does not change truth by moral fiat.

Finally, suspending reliance on an advance authorization need not entail stopping all assistance. Other grounds might justify temporary action, including an independently defensible response to an urgent threat. Such action must be defended on those grounds. A defect in one justification is not a demonstration that every alternative action is permissible or that every intervention is forbidden.

This restriction is philosophically important. If every practical difficulty were allowed to repair the defective appeal to consent, conditional authority would become an all-purpose label for whatever an implementer thought best. Keeping justifications separate makes it possible to admit both that continued help might be warranted and that the patient's directive does not presently warrant this use of it.

## Is this only an ordinary rule about destroyed evidence?

A third objection is that the argument simply imports familiar distrust of evidence destruction into an unusual setting. If a custodian destroys a document, of course the custodian cannot benefit from its absence. Has anything distinctive been learned about a future self?

The general insight is indeed familiar, and no claim of novelty should rest on renaming it. But the memory case exposes two obstacles to its application. First, the person disadvantaged by the loss may have requested that very loss. Second, the practice is designed to give some future objections less authority. These features can make an otherwise suspicious inference appear to follow from respect for autonomy. 'You chose to forget' and 'you told us to disregard later objections' combine into an apparently powerful defense.

The argument shows why that combination is insufficient. Neither premise identifies which uncertainties were accepted or establishes that the present situation falls inside the authorized class. More surprisingly, contesting continuation need not represent the later self's victory over the earlier one. A surviving check on exceptions can be necessary to preserve the earlier decision's authority. The familiar conflict between past and present selves therefore misses a third possibility: an implementer can misapply the past self's unchanged decision by making its limits impossible to establish.

The contribution is correspondingly modest. It is a specification of how two permissions interact, with consequences for a prominent evidential default in self-binding. It neither supplies a comprehensive account of personal identity nor displaces the existing concerns about autonomy and social power. Its value lies in isolating an error that those broad concerns can leave hidden, particularly when the enforcer is benevolent and the person's identity is stable.

## What must survive

We sometimes describe memory as what allows a person to remain herself. The case considered here supports a less demanding and more practical thought. Particular pieces of autobiographical information can determine whether others are entitled to act under the authority of an earlier choice. Their importance does not depend on their being essential to identity.

What must survive is therefore not necessarily the memory itself. A limited record, a trustworthy witness, or an appropriately chosen decision rule may preserve the distinction that matters while allowing a person to stop reliving the experience. Conversely, preserving a beautifully detailed record of Mara's original consent will not help if all evidence bearing on its stopping condition has been removed. More evidence of the grant is not a substitute for evidence of its applicability.

A person can ask for help against a future temptation. She can also ask to forget. Neither request, alone or together, authorizes the helper to treat ignorance produced by the help as proof that its limits have not been reached. An earlier self's authority deserves neither automatic defeat nor automatic extension. It deserves accurate application, including to the circumstances in which that self said to stop.
