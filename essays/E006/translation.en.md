# The Duty of the Only Person

*English reading translation prepared for this repository. The [Russian original](original.md) is the manuscript evaluated at midterm. This translation is a separate editorial artifact.*

On Wednesdays, only one person remains in the world.

On Tuesday there are two: Lea and Mark. Each has a house, a handwriting, a childhood of their own. But at midnight their bodies fold together, like two letters into one envelope. A person wakes up with access to both lives. He does not hear two disputants in his head. He has one field of vision, one hunger, one decision about which cup to take. He can understand at once why Lea fears the sea and Mark loves it, and decide to go to the river.

On Thursday the world opens the envelope again. Two people appear. One inherits Lea's memories, habits, and unfinished business; the other, Mark's. Both remember the shared Wednesday. They remember it from within, as a day they lived, not as a film about a stranger.

The arrangement of this world is implausible. But it must not be vague. On Wednesday there is exactly one experiencing and acting subject; two independent consciousnesses are not secretly hiding inside him. His memories of the two past lives are genuinely causally connected to them. Upon division, both successors receive the shared day, while their earlier lines of character and memory diverge again. These are conditions of the fantasy, not facts about the brain. In calling Thursday's woman Lea's successor, I am not yet claiming that she is numerically the same person. A sufficiently close connection to continue Lea's project and experience the consequences of Wednesday's decisions is enough.

One Tuesday, Lea lent Mark a violin. The instrument was to be returned on Thursday: Lea was going to play at the only concert in the world. Mark agreed. Neither by lending the violin nor by agreeing to another shared Wednesday did Lea authorize its destruction.

On Wednesday the only person took out the violin and thought: “The person it is owed to is me. The person who owes it is also me. So there is no one to return it to.”

He decided to make a small boat out of the violin. In such a world that too is possible, but for our story it is enough that the violin ceased to be a violin.

On Thursday Lea could not play. Mark remembered happily launching the boat. Lea remembered that pleasure too. It did not make the concert happen.

The question is not whether we like violins more than boats. It concerns the only person's argument. Does the fact that there is no one to return an object to on Wednesday really entail that it no longer needs to be returned?

I defend a negative answer: **the unity of the present subject does not by itself turn all the relations he inherits into his private affair**. To cancel an earlier claim, we must know how it arose, whom its cancellation will affect, and what powers have passed to the present person. Counting heads is not enough—even if there really is only one head.

The strange scenario is not wholly new. Parfit already described beings that merge and divide with the seasons. He distinguished numerical identity, psychological continuity, and direct psychological connections. Neither the shift from two to one to two, nor the possibility of continuation without ordinary identity, can therefore be presented as a discovery. My question is narrower: what happens to a claim between people when its parties pass through a single conscious present? [Parfit, “Personal Identity,” especially pp. 18–23](https://www.uvm.edu/~lderosse/courses/metaph/Parfit(1971).pdf).

Let us look at what work the word “I” does in Wednesday's reasoning.

When a person says “I am cold,” it points to the present experiencer. When he says “I took the violin yesterday,” it connects the present speaker with an earlier action. When he adds “therefore I am entitled to dispose of the violin,” a third connection arises: between a person and his authority. In ordinary life all three often coincide. But coincidence is not yet proof that one always entails another.

Locke already distinguished the question of personal identity from that of the same human being or the same substance. Consciousness allowed him to connect remote actions with the present self. Our world makes the cost of such a connection especially visible: consciousness can reach into two pasts, but the relations between their participants need not thereby disappear. [Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, II.xxvii, §§7, 9, 15–17](https://barryfvaughan.org/text/philtext/locke/bk2/27.html).

Lea lent the violin to another person. Mark accepted an object from another person. The shared Wednesday changes neither fact. It adds a new one: a single subject now continues both lines. If we replace “continues” with strict equality, Lea and Mark turn out to have been one and the same already on Tuesday. But on Tuesday, one could hear music while the other slept without dreams. The conditions of the story do not allow there to have been only one experience with two names.

Strict identity can be preserved in another way: regard the complete lives as distinct, while allowing them a shared stretch. David Lewis defended a related possibility by distinguishing the counting of extended persons from the counting of their present stages. We need not accept this entire picture. For what follows, it is enough to recognize that one present center of experience does not establish the number of complete biographies. [Lewis, “Survival and Identity,” available passage on shared stages and counting](https://www.andrewmbailey.com/dkl/Survival_and_Identity.pdf).

This is not a trick for hiding two more occupants inside Wednesday's head. No one argues in there. But the absence of two disputants now does not remove the distinction between the two histories from which the violin arrangement arose, or between the two futures on which the consequences will fall.

Otherwise, temporary merger could serve as a way to evade earlier promises. Mark promises to return an object. Then comes a single shared present. Afterward, a person closely continuing Mark's affairs and a person closely continuing Lea's appear. Everything that made return important has survived. Yet the obligation has supposedly disappeared merely because two streams of experience met between the loan and the return.

The story alone does not yet prove that the debt survived. It shows what explanation is missing: why should the meeting of streams of experience have the power to terminate it?

“Because they became one person” repeats precisely what needs justification. For temporary merger to extinguish a claim, an additional principle is needed: for example, that every common successor automatically receives unlimited authority to waive any claim of his predecessors. This principle is not a law of identity. It distributes power. It can be accepted or rejected, and it must be defended separately.

A strong objection arises here. Lea could have changed her own mind. She could have decided that the boat was more beautiful than the concert and released Mark from his debt. Why can Wednesday's person, who remembers her life from within and understands her interests, not do the same? Does prohibiting him from waiving the claim not forever subordinate a living person to the will of his earlier states?

This objection prevents an overly broad conclusion. We cannot declare every inherited claim eternal. Sometimes the power to waive it really does pass to a successor. Sometimes such a power can reasonably be presumed. Wednesday's person can make a meaningful decision, rather than merely executing Tuesday's wills.

But this still does not justify his original argument.

Compare two stories. In the first, Lea authorized the common successor in advance to decide the violin's fate, including its destruction. In the second, she authorized Mark only to use it until Thursday; temporary merger was an ordinary natural occurrence and expressed no consent to extend that permission. In both stories, one person exists on Wednesday. In the first, he has an explicit basis for disposing of the violin. In the second, he lacks that basis, although one might try to establish it through another argument.

The explicitly given grounds for disposing of the violin differ between these stories. This does not yet prove a difference in actual authority: perhaps some general rule of merger transfers it in the second story. But then that additional rule is doing the work, not merely the answer to “How many subjects are there now?”

One can object more forcefully: no permission from Lea is needed at all. By its very nature, merger transfers all power over her claims to the present person.

But “by its very nature” needs explanation here. Direct memory gives access to Lea's reasons, not an obvious power to cancel her claim. Continuing her project does not by itself establish the extent of that power either.

We must distinguish three cases. The debt might disappear automatically at merger. It might survive, but then end through a valid waiver by someone who acquired the power to release it. Finally, such a waiver might be valid but morally unjustified because of its treatment of future successors. These claims require different arguments. The further story about Lea's losses helps raise the third question, but does not itself settle the first two.

My conclusion is therefore not that making the boat is prohibited under every imaginable theory, or that the debt necessarily survived. It is this: neither automatic termination of the debt nor the authority to waive it has been established merely by the disappearance of the second person from the present. If the authority is nevertheless justified, the moral assessment of the waiver remains a separate question.

This may seem a modest correction to an unusual IOU. Behind it, however, lies a familiar conception of the boundary between personal choice and deciding for others.

We often assume that where there is one subject, there is one set of interests over which he is entitled to regard himself as master. He can weigh his own desires and choose. An interpersonal problem appears when someone else stands beside him and says, “What about my loss?”

On Wednesday, no one will stand beside him. Yet the decision already has a distribution of consequences. On Thursday, Lea's successor will lack the instrument for a project that matters to her. Mark's successor will retain what he borrowed the violin for on Tuesday, and the shared memory of the boat. He will not lose Lea's concert as his own unfinished project. He may sympathize with Lea; that is a different way of being affected.

The claim to have the violin returned connected precisely these different positions. It was not simply a wish voiced by some current speaker in a head. The disappearance of a separate voice therefore cannot be taken as fulfillment of the claim. Whether it was terminated in another way still needs to be established.

The thought that shared experience has already reconciled everyone is especially tempting. Lea herself remembers the pleasure of the boat on Thursday. So, we may be told, she received what she wanted: she knows from within how she wanted the boat.

But memory of approval is not unlimited authorization. Under the story's conditions, Lea also remembers that the shared decision took two pasts and two futures into account. It could have preferred one to the other. Being able to remember that choice from within does not establish that it distributed losses fairly. Lea retains grounds to criticize their distribution, even if the waiver was valid. But the reverse matters too: her loss does not by itself prove the waiver invalid.

Moreover, the interests of future successors need not coincide with the present sum of desires. The common person may like silence. After division, Lea's love of music will return. We know this in advance from the world's structure. The present absence of a desire for the concert then does not mean no one needs the concert anymore. It means that the person who will need it has no separate place from which to object right now.

Here lies the dangerous leap: from the unity of experience to a monopoly on justification.

There may be only one decision-maker. All the reasons available to him may be in one consciousness. But those reasons can still require attention to different lines of life. There need be no parliament sitting inside him. It is enough that there are different future losses and earlier relations through which one party gained the opportunity to determine another's fate.

Does this lead to the absurd conclusion that any forgetting creates new people and turns an ordinary decision to give up a violin into a violation of one's childhood's rights?

No. I am not using memory as a universal counter of persons. I am not proposing that a forgotten year be counted as a dead person. In the story, the distinction between the predecessors is established before merger: they acted separately, had different available opportunities, and entered into a mutual arrangement. The distinction between the future successors is stipulated too: they will act separately and bear different consequences. The argument uses these distinctions; it does not derive them from the number of memories.

In an ordinary person's life, earlier commitments to oneself and the interests of one's future self can also constrain decisions. Merger does not reveal an exceptional, previously unknown power of the past. It lets us test a more precise conclusion: this whole problem cannot be regarded as exclusively personal on the basis of one present consciousness. Otherwise, without separate justification, we would declare terminated even a claim that undoubtedly arose between different people.

There is another boundary. Suppose there will be no Thursday. Merger is final; no separate future successors will arise. Then the argument about the distribution of Thursday's losses does not work. Perhaps the shared life really will absorb the earlier conflict. Perhaps obligations connected with the past will remain. That will require a separate argument. The temporary character of our Wednesday matters: it connects different lives without ending every possibility of their separate continuation.

Now enlarge the world. Let billions of people inhabit it on Tuesdays, and let all of them converge into one on Wednesdays. The streets are empty; telephone books become thinner than a single name. The only person reads all the letters at once, because he remembers writing and receiving them. For tomorrow, he must distribute bread, return borrowed things, keep promises. He can call every future person his successor.

If he says, “There are no others, so I owe no one anything,” the mistake is no smaller because “there are no others” is literally true of present experience. The tense changes unnoticed. There is no other person in the room of the present; from this we infer that there is no other among those whose reasons and losses the decision affects.

This only person might be extraordinarily kind. Shared memory could help him understand everyone. But his kindness would not consist in discovering the absence of others. It would consist in retaining their differences while all of them are accessible only through him.

Perhaps this world's strangest possibility is not a person with two childhoods. It is a person for whom calling all the life available to him by the single word “I” is insufficient to justify a decision.

Lea and Mark could leave a short note for Wednesday on the violin case: “The fact that you are alone now does not yet explain why you need not return it.”

The note contains no secret soul that survived merger. It preserves the difference between those who trusted, those who gained the ability to act, and those who will meet Thursday. One consciousness is enough to read that difference. The mere fact of its unity is not enough to justify abolishing it.
