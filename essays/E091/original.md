# Permission to Find the Objection

A workshop can offer excellent tools for correcting mistakes while making certain mistakes practically undiscoverable. Consider an automated fabrication service that will investigate any demonstrated defect in its standard designs but permits exploratory prototypes only after a defect has been demonstrated. The service might respond conscientiously to every admitted complaint. Yet a maker who needs a prototype to discover what is wrong cannot obtain one through that procedure. The finding needed to enter the inquiry is the finding the inquiry is meant to produce.

This essay defends a limited objection to that arrangement. When an institution assigns someone a role in assessing the adequacy of a design, and controls an otherwise feasible means of doing so, it has a defeasible reason not to make access depend on already possessing the relevant finding. It may demand a reason to investigate. It should not ordinarily substitute a demand for the investigation's result. This distinction gives practical content to the familiar requirement that human control and contestability must be effective rather than nominal.

The argument applies both to discovering a failure under accepted standards and to reconsidering the standards themselves. The second case is especially revealing because an admission procedure can protect an existing specification from examination while leaving its users formally entitled to recommend changes. Skilled making exposes this possibility without requiring a story about wrongdoing, deception or catastrophic harm. The artifact can be a means of discovering what the work should achieve.

## What the prototype can change

Consider a hypothetical restoration project. An owner wants an old table usable again. The agreed initial brief gives preservation of original material priority over convenient future repair, provided the table is safe and serviceable. The craftsperson may propose changes to the brief, but the owner must approve them. This allocation distinguishes three powers: initiating an inquiry, recommending a revision and deciding whether to adopt it. They need not belong to the same person.

An automated workshop can fabricate replacement joints and prototypes. Its standard joints reliably meet the selected specifications. The craftsperson initially favors a joint that preserves nearly all the old wood but requires specialized tools for disassembly. A second design removes slightly more material and allows ordinary maintenance. Both are safe and serviceable. According to the operative brief, the first wins.

The craftsperson requests models that the owner can assemble, clean and take apart. Handling them may make the owner appreciate a consideration that an abstract description left obscure: being able to maintain the table as part of domestic life can itself be a way of preserving its place in the family. The owner can then reasonably revise the initial priority, accepting a small loss of old material to retain that practice. This is a possible response, not an empirical prediction or a claim that convenience ought always to prevail. The owner could instead reaffirm the original brief.

Contrast this with discovering that a proposed release motion tears fragile surrounding wood, contrary to the agreed preservation requirement. That discovery identifies a failure under an accepted standard. In the former case, the prototypes inform a reconsideration of the relative importance of the project's goals. Call these execution correction and specification correction respectively. Neither is inherently superior, and the same experiment can support both.

Someone might redescribe the owner's initial purpose as “preserve the table's overall value, whatever that ultimately means.” On this description the second case also applies an unchanged, more comprehensive standard. Nothing here requires denying that description. Institutions must work with operative standards: the priorities and descriptions that actually guide instructions, permissions and evaluation. A wholly inclusive purpose does not tell a workshop which trials to authorize. If material preservation is the operative admission criterion, changing its priority matters even if the change can retrospectively be reconciled with a broader commitment.

Now suppose the workshop authorizes prototypes only when the applicant demonstrates that a standard design violates the current brief. The second inquiry cannot qualify: both designs satisfy the brief, and its current priority selects the first. The maker is permitted to recommend a revised brief, but denied the available means of helping the owner assess whether to revise it. The rule does more than allocate scarce machinery. It makes the current specification the test of whether the specification may be explored.

This example does not show that physical handling is always necessary. A drawing, conversation or demonstration may suffice. Its force is conditional: where a proportionate exploratory procedure supplies an otherwise unavailable opportunity to assess a relevant uncertainty, an admission rule should be evaluated in relation to that epistemic function. If a cheaper substitute does the work, refusing the expensive prototype need not obstruct inquiry.

## Authority and its exercise

The owner retains the right to decide even when no prototype is available. A shortage of resources does not automatically transfer legitimate authority to the workshop's designers. Similarly, the craftsperson can understand the project better than the owner without acquiring the right to change it unilaterally. Decision rights, competence and institutional support are different things.

The relevant concern is effective exercise of an assigned role. The restoration arrangement asks the craftsperson to identify reasons for reconsideration and leaves the owner entitled to assess those reasons. It becomes defective when its own access rules systematically obstruct a feasible route for doing that work. The authority may remain legally and morally intact while its exercise is impaired. It may become fragmented or unrealized; no particular person must acquire what another cannot effectively exercise.

Why should the institution change its rule? First, assigning a person an assessment role presupposes that reasons bearing on the assessment are eligible for consideration. Otherwise the role is restricted to endorsing outcomes fixed elsewhere. Second, some relevant reasons can reasonably become available only through exploratory activity. Third, when the institution controls a feasible route to that activity, it can prevent those reasons from becoming available by conditioning access on their prior possession. Fourth, in the absence of an independent justification for that restriction, maintaining it conflicts with making the assessment role effective.

The conclusion is a presumption against this particular obstacle, not a general entitlement to everything that might improve judgment. Its substantive premise is the second: inquiry sometimes develops the grounds needed for a challenge, rather than merely testing a fully formulated challenge. The third identifies a procedural mechanism by which a formally open arrangement can close that development. A declaration that “all well-supported objections will be considered” cannot repair an access rule that prevents support from being acquired.

This also explains the asymmetry of the rule. The existing specification continues without having to survive the proposed exploration, while a possible rival must supply findings from that exploration before access is granted. There can be good reasons to favor an established design: previous testing, coordination, reliability and switching costs. But a general requirement to prove noncompliance gives more than justified evidential weight to the incumbent. In the specification case it can exclude reconsideration even when the incumbent performs exactly as expected.

The argument is strongest where the institution both assigns the assessment and controls the relevant means. A tool seller need not provide an unlimited research laboratory to every purchaser. An owner may commission only execution of a fixed design. In that case the craftsperson's role is narrower from the outset. The objection arises when an arrangement presents a standard as open to a person's assessment but uses that standard to rule out a pertinent way of assessing it.

## The difference between a question and its answer

The strongest objection is that institutions cannot fund undirected tinkering. A workshop should be able to require an applicant to identify an uncertainty, explain its relevance and propose a plausible, proportionate way to explore it. This does not demand tomorrow's discovery today. It asks why scarce resources should be spent.

That distinction is correct and central. In the restoration case the maker can state: “The owner has not experienced the maintenance difference between these designs; a small model may help determine whether the initial preservation priority still reflects the project's purpose.” This is a reason to inquire even if neither person predicts which design the owner will prefer. Requiring that explanation is different from requiring proof that the presently preferred design is defective.

A useful test asks what would count as a successful inquiry. If a trial that confirms the current design would still answer an important uncertainty, then its admission should not generally require evidence favoring rejection of that design. Otherwise the procedure makes access depend on anticipating a particular result, although the inquiry's value does not depend on reaching it. This test is defeasible: a well-tested design and a remote concern may warrant no further expenditure. The refusal should then rest on the weakness of the uncertainty, the poor method or the cost, rather than treating an absent finding as proof that no inquiry is warranted.

Consider a maker who asks to machine fifty complete alternatives because “something may be wrong.” Rejecting that request can be entirely reasonable. The uncertainty is unlocated, the cost large and the proposed activity poorly connected to a decision. A smaller initial demonstration may become warranted if the maker can identify a particular tension between maintenance and preservation. No fixed experimental budget follows from the argument.

Radically unfamiliar problems can resist even this degree of articulation. The present claim does not guarantee their admission. It establishes a negative constraint on a recurrent reason for refusal: lack of the very finding a feasible inquiry would seek is not, by itself, a sufficient ground to deny inquiry to someone charged with the relevant assessment. A broad policy for supporting unprecedented exploration requires further judgments about resources and institutional purposes.

Nor does permission to explore entail permission to deploy. Prototypes may be simulated, made from substitutes or tested under supervision. An owner can hear a recommendation and reject it. A manufacturer can maintain a safety constraint while opening the relative priorities within that constraint to examination. Preserving inquiry means giving findings an appropriate route to consideration, not granting every investigator final control.

## An implication of existing accounts

Making as inquiry is not a new discovery. Schön distinguishes solving a problem from setting the terms in which it becomes a problem, and describes experiments whose consequences lead practitioners to reconsider their framing (1984, p. 40 and pp. 133–135). Sennett's account of working with resistance likewise includes recasting a problem and adjusting one's approach (2008, chapter 8, pp. 219–222). These observations support the possibility used here; they do not independently establish the proposed admission norm.

The requirement of effective human control also has substantial precedents. Bainbridge shows how automation can remove the practice through which operators maintain abilities needed for intervention (1983, sections 1.1 and 2). Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven analyze control through responsiveness to relevant reasons and appropriately situated human responsibility, rather than continuous manual operation (2018, “Tracking” and “Tracing”). These accounts already undermine any inference from an available switch to effective control.

Contestability research directly includes help in developing challenges. Alfrink and colleagues recommend exploratory manipulation of inputs, support for learning and assistance in formulating arguments, alongside scrutiny of systems and effective review (2023, sections 6.1.3–6.1.5). Veluwenkamp and Buijsman require epistemic access to relevant reasons as well as authority to act; they discuss institutional restrictions, tools that reveal possible grounds of challenge, training and resources (2025, sections 3–4.2). Neither account is confined to people who arrive with completed objections.

The workshop therefore illustrates a possible failure to implement these broad requirements, not a counterexample showing that those theories overlook learning. The narrower contribution is an argument about admission to their supporting procedures. A sandbox can exist yet be accessible only after proof of failure. A review service can offer skilled assistance yet require applicants already to have made the technical case that assistance would develop. Listing the feature does not settle whether its admission conditions enable its intended function.

The distinction between grounds for inquiry and findings from inquiry provides a way to inspect those conditions. It also shows why an appeal procedure can be procedurally attentive once a case is admitted while still insulating operative standards from relevant experience. This is a diagnostic refinement within contestability, with an application to skilled making where liability and wrongful outcomes are absent. Its novelty should not be confused with inventing epistemic support or practical reframing.

## Ease without closure

The account supplies no reason to preserve difficult manual work for its own sake. Automation may expand the very inquiry defended here. A novice who cannot cut a joint can compare machine-produced prototypes; a person unable to manipulate heavy materials can use an accessible simulation. Ease becomes a problem only when the simplification forecloses a relevant path of assessment while the arrangement continues to assign that assessment to the user.

A person can also choose to delegate the assessment. The owner may knowingly commission the workshop's standard restoration and decline further exploration. That is different from accepting a role in reconsidering the brief and being told that the means to do so require first proving the brief inadequate. Delegation need not be discreditable for the distinction to matter.

The closed workshop may even produce the best result on a particular occasion. That possibility limits an argument based solely on product quality, but it does not erase the difference in effective participation. The account concerns a standing opportunity to develop and present relevant reasons. It does not promise that exercising the opportunity improves every artifact or overrides every competing value.

The appropriate question for a tool or institution is thus more specific than whether users can object. What must they already know to obtain the means of investigating? Can they justify an inquiry by its relevance and likely informativeness, or must they already possess its finding? Where the latter demand governs, authority can remain on paper while its exercise is obstructed. A system that makes good work easier should not make discovering what good work requires inadmissible.
