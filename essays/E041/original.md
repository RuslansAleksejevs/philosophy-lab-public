# What Foreknowledge Can Take Away

A reader learns a novel’s ending before opening it. She subsequently enjoys the novel more than she would have enjoyed an uninformed reading: knowing the destination lets her appreciate the construction. Yet she regrets learning it early. This need not be an error about how much pleasure she received. She may have lost a valuable way of arriving at her understanding, even while gaining a better experience overall.

The distinction matters beyond spoiler etiquette. If aesthetic value resides partly in what appreciators do, the order in which information reaches them can determine which valuable activities remain possible. An artwork can remain intact while one of its aesthetic opportunities disappears for a particular person. Conversely, the disappearance of that opportunity need not diminish the value of the person’s subsequent encounter on balance.

I defend a limited claim: some works offer an aesthetically valuable opportunity to discover, through their unfolding form, how one’s own initially reasonable estimate of a fictional situation went wrong. Foreknowledge can eliminate that opportunity. Its loss is distinct both from having information imposed without consent and from enjoying the work less. Recognizing it supports a defeasible reason to sequence explanations after initial engagement, but no general prohibition on advance knowledge.

## 1. Two questions about spoilers

Neil Levy distinguishes the effect of foreknowledge from our objection to having it thrust upon us. He proposes that much spoiler aversion concerns epistemic autonomy. He also expressly recognizes twist and puzzle stories as cases where foreknowledge can remove something enjoyable. These qualifications matter: merely announcing that spoilers interfere with discovery would repeat ground already covered. The question here is whether an aesthetically valuable opportunity can be lost independently of both an autonomy violation and a net hedonic loss. This is a question about grounds for regret, rather than a general explanation of the intensity of spoiler aversion. (Levy, section 3 and notes 5–7.)

Consider three distinct judgments. “You had no right to tell me” concerns another person’s conduct. “I would have enjoyed this more without knowing” compares pleasures. “I no longer have the chance to arrive at this understanding in that way” concerns an opportunity. These judgments often coincide, but none simply entails either of the others. A reader can request a plot summary, rationally prefer the informed experience, and still acknowledge something forgone. Another reader can resent an unsolicited summary of a work she has no interest in experiencing. The latter resentment does not by itself establish an aesthetic loss.

To separate the questions, start with an accidental disclosure involving no objectionable agent. A reader opens an electronic book; a software fault momentarily displays the final page. Set aside inconvenience and suppose no one was negligent. Something may still have been lost. Now suppose she intentionally opened that page because, after considering the options, she reasonably wanted a guided reading. The same opportunity may disappear, though she has no complaint against anyone and no reason to reverse her decision. The value of a foregone option does not depend on the choice being wrong.

But this establishes little until we explain what the option is and why it is aesthetically valuable. Almost every event excludes unrepeatable alternatives. Irreversibility is not a source of value by itself.

## 2. A viewer implicated by the form

Imagine a short film with two presentations of the same encounter. Initially, distant shots show a woman standing outside a building, looking at its entrance, checking her watch, and concealing something in her hand. The editing cuts away whenever she begins to move. There is no threatening act and no private glimpse of a criminal plan. Nevertheless, the sequence gives a viewer reasonable grounds to suspect that the film is establishing surveillance. Later, a sustained shot follows her interrupted movements. She holds a letter, repeatedly starts toward the door, and retreats. She is rehearsing how to approach an estranged family member.

The initial shots then return. This time their durations extend beyond the earlier cuts: the apparently decisive watch-check ends in an abandoned approach; the hidden object is the letter she cannot bring herself to deliver. The repeated composition invites the viewer to locate her changed understanding in the precise intervals that editing previously withheld. The film need not imply that the viewer was foolish or morally blameworthy. It shows how an economical sequence of apparently diagnostic details supported one explanation by concealing the continuation that would undermine it.

This construction does more than reveal a family relationship. It puts the viewer’s way of making sense of the images among the film’s subjects. The withheld continuations are not merely background facts now supplied. Their return makes an earlier economy of attention perceptible: a gesture had become evidence of menace because the viewer accepted its edited boundary as an adequate unit of interpretation. The expressive movement from threat to hesitation is joined to a reconsideration of how the film elicited that estimate.

Three attitudes must be distinguished. A viewer can believe that the woman is a relative, imagine her as a possible threat within the sequence, and still find her posture menacing. Knowledge need not eliminate either the imaginative role or the felt aspect. Neither of those is the particular attitude at issue here. The relevant attitude is the viewer’s actual, revisable estimate of what explains the depicted conduct. It may be a confident belief, but it may instead be a serious suspicion or an allocation of confidence among alternatives. Someone who treats estrangement as a real possibility need not have settled on surveillance to discover that she gave the latter too much weight.

Now compare an uninformed viewer whose estimate favors surveillance with an informed viewer who reliably knows the explanation and continues to regard it as settled. Both may find the first sequence menacing, shift toward vulnerability, and appreciate the editing. Only the former can discover that her own estimate of this situation was mistaken. The latter can reconstruct that error without having made it. This is a narrow exclusion. Foreknowledge may leave many other estimates unsettled, and if the viewer doubts the disclosure, the relevant correction can remain possible.

## 3. Why this particular correction can matter

A difference between activities is not yet a difference in value. The argument therefore needs an evaluative premise: when a work successfully makes a manner of interpretation part of its expressive subject, discovering one’s own participation in that manner can be a worthwhile mode of appreciating the work. It need not be indispensable or superior. Its value is to encounter the relation between form and interpretation as something one has enacted and can now recognize, rather than solely as something the work demonstrates about possible viewers.

The film supplies a reason for that premise. Its return to the identical shots makes the viewer’s earlier estimate available for comparison with what the extended intervals now disclose. She does not merely learn a general lesson that appearances can mislead. She recognizes how these cuts made her treat this stillness as deliberate surveillance, and how continuing the same gestures makes hesitation visible. The revelation concerns both the woman’s predicament and the viewer’s previous use of the film’s visual evidence. Because the comparison is organized through duration, repetition, and gesture, the self-correction is an apt response to the film’s construction.

Contrast a crude version that initially displays fabricated evidence of a crime, then retracts it through an unexplained announcement. Being wrong would still be possible, but the viewer’s error would have little to reveal about her uptake of the form. Or keep the original film intact, but suppose a viewer initially suspects surveillance solely because she confused the actor with someone from another film. Correcting that error is not the good just defended. Its source is accidental to the construction. These contrasts identify a basis for valuing the correction that neither bare fallibility nor a pleasant shock provides.

A skeptical reader may concede that correction produces self-knowledge while denying that its value is aesthetic. Some of its value can indeed be epistemic. The aesthetic reason is more particular: the film makes its treatment of hesitancy appreciable by reorganizing the evidential force of the gestures through which the viewer understood it. Recognizing one’s own participation is one way of appreciating the success of that organization. It is not an unrelated personal benefit that happens to follow a screening. A lecture could teach the same general lesson about inference; that would not preserve the expressive encounter with these images and intervals.

This still does not prove that actual self-correction is necessary to appreciate the film fully. It is not. An informed critic may understand and evaluate the construction better than the initially mistaken viewer. Nor does praise for the film’s capacity entail that every realization of that capacity has special value. The premise is instead about a particular mode of appreciative participation whose claim to value is supported by the work’s reflexive construction. A reader who rejects that claim has located the substantive disagreement. Mere irreversibility cannot decide it.

Nguyen’s engagement account is the nearest general precedent: it values active investigation and interpretation rather than simply possession of correct aesthetic judgments. His discussion also allows guidance that stimulates further engagement; assistance is not automatically surrender. The present claim is a restricted application, not a new discovery of either point. It asks what happens when one valuable activity has a precondition—an unsettled or mistaken estimate about a particular matter—that new knowledge can remove. (Nguyen, abstract and author-manuscript discussions of direct autonomy and testimony.)

Greater epistemic caution is therefore not aesthetically disqualifying. A viewer who evenly suspends judgment may enjoy other valuable routes through the film. She need not manufacture error in order to deserve the artwork. Even a viewer whose suspicion is corrected need not experience shame or pride. The good is participation in an expressive reorientation of her own understanding, not earning a reward for being clever or blame for being wrong.

## 4. What informed imagination preserves

Chasid distinguishes knowledge of fictional facts from the imaginative role governed by what a fiction presents as credible at successive stages. His discussion allows informed re-engagement even while arguing that foreknowledge can interfere with its fluency. That distinction blocks an easy inference from knowing the ending to being unable to follow the earlier perspective. (Chasid, abstract and manuscript pp.10–15.)

The informed viewer in the film can accordingly imagine the threatening interpretation, feel its force, and respond to its transformation. Nothing here establishes that she must experience less suspense, absorption, or pleasure. She may also recognize her own susceptibility to the framing: the scene still looks menacing despite what she knows. This is itself a kind of self-discovery. It would be a mistake to describe informed viewing as merely detached observation of someone else’s potential response.

What settled foreknowledge excludes is more specific: discovering that one’s actual estimate of the woman’s situation favored the wrong explanation. The informed viewer may undergo an aspectual change without that correction. If her interpretation nevertheless becomes genuinely unsettled, then she can realize the correction too; the proposed exclusion no longer applies. The thesis is about a condition that knowledge can sustain, not an exceptionless psychological law about what every spoiler does.

The result remains neutral between two accounts of aesthetic value. An experiential account can value the distinctive felt character of recognizing one’s own earlier mistake, regardless of whether that recognition maximizes pleasure. A relational account can additionally value the actual history of responsiveness that the feeling registers. The argument does not establish what would happen if the two encounters were identical in every experiential respect. It requires only that a more pleasurable informed encounter can lack a valuable mode of engagement available without settled foreknowledge. Equal pleasure is not identical experience, and neither entails complete interchangeability.

Nor does the argument show that every attempt to reconstruct uncertainty is unsuccessful. Reconstruction can preserve important parts of the experience. It need not restore the exact activity it represents. Someone can vividly rehearse a decision she never actually faced; the rehearsal’s value does not make it the original act of deciding. Similarly, the informed viewer can appreciate a correction without it being a correction of that estimate of her own. If the first-person mode is valuable for the reasons just given, its absence is a loss of that opportunity, however good the reconstruction.

Forgetting can restore the opportunity. The temporal claim is therefore practical rather than absolute: replay alone normally cannot undo recently acquired knowledge. We ordinarily cannot choose to make a settled explanation genuinely open again. That asymmetry matters once a reason to value the alternative route has been established; it does not establish the reason by itself.

## 5. Realization, replacement, and foreclosure

The fate of an opportunity cannot be read directly from whether knowledge was self-generated. Three possibilities should be separated.

First, a viewer initially suspects surveillance, notices the poorly fitting details, and revises her estimate before the extended shots appear. She has realized the relevant correction through attention to the work, even if she reaches it earlier than the filmmaker expected. Subsequent disclosure confirms and develops that activity.

Second, a viewer infers estrangement immediately and never seriously favors surveillance. She realizes a different good: perceptive discovery of the explanation through the early presentation. Calling that a realization of the correction would be inconsistent, since she corrects no estimate. Her discovery may prevent that particular correction while supplying an equally good or better interpretive activity. The absence of regret is unsurprising. There is a real distinction between losing an option and making a worse choice.

Third, a confident answer occurs to a viewer through a lucky association unrelated to the film’s evidence. She treats it as settled. The correction may be foreclosed without either of those work-responsive activities being realized. This does not establish that her eventual encounter is worse. It shows only that self-generation is no guarantee that the relevant opportunity was used rather than closed.

External assistance cuts across the same categories. A companion who asks the viewer to reconsider where a shot ends can help her realize the correction. A requested explanation can replace it with a valuable informed encounter. An unsolicited answer can foreclose it while also violating the viewer’s wishes. These are different evaluations, not different names for a single wrong. The activity’s actual structure matters more than whether its ingredients arrived from outside.

There is consequently no automatic verdict about artistic defects. If a viewer correctly anticipates the situation, that need not make the film too predictable. If a disclosure removes one opportunity, the work can remain excellent and many other routes remain open. The thing lost is a possible relation between this viewer’s estimate and the unfolding presentation, not an intrinsic property scraped off the film.

## 6. The modest priority of an uncompleted route

What follows for criticism and presentation? Where both informed and uninformed engagement are attractive, an uninformed encounter can normally be followed by informed reconsideration. The reverse sequence cannot normally recover the initial live question. This gives an initial encounter a kind of option-preserving priority.

The qualification “where both are attractive” prevents a false dominance argument. Time and attention are scarce. An unsupported viewer may abandon a difficult work, become confused in an unrewarding way, or simply prefer the richer first encounter that explanation enables. Starting without help can also influence later appreciation adversely. It is false that uninformed-then-informed experience always contains everything obtainable through informed experience alone.

The defensible recommendation is conditional. When an appreciator has reason to value both routes, can realistically take both, and loses little by delaying decisive information, preserving the live interpretive question has an aesthetic justification. A critic can therefore give orientation before giving resolution: direct attention to relevant features, then offer a settled interpretation when the reader chooses it. This is not an instruction to maximize ignorance. Good orientation makes a valuable question tractable; withholding needed context can make it unintelligible.

The same distinction guards against paternalism. Preserving an option is not a mandate to impose it. A reader who knowingly chooses advance explanation may sacrifice a real good for a greater one. Respecting that decision is compatible with describing the sacrifice accurately. Calling every disclosure “spoiling” obscures both the worth of guided engagement and the specificity of the opportunity forgone.

The proposed reason for valuing self-correction concerns more than the truth of the viewer’s final judgment or how pleasant the encounter is. Some works make the viewer’s own revision an apt mode of appreciation, while leaving other excellent modes available. Once that relation becomes unavailable, a later encounter can still be excellent, perhaps better overall. A lost way of appreciating need not make what remains a lesser work—or a lesser appreciation.
