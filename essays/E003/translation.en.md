# Signing Beneath Someone Else's Crime

*English reading translation prepared for this repository. The [Russian original](original.md) is the manuscript evaluated at midterm. This translation is a separate editorial artifact.*

At midnight, yesterday came to visit Vera. It was folded in four. On one crease, her house was still burning down.

The day placed two forms on the table. The first was called “Keep.” The second, “Rewrite.” If Vera signed the second, the fire would disappear from history: the matches would grow damp, the arsonist would leave, the house would remain intact. It was not just the ashes that would disappear. In the history with which the city met the morning, there would have been no fire at all.

Vera signed the first form.

In the morning, the arsonist demanded that both of them be tried.

“Whether there would be a fire depended on me,” he said. “Exactly the same thing depended on her. She chose a world in which I burned down her house. Why, then, am I the only arsonist?”

The judge looked at the two documents. One bore the signature of a person who had ordered a match to be struck. The other bore the signature of a person who had left the match its yesterday's flame. The papers looked remarkably alike.

Before deciding the case, we must explain how yesterday works in this city. Otherwise, the judge could reach any verdict by changing the meaning of a single word.

The city has two temporal orders. Houses, matches, and calendars exist in the first. Revisions of the first succeed one another in the second. Initially, yesterday evening contains a fire. Then the victim is given an opportunity to replace that evening with another. After the replacement, no one lives in a continuing parallel city that is still burning. There is now only one ordinary history—the corrected one. But the truth about the sequence of revisions remains: first the arson was chosen and carried out; then its consequences in ordinary history were undone. The participants remember both stages; this memory does not itself torment them.

Let the machine leave the participants' identities unchanged, destroy no children conceived through chance events after the fire, and bring no hidden disaster. The house returns without effort or payment. In the first version, no one except Vera was harmed. She knows everything relevant, is not in shock, and is not acting under threat. The possibility of revision appeared unexpectedly: the arsonist could not have counted on her decision in advance.

This is the invented mechanics of a world. It lets us test a moral thought, but does not establish that such a machine is possible. In a model with a single unchanging past, one cannot first burn a house and then obtain a different actual content for that same evening. David Lewis specifically distinguishes his one-time model from alternatives with two dimensions of time. Here we have chosen precisely an additional order of revisions. [Lewis, pp. 145–146, 149–150](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/science-fiction/time-travel/1976-lewis.pdf).

Even our machine does not make it the case, without qualification, that the fire never happened anywhere. G. C. Goddu distinguishes changing the past from the much stronger removal of an event from the world's entire temporal extent. Our story does not promise the latter: the initial episode remains in the sequence of revisions. “Rewrite” is more accurate here than “destroy.” [Goddu, §§2–3](https://www.mdpi.com/2409-9287/6/3/71).

This qualification matters. The court is examining the responsibility of people who can choose which history continues, although they cannot make false everything that was ever true in either temporal order.

Still, Vera has greater power than an ordinary fire victim. She can obtain a morning whose past contains no burning of her house. It is no longer enough for the judge to say, “It is too late to prevent anything.” In this city, it is not too late.

I propose rejecting the arsonist's demand. Not because every decision by the victim is beyond reproach. Vera may refuse revision for a bad reason, and in another version of the story her refusal will be a grave wrong. But the ability to repair someone else's action does not provide a ready answer to the question of what her own fault consists in. To blame her, we must show what demand she violated through her decision. We cannot obtain that demand simply by copying the charge against the first person.

This rule seems almost dull. The machine is needed to make it difficult.

The arsonist offers a very tempting way to count responsibility. Look at the morning's history and ask who could have made it different. If he had not struck the match, it would have contained no fire. If Vera had signed the second form, it would likewise have contained no fire. Both understood their possibilities. Both chose freely. Both were necessary for this particular history to remain actual in the morning.

Why not equate them?

Because an outcome's dependence on a decision does not yet tell us what the choice was directed toward or what attitude of one person toward another it expressed. It gives the court an important part of the case, but not the charge itself.

The question addressed to the arsonist is: “Why did you decide that you could dispose of someone else's house against its owner's wishes?” The question addressed to Vera is different: “Why did you not use the opportunity to get your house back?” If the second answer is bad, it is bad as an answer to the second question. The first question cannot be addressed to her on the basis of her signature: the house is hers, and someone else initially appropriated the authority to dispose of it without permission.

One may think Vera has a duty to care for her own welfare. One may consider her refusal pointless, vindictive, or ungrateful toward the gift of repair. Let us even suppose that one of these assessments is right. It still does not answer on the arsonist's behalf. Blame for managing one's own life badly and blame for appropriating authority over another's life can both be serious. That does not make them the same blame.

Peter Strawson proposes looking at the attitude toward people manifested in an action: the same pain can be caused in an attempt to help or in a desire to insult. Moral responses need not equate these cases. I use this thought in a stranger setting: equal power over the final history does not oblige us to ask everyone the same question either. [Strawson, §§III, V](https://www.informationphilosopher.com/solutions/philosophers/strawson/freedom_and_resentment.html).

The judge may object, however, that I am saving Vera through the old distinction between action and inaction. The arsonist did something; she failed to do something. Surely that hardly justified introducing two times.

Let us change the court's arrangement. Now history cannot continue at all without a signature. Vera must press one of two identical buttons. Both require the same movement of a finger. One starts a history with an intact house; the other, a history with a fire. Vera presses the second. She performs an action that chooses which yesterday evening the city will receive.

But bodily movement does not yet determine the moral structure of participation. Pressing a button can allow harm that has already arisen to continue; identical movements do not erase the distinction between causing harm and failing to rescue. The button experiment does not settle that dispute. Its narrower result is this: even an active choice of history does not by itself make Vera a participant in the original attack. If the machine causes harm to someone anew, that very causation may ground a new reproach. It must be examined separately, rather than inferred merely from the similarity between two signatures.

A substitution is easy here: from the fact that someone preferred one of the options offered to them, infer that they authorized others to place them before that choice. Yet choosing how a situation continues does not amount to consenting to its original creation. Consent to surgery after an assault does not give the attacker permission to inflict the wound. We know this without the machine. But time usually helps us: the assault has already happened, and future surgery will not prevent it. The machine removes this convenient support. Vera can obtain a version of the past without an attack on her property, yet her present choice still does not become the permission the arsonist lacked when he acted.

Why? Because permission must figure in the relations within which the action is performed. The arsonist treated Vera as he pleased, without her consent. A later decision can change their relationship: she may forgive him, accept an explanation, even say that she welcomes some consequences of what happened. But these new relations do not turn the absence of permission in the original action into its presence. That would require changing the sequence of the decisions themselves as well, not merely the content of ordinary yesterday.

At the same time, Vera's signature can mean much more than a refusal of restoration. If she says, “You acted rightly; treat others this way in future,” we have grounds for blaming her for endorsement. But those grounds come from the content of her position. They do not follow simply from her pressing the fire button. The fact that the chosen world coincides with the world of the crime does not automatically tell us exactly what the person endorses within it.


This is the boundary of the proposed thought. It gives no immunity to later participants in events. It requires us not to replace an inquiry into their participation with the geometry of available worlds.

In Harry Frankfurt's example, a hidden overseer is ready to intervene if a person chooses an unwanted action, but in fact never intervenes: the person does what the overseer wants on their own. Frankfurt uses this case against the necessity of alternatives for responsibility. We need not accept his entire conclusion. It is enough to learn caution: a map of what a person could have done and an explanation of what they did are different things. Vera really does have alternatives. The dispute concerns what their availability does not yet establish. [Frankfurt, §IV](https://studylib.net/doc/28328469/frankfurt-alternatepossibilitiesmoral-1969).

But so far I have been defending a fire victim who may enjoy living among charred boards. This is too easy a case. Let a children's room appear on the third crease of yesterday.

Now the arsonist has burned Vera's house along with the neighboring house. Several children survived, but will die without immediate help. The machine's design makes Vera the only person who can initiate a revision in which there was no fire and the children are healthy. There is no other way to save them. The correction will cause Vera no harm, require no forgiveness of the arsonist, and destroy no evidence of his initial decision in revision time. The children ask her to press the button. She refuses solely because she wants his crime to remain as terrible as possible.

Now she has something to answer for.

The children have an independent claim upon a person who can easily save them. Their lives do not belong to Vera as a means of strengthening someone else's indictment. The fact that another person created the danger does not make their request empty. Vera can rightly regard herself as a victim of arson while wrongly treating other victims.

Here we should concede to the arsonist everything that really follows from the case. Whether the children die depends on Vera. She knowingly leaves them in mortal danger. Her guilt may be considered no less than his. The proposed distinction does not itself rank the gravity of wrongs.

But they are guilty before the children for different decisions. He made their lives depend on a fire. She made them depend on her own desire to preserve the accusation in its most terrible form. If the button is described as preserving harm already present, Vera should have respected the claim to rescue. If her pressing it causes the children to suffer anew, she also violates the demand not to harm them to strengthen someone else's indictment. This may make her decision graver still; it still creates no original conspiracy. He should not have attacked. Neither answer absorbs the other.

If Vera is guilty, her guilt does not reduce his according to some rule for dividing a pie. If she saves the children, this does not retroactively give him the concern for them that he failed to show. The original ground for blame remains: he decided to attack without permission. But it does not follow that the severity of responsibility or just punishment is unchanging. These may also depend on which consequences ultimately remain. In one case, two independent grounds for blame arise; in the other, one of them does not arise. Distinguishing these grounds does not replace an assessment of the full gravity of what happened.

This matters more than defending the single word “arsonist.” At the perpetrator's request, the court was supposed to make the mere fact that Vera was given an opportunity sufficient grounds for a joint indictment. Then, even before the children's request appeared and before her reasons were discussed, she would already be morally tied to his match. My analysis does not allow this assignment. The connection arises where there is a demand to which she herself must answer. That is why blame is justified in the version with the children; in the version involving only her house, it still needs a different justification.

The distinction also changes what we may demand. Vera must save the children, but it does not follow that she must justify the arsonist. The court can say both “Press the button” and “He had no right to put you before this choice.” The order of revisions allows these two statements to remain true even after the fire disappears from ordinary history.

The strongest objection is now obvious: I have hidden an unchanging past one floor higher. What kind of victory over the accusation is this, if the initial act survives in a second time? In the ordinary world, too, a criminal remains the author of an assault after the victim has been treated. Is the entire city not just an enormous clinic with an unusual calendar?

In one respect, yes. If the task was to prove that guilt survives the unconditional destruction of every event on which it rests, the experiment failed. I have offered no such proof. After removing not only the fire but every fact of choice, every relation between people, and the entire sequence of repairs, the case described would no longer remain. Without a new argument, we cannot relocate the accusation into an entirely different history in which no one chose anything of the kind.

But the comparison with a clinic leaves unanswered the question for which the machine was built. In an ordinary clinic, a patient's decision determines their future condition. Here, Vera's decision determines whether the fire will be part of continuing ordinary history at all. She therefore cannot be justified simply by a lack of control over that matter. She has been given control in the strongest form permitted by the story's conditions. And it is precisely after this concession that we can see that a moral charge still lacks an independent ground.

The novelty of the machine is not the novelty of the conclusion. The distinctions between causation and guilt, repair and forgiveness, an action and its continuation, are long familiar. The proposed experiment brings them together in a specific test: when we are invited to transfer responsibility to someone who could have chosen another history, we must name the demand violated by that person's own decision. The active-button experiment additionally checks whether we have mistaken an absence of movement for an absence of participation, but it does not decide for us whether harm is preserved or caused anew. If all that remains after this is “they could have made the world different,” the charge has not yet been built.

This analysis depends on the people being genuinely distinct, understanding their actions, and not having been accomplices in advance. Change that last condition: before the arson, Vera promised to preserve the fire in history so that she and the arsonist could benefit at a third person's expense. Her promise then figured in the original plan. The demand upon her is already different. She helped create the situation, rather than merely receiving an opportunity to revise it. Such a Vera cannot be defended by the chronology of her final button press alone.

Conversely, the original wrongdoer may use the repair button himself. Repair can then be part of repentance and change how it is reasonable to regard him now. Nothing said here entails an eternal duty to blame someone with the same force as before. A more limited point follows: contributing to repair must be distinguished from trying to enlist the person doing the repairing among the authors of what they repair.

The city's most unpleasant feature was revealed not by the fire. It was revealed by the fact that an evil inflicted could give its victim a new office she could no longer simply dismiss. Vera gained power over yesterday. Along with it, she might acquire a heavy duty toward the children. She asked for neither the power nor the duty. She really did have to make a moral choice.

Thus, “You were harmed, so you are responsible for nothing” would be a lie. But “Everything now depends on you, so you did everything” was a lie as well.

The judge unfolded yesterday. It held enough room for two decisions, even if they converged on one fire in the ordinary calendar. He crossed out the heading “Joint Crime” and ordered two questions to be written.

The first began: “Why did you do this?”

The second: “What should you have done when you gained the power to repair it?”

Once the questions were separated, identical signatures ceased to be identical answers.
