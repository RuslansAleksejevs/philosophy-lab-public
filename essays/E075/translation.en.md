# The Archive That Cannot Be Fully Justified

*English reading translation prepared for this repository. The [Russian original](original.md) is the manuscript evaluated at midterm. This translation is a separate editorial artifact.*

In a city where the dead turn into rain, a repository of unique memories was built. Each glass drop holds someone's final, untold story. You can listen to a drop: it speaks softly, sometimes gets confused, sometimes asks you to come back tomorrow. But counterfeits sound exactly the same. They are produced by a harmless mold that has never lived anyone's life.

There is an infallible instrument. It reveals whether a drop was genuine, and evaporates it in the process. One word remains on the screen: “yes” or “no.” The story disappears. What has been heard can be written down, but a genuine drop retains the capacity to remember something more; neither a recording nor a copy preserves that capacity. These are the fictional conditions. Their purpose is not to offer a physical theory of memory, but to test a requirement: every serious belief must be prepared to undergo an available decisive test.

The keeper does not turn on the instrument. Beside each drop, she writes: “Authenticity not established.” The city auditor sees a contradiction: if she really allows for deception, why preserve the drops? If she preserves them, she must already believe. And if she believes, let her prove it.

The auditor combines three different questions. What is in the drop? How confidently can we judge that? What should be done with it? The answers are related, but they do not coincide. Low confidence in authenticity does not yet mean that a possible memory can be destroyed at little cost. Equally, the cost of destruction does not make authenticity more likely.

Suppose there are grounds for thinking that one drop in ten is genuine. This estimate was reached independently of the keeper's wishes. Preserving all ten takes a shelf and a little time. If it succeeds, it preserves the only continuing access to someone's memory. If it fails, it preserves mold for a while. With these stakes, it is reasonable to protect all ten while considering each of them most likely a counterfeit. There is no need secretly to raise its probability from one in ten to nearly one. The action expresses an attitude toward a possible loss, not only a degree of confidence.

In *The Will to Believe*, William James distinguishes seeking truth from avoiding error; he also shows the significance of a choice that cannot be postponed without loss. A narrower case arises here. A choice of action is unavoidable, but judgment about authenticity can remain open. Keeping a drop and believing it are different decisions. Our archive therefore does not ask for permission to believe without sufficient grounds. It asks for permission not to obtain every available ground at any cost. [Source](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Will_to_Believe_and_Other_Essays_in_Popular_Philosophy/The_Will_to_Believe)

So far this resembles an ordinary choice between knowledge and something valuable: one should not burn a painting to discover the composition of its paint. But the drop adds a difficulty. It is not only value external to knowledge that is destroyed. A source of questions not yet asked disappears. We might have learned what the deceased remembered about his sister, why he feared staircases, or where he went wrong in his own account. The instrument exchanges all this for certainty about a single claim about the past: a memory really was present here.

But a story's origin does not yet guarantee its truth. Genuine memory can be mistaken; the infallible instrument certifies only that it belonged to someone who lived. Nor do new answers become knowledge by themselves. By preserving the drop, we leave open the possibility of comparing them with independent evidence, noticing contradictions, and asking further questions. This is an opportunity for inquiry, not a promised stock of truths. If ways of making such comparisons never become available, the epistemic benefit will be smaller; a more modest reason to protect a possible memory will remain.

The auditor promised to separate knowledge from self-deception. Having obtained ten certain answers, he will leave the city without a single interlocutor. The archive's proven authenticity will be achieved at the very moment when the archive no longer exists.

Of course, the auditor need not test every drop. If their origins are connected on good grounds, testing a few may help assess the rest. Sometimes such a sacrifice is justified. My argument targets the duty to test each drop decisively at any cost; it does not prohibit every destructive test.

It does not follow that memory must prevail over testing. What follows is only that an increase in certainty cannot be assessed apart from the opportunities for knowledge destroyed by the means of obtaining it. The number of definitively settled questions does not measure all epistemic gain. Sometimes the price of settling one question is losing the very possibility of asking others.

Precision matters here. Historical truth is not destroyed: if the drop was genuine, that will remain true after it evaporates. What is destroyed is its living bearer and access to what we have not yet learned. The keeper therefore cannot be justified by the dramatic phrase “testing kills truth.” It kills part of a possible further inquiry. That is the loss the auditor must take into account.

A strong objection comes from the next room. There, the keeper of counterfeit prophecies forbids testing them: what if proof destroys hope? A dictator claims that investigation will destroy trust. A fraudster declares his product too fragile for examination. If we have recognized a right to refuse testing, have we not handed all three a convenient defense?

Not if the right to preserve is separated from the right to declare authentic. Our keeper may not apply a seal of approval, sell the stories as reliable testimony, or cite her refusal as confirmation. She must allow less destructive investigations, disclose the grounds for her assessment, and correct the labels when new evidence arrives. If an investigation is safe, her argument against it disappears. If the danger has been invented by the owner and is unsupported, it cannot serve as an established reason for refusal. In the story, the instrument's destructive effect is stipulated; in a real dispute, it would have to be established separately.

Moreover, the label “authenticity not established” does not make every use of the drops acceptable. If a court is about to convict someone on the basis of their stories, the cost of preserving uncertainty rises sharply. It may become necessary to turn on the instrument; or simply to reject that testimony. Unresolved doubt must limit the power of a story over other people. Protecting a supposed source and giving it authority to determine someone else's fate are, again, different actions.

But why preserve something that is most likely false at all? Because mistaken care and mistakenly accepting something as true have different consequences. A shelf of honestly labeled, questionable drops is not yet a catalogue of knowledge. It is a set of possibilities for which the city has chosen to pay. That decision can be reversed if storage becomes dangerous, a safe test becomes available, or convincing grounds arise for considering all the drops mold. No unlimited duty to preserve everything follows.

The auditor may object one last time: the archive will remain unverified. Yes. This is not a hidden defect of our decision to be eliminated by one final clever argument. It is its openly acknowledged cost. Sometimes doubt has to be preserved along with the thing, rather than treated as a temporary obstacle that reason must necessarily remove.

The keeper returns to the shelf. She does not tell the drop: “I know you are genuine.” She says: “While I do not know, I will not pretend that destruction costs nothing.”

The maturity of doubt reveals itself here not in the force of the blow with which it is prepared to shatter any object. It reveals itself in the ability to keep doubting after deciding to care.
