# Who Chooses the Last Citizens’ Assembly?

## 1. An honest lottery, a selected answer

Imagine a mayor who wants a congestion charge. A citizens’ assembly rejects it. The mayor commissions another assembly on the same question, with the same information. It also rejects the charge. A third approves, and consultation ends. Every panel was honestly recruited by a fair lottery. Its members deliberated freely, and every report is readily available to the public. The mayor now claims that the proposal has passed an independent citizen check.

The charge might be excellent policy. The third panel might offer the best arguments. Neither possibility settles the mayor’s claim. The relevant question is not simply whether citizens independently produced an approving judgment. They did. It is why that judgment, rather than the earlier judgments, receives the standing of the completed consultation.

I defend a limited thesis: **fair recruitment does not by itself justify the additional authority assigned to an approval-selected report. A sponsor claiming independent citizen support must account for the procedure that gives reports their institutional standing, including its continuation and stopping rules.** That procedure might have a separate justification. Its results might remain informative. But neither its evidential meaning nor its checking role can simply be inferred from the fairness of its constituent lotteries.

This is not a general ban on repeated consultation. It grants no permanent veto to a first panel. Its target is an unsupported transfer: from the independence of the people forming a judgment to the independence of the procedure that makes their judgment count.

## 2. Three things a report can establish

We should distinguish three possible claims.

First, a report can be an **authentic judgment**: these participants, after this deliberation, endorsed this conclusion. Selecting the report because it approves does not make its authors insincere or turn them into the mayor’s agents.

Second, it can be **evidence about citizen judgment beyond this group**. This need not mean that the panel speaks for every citizen. A more modest claim concerns how other properly recruited groups might respond under comparable conditions. Even that claim depends on how the displayed group and report were selected.

Third, it can have **institutional standing**: its delivery completes a consultation, triggers a response, satisfies a review requirement, or receives special weight in an official decision. Standing need not include a power to enact policy. An advisory report can still discharge an obligation or support a claim that citizens have checked a proposal.

These claims can come apart. An authentic report need not estimate typical panel judgment accurately. A useful piece of evidence need not have a right to settle a dispute. A validly assigned institutional role need not make its holder statistically representative of opinion.

Lafont’s discussion helps locate the distinction. She rejects an automatic move from a representative sample to citizens’ acceptance of its decisions, yet allows mini-publics to supply independent evidence within wider public debate. Her account therefore challenges both an inflated authority claim and the assumption that refusing decisional authority leaves a panel useless (2017, pp. 90–97). [Lafont, “Can Democracy be Deliberative & Participatory?”](https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/publication/downloads/09_lafont.pdf)

The present argument does not settle the larger disagreement about empowering assemblies. It asks a question that remains even if a single panel’s limited evidential or checking value is granted: does the same value attach, without adjustment, to a report selected because it gives the desired answer?

Nor is recruitment research the appropriate opponent. Flanigan and colleagues define fairness through individuals’ chances of panel membership, subject to representation constraints. That is an important target, not a promise to solve every problem of political authority (2021, “Algorithmic framework”). [Flanigan et al., “Fair algorithms for selecting citizens’ assemblies”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03788-6)

More recent research also considers membership across sequences. Kalaycı and Micha’s temporal model takes the number and sizes of panels as inputs and evaluates individual, panel, and cumulative representation. Thus it would be wrong to say that fairness over time has been ignored. The different issue here is assigning standing to completed reports according to their verdicts (2026, §2). [Kalaycı and Micha, “Temporal Panel Selection in Ongoing Citizens’ Assemblies”](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16194v1)

## 3. What stopping changes—and what it does not

A simple model isolates the difference. Suppose each potential panel independently approves the same proposal with probability p. Hold the question and information fixed. Allow at most n panels, and compare two procedures:

* **Fixed last:** obtain n reports and designate the nth report, whatever it says.
* **First approval:** stop at the first approval; if all n panels reject, designate the last rejection.

The first procedure yields approval with probability p. The second yields approval with probability

**1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.**

The second procedure rejects only when every potential panel rejects. With p = 1/4 and n = 3, its probability of approval is 37/64, or about 0.578, rather than 0.25. The fallback matters: if repeated rejection produces no designated report, approval is certain conditional on a designated report existing. With unlimited independent attempts and constant p greater than zero, an approval eventually occurs with probability one.

These are elementary calculations, not a theory of assemblies. Here p is the probability that a panel approves, not the proportion of individual citizens supporting the policy. Real panels may influence one another, encounter changing evidence, or depend on common events. Perfectly correlated verdicts could eliminate the numerical difference between the procedures. None of the stated probabilities is an empirical estimate.

The model nevertheless supplies a counterexample to an inference: fair recruitment of each panel does not ensure that the final verdict has the distribution of an unselected panel verdict. It need not even be necessary to show unequal seat chances in the final body. If citizens are symmetrically situated and verdict variation is independent of their identities, equal terminal membership chances can coexist with answer-selected verdicts. Membership equality and selection of an answer are different questions.

However, it would be a mistake to conclude that an approval obtained this way is no evidence at all. Consider two hypotheses about the panel process: p = 1/4 and p = 3/4. The following table gives the likelihood of three different observations. The final column divides the likelihood under the higher p by that under the lower p.

| Observation | p = 1/4 | p = 3/4 | Higher/lower likelihood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One unselected panel approves | 1/4 | 3/4 | 3 |
| First-approval procedure obtains approval within three attempts | 37/64 | 63/64 | 63/37 |
| Complete observed sequence is reject, reject, approve | 9/64 | 3/64 | 1/3 |

An approval from the first-approval procedure still favors the higher approval propensity, but less strongly than a single unselected approval. By contrast, the complete rejection–rejection–approval record favors the lower propensity in this likelihood comparison. No prior probabilities are assumed, and the table does not determine anyone’s final beliefs.

For that complete record, the likelihood is (1 − p)²p under both procedures. In the fixed-last procedure all three reports are obtained; in the stopping procedure the first approval arrives at the third attempt. Someone who observes the full sequence and knows the rule need not lose this information merely because the process stopped on approval.

This is important counterevidence. Stopping does not necessarily contaminate every inference from a public record. Nor does correcting for selection necessarily reduce evidential value to zero. What fails is the unadjusted comparison between a selected terminal approval and an approval whose privileged status did not depend on its answer.

A mathematical correction also leaves a different question open. Even an observer who understands the complete record can ask why the institution has designated its approving report as the answer that completes the citizen check. That is a question about standing, not just inference.

## 4. Why priority needs an additional reason

Return to the three actual reports. They share the properties being invoked to support the last report: fair recruitment, adequate information, free deliberation, and authentic authorship. Those properties cannot, by themselves, explain why only the approving report should override or supersede the others.

The underlying requirement is modest: **when conflicting reports share a feature, that shared feature alone cannot justify the comparative priority of one report.** A further difference or a justified rule is needed. This does not require equal weight for all reports. Later reports may correct errors, answer objections, consider new evidence, or occupy a separately authorized review role. Any of these differences could matter. But simply repeating that the third panel was randomly selected does not identify one.

The argument has four steps:

1. A source-based claim to special authority must track the process that produced the report in its claimed role.
2. If the role belongs to a designated report, the process includes both the formation of judgments and the designation of the report that counts.
3. Fair recruitment supports claims about the first operation. It does not settle the justification of the second.
4. Selecting for agreement is a reason to choose an endorsement. It is not, without more, a reason to regard that choice as independent of the sponsor’s preferred answer.

The fourth step concerns a particular claimed independence. The third panel’s members remain independent in forming their views. Yet the procedure giving their report priority is dependent on agreement. The mayor has not controlled what any panel thinks; the mayor has controlled which kind of completed judgment can end the inquiry early.

An independent check need not be independent of every official choice. Someone must choose a question, provide resources, and specify a role. The objection to answer-selected finality is functional, not a demand to remove all political control. If the role is to confront the sponsor with an outside judgment, the sponsor’s approval of that judgment cannot itself explain why it alone fulfills the role. Otherwise the sponsor’s assessment supplies the very priority that the appeal to an outside assessment was meant to support. A separately justified mandate may instead authorize an asymmetric test; that possibility is considered below.

With a finite cap, rejection remains possible at the end. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that the procedure contains no check at all. But the cap does not erase the asymmetry: one approval suffices, whereas one rejection does not. The institution must justify that distribution of settling power. The mere possibility of eventual rejection is not enough to establish that whatever checking warrant belongs to a single panel survives unchanged.

Even without a cap, published rejections can check the mayor politically: they may persuade voters or strengthen public opposition. Citizens do not lose every route of influence just because rejection cannot acquire terminal status. Here settling power means the power to complete the specified consultation, not a binding veto over policy or a monopoly on public influence. An advisory check could be completed by a rejection that officials subsequently answer and override. The disputed shortcut is to treat a later selected approval as sufficient to discharge that checking task without accounting for the earlier judgments.

Perhaps the third report genuinely contains better reasons. Then the mayor can explain why those reasons defeat the earlier objections. That is not an evasion of democratic responsibility; it is a way to exercise it. But the justification is now comparative and substantive. It cannot be replaced by the claim that this report comes from randomly selected citizens, since the contrary reports do too.

This also explains why a good policy does not automatically vindicate the attribution. There are two questions: should the city adopt the charge, and may it describe the selected approval as having supplied a particular independent citizen warrant? A sound answer to the first does not answer the second. Officials can openly defend proceeding against advice without claiming that consultation endorsed them in a stronger sense than the process supports.

The distinction fits within, rather than displaces, Bagg’s broader anti-capture argument. Bagg explicitly separates representativeness from resistance to capture and examines the discretion left to facilitators and institutional designers. He also allows varied oversight mandates and identifies the dangers of biased design and additional veto players (2024, “Two Models of Sortition” and “The Structure of Citizen Oversight”). [Bagg, “Sortition as Anti-Corruption”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12704)

My narrower application concerns control after honest deliberation has finished. The sponsor can leave the formation of every report untouched while controlling the allocation of final status. The general thought that random recruitment cannot cure institutional capture is already available in Bagg. The stopping case specifies one route by which that limitation can arise; it does not establish an independent theory of democratic legitimacy.

### Is transparency enough?

Suppose nobody is deceived. Every report and the stopping rule are public, and citizens understand both. This eliminates concealment, not the need to justify institutional priority. A report can openly be given an unsupported role. If its designation formally discharges an independent-review requirement, citizens’ awareness of how it was chosen does not itself show that the requirement was properly discharged.

But transparency can do more than expose a defect. It can enable valid inference from the complete history, as the model showed. And accurate disclosure can change the claim being made: “we found one approving panel” is not the same assertion as “the final report measures typical considered judgment.” Where the institution makes only the former claim and has no incompatible obligation, this argument supplies no automatic further objection.

The thesis is therefore not that disclosure never helps. It is that disclosure does not, on its own, justify transferring one kind of warrant into another.

## 5. Reopening for a reason

The strongest objection is that democratic inquiry must be allowed to continue. An unwanted answer might reveal a real problem, not merely frustrate an official’s preferences. A first panel could also be mistaken. Why should its report freeze public judgment?

It should not. Consider a rejection that identifies missing evidence about who would bear the congestion charge. Under a publicly defensible protocol, an independent audit addresses that gap and one further panel assesses the proposal with the new evidence. Either answer ends that specified follow-up. The first report remains part of the record and receives a response.

This rule may be asymmetric in practice: rejection identified the gap, so rejection triggered continuation. Yet the later report was not awarded finality because it approved. Its place was justified by a change in the basis of inquiry, and its answer was not known when that place was assigned. An equivalent material gap discovered after approval should also require attention. This is not a demand that every reason has an identical counterpart on the opposite side. It is a demand that the identified evidential defect, rather than disagreement itself, explain the reopening.

Neither independent administration nor the arrival of some new information is sufficient. An independent administrator can mechanically implement an approval-seeking rule. Officials can always commission an additional fact sheet. They need to explain what material problem the new inquiry addresses and why its results deserve their assigned weight. That explanation can be contested; no uniquely neutral expert is assumed.

Nor must sincere motives settle the issue. A mayor may honestly think every rejection reflects a failure to understand good policy. If that belief alone permits an unchanged rerun while approval is immediately treated as adequate understanding, the belief reproduces the disputed asymmetry. What is needed is an argument about the report’s defect, not the sponsor’s confidence in the preferred conclusion.

Finally, completing a particular consultation is not a commitment never to revisit the issue. New circumstances, revised proposals, or later political mandates may justify a new episode. The present argument protects no report against all future reconsideration. It asks what licenses a report’s priority within the episode for which citizen warrant is claimed.

## 6. Searching for an endorsement may be legitimate

A more challenging objection changes the purpose of the exercise. Suppose officials openly seek one deliberatively defensible case for their preferred policy. They do not claim to measure typical citizen support. Earlier objections retain their force, and the officials must answer them. Why should they be forbidden to keep looking?

Nothing in the argument forbids that search. Finding a group of ordinary citizens who can defend a policy might reveal possibilities that experts missed. Its reasoning can contribute to public debate. Honest random recruitment may still distinguish it from a hand-picked group of supporters. What cannot be inferred is that successful search has the evidential meaning of an answer not selected for agreement.

Lafont’s own discussion makes this qualification particularly important. Her defense of mini-publics as independent evidence does not make that evidence decisive; it is meant to stimulate wider examination (2017, pp. 96–97). The stopping argument is my inference about a possible qualification of such evidence, not a claim that Lafont endorses repeated unchanged commissions. [Lafont, “Can Democracy be Deliberative & Participatory?”](https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/publication/downloads/09_lafont.pdf)

There is also a harder possibility: an explicitly authorized asymmetric gate. Imagine an arrangement that leaves policy discretion with elected officials but requires at least one of three fairly recruited panels to find a proposal defensible. If that threshold has an adequate separate justification, first-approval stopping might fulfill it. The arrangement would seek a specified minimum of citizen endorsement, not a representative verdict on the balance of considered opinion.

I have not established that this would be a wise arrangement. Its justification would have to address costs, the allocation of opportunities, and the risks of stacking procedures in favor of change or the status quo. Mere enactment or advance announcement would not settle those questions. The conditional example establishes a limit: asymmetric stopping is not conceptually incompatible with legitimate standing. The standing would come from a justified mandate together with fair recruitment, not from recruitment alone.

A rule can thus legitimately give approval and rejection different effects. What it cannot do is borrow the warrant of a different rule while declining to justify that difference. Calling “first approval wins” transparent, preannounced, or independently administered leaves the central question untouched if these labels are offered instead of that justification.

## 7. Two burdens for institutions claiming citizen warrant

The discussion yields two burdens, not a universal stopping algorithm.

The first is **evidential calibration**. What does this report show, given how it was selected? A complete record can meet this burden much better than a terminal headline. It can support qualified evidence of disagreement or of the existence of a considered endorsement. Numerical adjustment is not always possible, since actual panel probabilities are usually unknown. Accurate qualitative claims are still possible.

The second is **justification of standing**. Why does this report complete the institution’s task, and what happens to earlier contrary judgments? Valid inference from the whole record does not by itself answer that question. Conversely, a justified threshold or review mandate may confer standing without making its output a measure of typical support. The two burdens must not substitute for one another.

Neither burden is a reason to ignore existing process standards. The OECD’s good-practice principles already demand public process design, a commitment to respond or act, participant control over recommendation wording, and process decisions made at arm’s length from commissioning authorities (2020, ch. 5, “Accountability,” “Transparency,” and “Integrity”). The generic demand to evaluate more than membership is therefore not new. [OECD, *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions*](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2020/06/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_11aa2baf/339306da-en.pdf)

Nor is a duty to respond equivalent to a duty to obey. For a concrete institutional distinction, Ireland’s Citizens’ Assembly describes its recommendations as advisory while reporting advance commitments by government and parliament to respond in detail. This is a description of the stated arrangement, not evidence that officials shop for reports or always honor their commitments. [Citizens’ Assembly, “FAQ,” questions on completion and implementation](https://citizensassembly.ie/about/faq/)

Applied to the mayor’s case, the burdens support three practical questions:

* What role was consultation supposed to perform, and what rules—including any cap and fallback—connect completed reports to that role?
* What reason licenses reopening or giving a later report greater weight? Is it a material change, an argument about quality, a justified mandate, or simply the desired answer arriving?
* How are earlier judgments represented and answered when the institution claims that its consultation is complete?

These questions do not predetermine a fixed number of panels, majority aggregation, or equal report weights. Such rules can have their own defects. Even a fair lottery to choose among completed reports would require a defense of why chance, rather than reasons or some authorized threshold, should allocate their standing.

The immediate remedy for an unsupported claim need not be yet another assembly. Officials can withdraw an inflated attribution, present the complete disagreement, answer the adverse arguments, and take responsibility for the policy decision. Where an independent check was actually promised, the institution may also need a process that fulfills that promise. Which remedy is sufficient depends on the mandate; the example alone does not determine it.

## 8. The limited conclusion

The argument leaves substantial work unresolved. It supplies no complete standard of legitimate commissioning, no empirical estimate of panel variation, and no verified case of officials repeatedly commissioning an unchanged question until approval. Its probability results are elementary. Its account of institutional control overlaps with existing anti-capture reasoning and process safeguards. The precise priority of this application is not established.

Nonetheless, a useful distinction survives those limits. Independence in forming a judgment and independence in allocating its standing are different achievements. Honest recruitment can secure the first without securing the second. Publicity can preserve usable information without justifying every institutional use of that information.

A lottery chooses who deliberates. It does not, merely by choosing them fairly, justify which of their completed answers the sponsor makes count as the answer of the consultation.
