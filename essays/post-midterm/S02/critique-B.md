# Critique B — S02

## Result

The limited insufficiency thesis is defensible: fair selection of deliberators does not, without further premises, justify the institutional priority of an answer-selected report. The manuscript also correctly separates a report's authenticity, evidential use, and institutional standing.

**The main revision needed is closer comparison, not correction of a demonstrated false attribution.** I established no load-bearing misquotation or attribution error in the six bibliography entries. However, independently consulted predecessors substantially narrow the contribution: selective uptake and its justificatory burden, repeated honest randomization followed by outcome selection, and the full-history stopping-rule qualification all have close antecedents. The potentially useful remainder is their application to **transparent, verdict-conditioned finality across independently deliberating panels**. Priority for that application remains unresolved.

References below use the supplied manuscript's line numbers, including its bibliography (lines 1–177). This is one independent review; no other review or essay was consulted.

## 2. Closer predecessors missing from the comparison

### A. Selective uptake after deliberation: substantial prior overlap

**Maija Setälä, “Connecting deliberative mini-publics to representative decision making” (2017), EJPR 56(4): 846–863.** The article addresses strategic commissioning, selective uptake, public reasons for accepting/rejecting recommendations, the limits of publicity, and ex post scrutiny. Relevant primary HTML locations are lines 548–575 and 596–620, particularly the sections on public interaction and suspensive vetoes. This is closer to the manuscript's institutional problem than an algorithmic recruitment comparator. It already places an important locus of control after the mini-public's deliberation. Its proposal for iterated deliberation also supplies a serious alternative to treating repetition as suspect. It does not, in the inspected passages, develop the draft's exact first-approval protocol. [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6765.12207).

**José Luis Fernández-Martínez, Joan Font, and Graham Smith, “The Sin of Omission? The Public Justification of Cherry-Picking” (2023), pp. 233–254.** The chapter expressly asks whether selective listening can be legitimate; it makes justification central while distinguishing it from obedience to recommendations. Its discussion on pp. 249–250 applies the issue to citizens' assemblies. Read especially pp. 233–234 and 238–239, author-upload extraction lines 310–328 and 604–667. This substantially precedes the draft's broad post-deliberation justificatory burden. The important difference is **selection among recommendations for adoption**, rather than **recommissioning independent panels and allocating consultation-completing status to one report**. That difference should do argumentative work, not merely be announced. The publisher dates the book to December 2023; the author's copy was uploaded in January 2024. [Author-provided primary text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377487747_The_Sin_of_Omission_The_Public_Justification_of_Cherry-Picking), [publisher metadata](https://ecpr.eu/Shop/ShopProductInfo?productID=192).

These comparisons are evidence of overlap, not proof that the exact thesis has already been published or that the manuscript copied it.

### B. Repeated honest randomization: an especially close mechanism

**Stern, Simplicio, Silva, and Castellanos Pfeiffer, “Randomization and Fair Judgment in Law and Science” (2020), §4.** The versioned preprint describes an organizer repeating an honest randomization and selecting the recording best suited to its aims; see PDF pages 8–9, extraction lines 194–214. Thus honest random devices plus outcome-conditioned retention are not a new general mechanism. Their example conceals discarded draws and randomizes allocations directly; S02 makes all panel reports public and asks about institutional standing after deliberation. The latter distinction is a possible extension, not a finding established by the prior text. [Primary preprint, version 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06709v4).

### C. Stopping-rule independence: important counterevidence, correctly accommodated

**Hendriksen, de Heide, and Grünwald, “Optional Stopping with Bayes Factors,” §2.1.** The primary preprint distinguishes stopping-rule independence from other properties and derives the invariance of posterior odds for the same compatible observed data; see extraction lines 168–281, PDF pages 4–5. S02's full-history qualification is consistent with that existing result, not a novel statistical discovery. Neither this result nor the draft's calculation grants institutional authority to an approving report. [Primary preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.09077).

### D. A closer inference already inside Lafont

The comparison should also identify Lafont's argument on pp. 91–92: minority participants considered the same information and deliberated without reaching the majority's conclusion. That is a nearby parity challenge to moving from shared deliberative credentials to a particular conclusion's authority. S02 transports the challenge **between panels** and adds a verdict-dependent designation rule. It should explain why that move adds more than another instance of the general authority problem. This is not an attribution of repeated-panel stopping to Lafont. [Original discussion](https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/publication/downloads/09_lafont.pdf).

## 3. Test of the crucial inference

### Statistical comparison: correct, but specify the observation

An independent exact-rational enumeration reproduced the manuscript's values: 37/64 and 63/64 for approval within three attempts, likelihood ratios 3, 63/37, and 1/3, and equal terminal identity probabilities in the stipulated identity-independent example. The check covers 40 finite parameter cases; it is not empirical evidence. Code and results are retained in `sources/016-independent-model-check.*`.

One useful clarification: under the known binary first-approval protocol, learning that **the third panel approved and the process stopped** already reveals reject–reject–approve. Full report texts are not necessary to recover that binary history. The reduced likelihood ratio in the second table row concerns the coarser event *approval occurred by the cap*, with the attempt index omitted. Therefore the contrast is not simply “terminal report versus full record.” It is a contrast between specified information sets. The current table is correct, but prose about terminal headlines should not imply that every terminal report loses the index/history information.

### Normative comparison: an insufficiency result, not an illegitimacy theorem

The shared-feature principle at lines 68–77 shows that fair recruitment alone underdetermines comparative priority. But it also applies to fixed-last designation and to randomly choosing among completed reports, as the manuscript itself recognizes at line 149. Consequently, the conclusion that **some further justification is needed** is not specific to approval stopping.

The distinct objection must come from the *claimed function*: a consultation represented as an outside check cannot discharge that function merely because an acceptable answer finally appears. That is plausible, but the relevant promised function must be specified. The authorized asymmetric gate at lines 125–129 is a genuine limit, not a peripheral escape clause. A finite first-approval rule can still expose a sponsor to rejection; a complete public history can still inform and constrain it. Those concessions prevent an inference from “outcome-dependent” to “not independent in any useful sense.”

After the literature comparisons, the defensible claim is therefore: **the membership warrant cannot by itself supply the missing justification of an institution's designated role**. The manuscript has not established that every transparent first-approval arrangement violates a freestanding democratic norm. It should retain that limit prominently.

### Episode boundaries and formation independence

The distinction at line 115 between reopening one episode and starting another needs a criterion not controlled solely by the sponsor's labeling. Otherwise an unchanged rerun can be redescribed as a new consultation. Specify what maintains continuity of the warrant being claimed: the proposal, question, outstanding obligation, or mandate. This matters precisely where an existing institution delegates commissioning and uptake to different actors.

Also qualify line 157's suggestion that honest recruitment can secure independence in judgment formation: the example additionally stipulates free deliberation and suitable information. An honest recruitment lottery alone cannot secure those conditions. This is a precision issue, not an established misattribution.

## 4. What remains, and what must change

**What survives comparison:** a clear case separating authentic authorship, calibrated evidence, and consultation-completing standing; a transparent stopping example that does not depend on hidden adverse reports or unequal terminal seat chances; and a limited explanation of why statistically usable information need not justify every institutional designation. That is a potentially useful applied clarification. Its exact priority, and its significance beyond the existing selective-uptake literature, are not established.

Required revisions:

1. Engage Setälä and Fernández-Martínez/Font/Smith directly, rather than treating Bagg and generic standards as the closest whole-process comparison.
2. Acknowledge the rerandomization predecessor and distinguish hidden discarded draws from public, independently authored reports with selected standing.
3. Identify the existing stopping-rule qualification; make the table's information sets explicit.
4. Explain the extra normative premise supplied by an independent-check mandate, including why it applies to the mayor rather than the admitted endorsement-search or asymmetric-gate cases.
5. Clarify the consultation-episode boundary and qualify the conclusion about recruitment securing formation independence.
6. Preserve the existing disclaimers: no new probability theorem, no demonstrated real unchanged-question rerun, and no novelty certification.
