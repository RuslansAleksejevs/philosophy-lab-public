# After Agreement Ends: What Denial Can Change

A father persuades his son Leo to attend a basketball game by saying that he will buy him a big pretzel. At half-time, the father denies having promised anything: his earlier remark, he now says, was merely a prediction. Leo dismisses the explanation but asks for a coke instead. Leonard Clapp uses this case to argue that a speaker can escape an illocutionary obligation without undermining the hearer's epistemic position concerning the speaker's original intention. The father's denial changes what they jointly accept about their exchange, and that change takes him off the illocutionary hook. [Clapp 2025, §2.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

What exactly has ended? A shared description of the earlier exchange has certainly become disputed. Leo has also stopped pressing his immediate demand. But neither change, without a further rule, establishes that his claim to the pretzel has ceased to exist.

I defend a limited objection. Clapp supplies a proposed rule connecting conversational registration to normative status; he does not simply overlook the need for one. Yet his case does not adequately distinguish cancellation of the original performance claim from practical non-pursuit, negotiated release, or a restriction on how that claim may presently be invoked. His rule also needs a temporal interpretation: changing the present classification of an utterance, ending one of its current effects, and changing whether a promise was previously made are different achievements.

This is not a demonstration that every loosely expressed promise survives every permissible denial. Nor is it a new general discovery that collaborative commitments can outlast current agreement. That distinction has substantial predecessors. The contribution is to apply it to Clapp's explanatory transition, identify the alternatives his example leaves open, and state two conditional diagnostics for evaluating a proposed discharge rule.

## 1. Four questions about one exchange

An utterance's *illocutionary force* is its character as an assertion, promise, request, or another kind of speech act. Some such acts generate rights and obligations. A promise can give its recipient a claim to the promised performance. Calling this a normative effect does not yet say that it is an overriding moral duty. An obligation within a practice and what one ought to do all things considered can differ.

For the pretzel case, distinguish four questions:

1. **Historical performance:** Did the father make a promise in the original exchange?
2. **Current claim:** Does Leo still possess the particular claim to purchase that the promise generated?
3. **Appropriate invocation:** May Leo now demand purchase, criticize the denial, or report the promise to someone else?
4. **Effective pursuit:** Would those reactions succeed, or be worth their practical costs?

These are not four mutually independent facts. A theory can connect them. The point is that the connections require explanation. A claim can exist although its holder lacks evidence sufficient for a particular accusation. A permitted demand can be too costly to pursue. Conversely, one may correctly report a past promise whose performance claim has been validly released.

The claim at issue here is specifically the performance claim created by the original promise, not every duty the father might have. His treatment of Leo could generate other moral complaints even if that claim ended. Invoking those complaints would not show that Clapp's proposed cancellation failed. Likewise, demonstrating that Leo can criticize dishonesty would not establish that he can still demand the pretzel under the original undertaking.

Two ordinary contrasts make the separation intelligible. Retracting an assertion can end a speaker's commitment to defend its content without making it false that she asserted it. Withdrawing permission to borrow a bicycle can end tomorrow's permission without making yesterday's borrowing unauthorized. A theory need not make all consequences permanent in order to distinguish a completed event from its changing consequences.

## 2. Clapp's rule, with its restrictions

Clapp stipulates that the initial future-tense utterance is a promise, generates obligations and rights, and remains sufficiently open to interpretation as a prediction. The subsequent denial does not undermine Leo's epistemic standing concerning the father's original intention. Thus the case is not simply an apparent promise later exposed as a misunderstanding. [Clapp 2025, §2 and n. 23.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

His limited-deference-to-speaker rule, LDS, says that when the scoreboard registers both an available alternative force and a denial of the original force in favor of that alternative, the next score no longer registers the original act. The scoreboard contains mutually accepted conversational information. Since Leo rejects the father's proposed replacement, neither the promise attribution nor its negation becomes shared. What becomes shared is that they disagree. Deleting an entry is not adding its negation.

LDS is not an unrestricted license to annul speech acts through dissent. Clapp's complementary rule, FIG, excludes reinterpretations of force-closed utterances. He also invokes Camp's history-sensitive standard: an alternative interpretation must respect the utterance's conventional meaning, earlier conversational commitments, and accessible assumptions, while remaining minimally cooperative. The mere fact that a sentence could express a prediction in another context is therefore insufficient. [Clapp 2025, §2; Camp 2018, p. 50.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

This matters for a seemingly clearer example. Lin asks Noor to open a workshop on Saturday. Noor says she will arrive at eight; Lin treats this as an undertaking to open; Noor explicitly confirms that the task is settled. If Noor later calls her remark a mere forecast, the confirmation may make that reinterpretation inadmissible. The example illustrates how a whole exchange can close an option left open by its first sentence. It does not independently refute Clapp's restricted rule.

I therefore grant the operative restrictions in the pretzel case for the present argument. The question is what follows even if the denial is admissible and successfully changes the mutually accepted score.

Clapp answers by introducing a further assumption in §4: performing an illocutionary act is equivalent to its performance being registered on the conversational scoreboard. In §3, he connects removal of the act attribution to escape from its constitutive normative effects. Note 24 contrasts the mutual acceptance required for appropriately holding someone responsible with the lack of acceptance sufficient for freedom from responsibility. These passages give a strong responsibility reading genuine textual support. They also leave room for a weaker reading about appropriate accountability. [Clapp 2025, §§3–4 and n. 24.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

The objection is not that Clapp states no bridge. It is that the bridge can do different jobs, and the evidence offered for the father's benefit does not yet establish which job it does.

## 3. Collaboration without a continuing veto

There is already good reason not to derive a continuing veto from collaborative creation alone. Carassa and Colombetti reject an account on which a joint commitment disappears when a participant's willingness changes. In their invitation example, a speaker who accepts the hearer's construal cannot subsequently retreat to his original intention without violating the resulting joint commitment. Their joint-meaning theory substantially anticipates the distinction between negotiated settlement and later repudiation. It is not a theory of universal persistence: they also allow some joint commitments to end through violation. [Carassa and Colombetti 2009, §§2.2–2.3 and 4.2, preprint pp. 6–7, 17–18.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/28765436_Joint_Meaning)

Gilbert explicitly distinguishes the psychological process of forming a joint commitment from its normative product. Her default rule prohibits unilateral rescission but allows background understandings that authorize it. Her collected discussion of promising also treats an ordinary future-tense assurance as potentially binding without an explicit performative formula. These are existing models of joint formation without a default continuing unilateral exit. [Gilbert 2016, pp. 21–22](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/pam/article/download/7210/7190/7135); [Gilbert 2013, opening discussion and §III](https://academic.oup.com/book/9917/chapter/157264813).

Gilbert also supplies a qualification important here. A promisee's supposed unilateral power to rescind the whole promise is not uncontroversial. What looks like unilateral release may involve the promisor's concurrence. Waiving a directed claim and rescinding an entire joint undertaking should not simply be equated. Moreover, a recipient's release power could coexist with a speaker's limited cancellation power. Directedness does not establish exclusivity. [Gilbert 2013, §IV.](https://academic.oup.com/book/9917/chapter/157264813)

Camp's account provides an especially close antecedent for distinguishing current acceptance from liability. On printed p. 59 she separates actual conversational commitments from attributed attitudes and presently available assumptions. She also uses transcription and video evidence to explain how liability can be established despite an audience's lack of evidence. The recording argument below develops that point rather than discovering it. Her account nevertheless allows deniability in apparently on-record speech and treats the boundaries as blurry. Its ideal of a well-formed conversation places the record in common ground; it is not a worked-out universal rule for every later dispute. [Camp 2018, pp. 58–63.](https://www.elisabethcamp.org/Papers/Camp.Insinuation.pdf)

Gilbert also explicitly distinguishes having a demand-right from being justified in making its corresponding demand. The separation of claim and appropriate invocation therefore has a further antecedent, not only an application here. [Gilbert 2016, pp. 23–24.](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/pam/article/download/7210/7190/7135)

McDonald's collaboration theory concerns a hearer communicating an interpretation and a speaker communicating acceptance of it. Her bad-faith-veto discussion distinguishes failure to achieve agreement from residual responsibility for misleading speech, and allows overt denial to coexist with tacit agreement. These passages do not themselves demonstrate a general power to annul an already completed promise. [McDonald 2022, §§III and IV.3.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356942768_Reimagining_Illocutionary_Force)

Together, these sources establish an alternative explanatory possibility: collaboration creates statuses whose later alteration depends on practice-specific powers. They do not by themselves settle whether Clapp's force-open promises contain just such a denial power.

## 4. What Leo's response establishes

Clapp's crucial evidence is that the denial benefits the father even though Leo rejects it. Before the denial, the promise attribution is jointly accepted; afterwards, it is disputed. Leo does not insist on the pretzel but requests something else. Clapp explains the benefit as escape from the illocutionary obligation.

The argument needs more than two observations: that the shared attribution changed and that Leo stopped pressing for performance. There are at least three explanations compatible with those observations.

**Unilateral discharge:** the permissible denial terminates Leo's performance claim. This is the strong reading of Clapp's explanation.

**Practical non-pursuit:** the claim remains, but Leo sees little point in another argument. Rejecting the denial once may be cheap; repeatedly demanding purchase may be costly or futile.

**Negotiation or release:** Leo accepts an alternative resolution, perhaps substitution of a coke, and his response contributes to ending or modifying the claim. The father then escapes performance through the ensuing interaction, not through a unilateral power already exercised by denial alone.

The last explanation is not forced by Leo's response. Requesting a coke need not waive a pretzel claim. Nor must the practical explanation be correct. The case simply does not specify enough to exclude either. One cannot infer that a claim has ended merely because its holder switches tactics.

Clapp anticipates a practical alternative in note 18. He rejects a diagnosis in terms of Dinges and Zakkou's untouchability by pointing out that Leo openly challenges the denial. This response has force against an account on which *every pertinent challenge* is practically irrational. But it does not exclude practical obstacles to *pursuing performance*. The two reactions differ. A person can sensibly object once and also sensibly avoid a protracted quarrel. [Clapp 2025, n. 18.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

Dinges and Zakkou separate epistemic restrictions from practical untouchability, and their generalized epistemic account distinguishes reactions with different requirements. Their terminology need not be stretched to cover the entire pretzel exchange. Its useful lesson here is that the availability of one reaction does not decide the availability or rationality of another. Peet likewise expressly distinguishes epistemic deniability from non-epistemic escape in his opening discussion. Neither account requires actual claims to disappear whenever accountability fails. [Dinges and Zakkou 2023, §§7–8](https://juliazakkou.net/Dinges_Zakkou_OnDeniability_Mind.pdf); [Peet 2024, §1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-024-04600-4).

Could practical non-pursuit explain a benefit specifically produced by *denial*, rather than by the father's refusal to buy? It could. An acknowledged breach leaves the undertaking as the uncontested basis of further demands. A denial can redirect the exchange toward a dispute about interpretation, so that insistence invites an argument about what was said. That change can increase the social cost of continued demands without reducing Leo's confidence about the father's intention or terminating Leo's claim. This is a possible mechanism, not an established psychological description of this particular child.

The father's conversational benefit therefore does not uniquely identify a constitutive cancellation power. That remains true even if the benefit is causal: denial can cause non-pursuit without causing discharge.

One way to sharpen the issue would be to extend the case. Does the father need Leo's concurrence to substitute a coke? Can Leo refuse the substitution while remaining entitled, under their undertaking rather than merely on general moral grounds, to the pretzel? If the father asks to be released, is there an existing claim for Leo to release? These questions concern a particular distribution of authority. They are more discriminating than whether Leo criticizes the father's dishonesty or gets the pretzel in practice.

They are not answers already contained in the original vignette. A genuine cancellation convention might make the father's request for release unnecessary. A convention granting both parties cancellation powers could preserve some asymmetries without preserving this claim. The proposed extension identifies what needs to be determined; it does not smuggle a contrary norm into the case.

## 5. Three temporal bridges

Suppose LDS successfully removes the mutually accepted promise attribution. What further bridge connects that removal to being free of responsibility?

### Historical reclassification

The strongest bridge makes whether the father previously performed a promise depend on its registration *now*. At the earlier time, the exchange counts as a promise; after denial, it no longer counts as his having promised. This is not inferred merely from deletion. It follows only if the constitutive equivalence is evaluated at the later time and applied to the historical act.

Such a rule is not conceptually impossible. Social practices can authorize annulment or later changes of status. But this interpretation owes an account of how earlier normative effects are revised. Does an already incurred breach liability disappear? Are others required to stop reporting the act as a promise? Which later audiences have that authority? A present score change cannot answer these questions simply by lacking the disputed entry.

### Prospective termination

A second bridge preserves the fact that the promise was made but gives permissible denial a power to terminate its remaining performance claim. This is coherent. We can represent the claim as existing before denial and ending afterwards. Historical truth and current liability then diverge without contradiction.

Consider the workshop timeline. The undertaking is completed on Thursday, Noor denies it on Friday, opening is due on Saturday, and a recording is recovered on Sunday. If a valid Friday cancellation power exists, Sunday's recording confirms Thursday's promise but does not restore Saturday's performance obligation. Camp's evidential point is compatible with prospective discharge. The recording distinguishes a promise from no promise; by itself, it does not distinguish an outstanding promise from a discharged one.

Change the sequence: Noor denies the undertaking only after Saturday's failure. Now the question concerns liability for an earlier breach, not merely whether future performance remains due. A rule terminating future performance need not eliminate breach liability. The theory requires another transition rule if it claims that effect too.

This reading avoids historical erasure, but must support the particular cancellation power it attributes to admissible denial. That a sentence remained interpretable in different ways does not yet establish that previously generated claims were defeasible by its speaker's later selection among them.

### A gate on present accountability

A third bridge connects shared registration only to what the audience may appropriately invoke in the current conversation. Leo's performance claim may remain, while the disputed promise cannot presently be used as an uncontested premise for a demand.

This is not necessarily a merely practical restriction. A practice could normatively require participants to suspend certain demands while their basis is disputed. The father's benefit would then concern appropriate invocation, not the extinction of Leo's claim. Nor does the gate rule itself show that every demand becomes inappropriate: repeating a contested claim and proceeding as though it were jointly accepted are different moves.

Some of Clapp's language about appropriate accountability supports this weaker reading. It may explain a conversational escape. But it would not, without another argument, establish the stronger conclusion that the obligation is gone, or that the force of the historical act is no longer a promise. The explanatory achievement would be narrower.

These bridges are alternatives within an analysis, not three equally confirmed descriptions of ordinary practice. Their point is that Clapp's constitutive assumption needs its object and time fixed before the claimed escape can be evaluated.

## 6. Two conditional diagnostics

### The uncertainty diagnostic

Suppose participants know all of the following: the earlier promise generated the specified claim; the denial satisfies the rule's force-openness and admissibility conditions; it has successfully effected the required score update; and that update terminates this claim. They should then know that the claim has ended, even if they disagree about the original intention.

This is a consequence of the restricted discharge theory, not a contradiction in it. If participants instead remain uncertain whether that very claim survives, a defender must locate what they do not know or explain why their uncertainty is mistaken. They might be unsure about admissibility, successful update, or tacit acceptance. Their question might concern an earlier breach or a different duty. Any of these replies can preserve the theory.

Thus publicly recognized disagreement alone is not decisive evidence against Clapp's restricted rule. Nor does Leo's confidence about the father's original intention prove that Leo knows his present performance claim survives. The diagnostic becomes discriminating only when the relevant rule, its satisfaction and the particular disputed claim are fixed. The pretzel case does not independently establish those epistemic conditions.

The diagnostic is still useful. It forces a theory to say whether an interlocutor's question is about discovering an existing claim, questioning a cancellation power, or simply failing to understand an already accomplished discharge. Those descriptions have different implications for what further evidence could resolve the dispute.

### The acknowledgment comparison

Take a proposed explanation of discharge and compare it with a description that acknowledges the earlier undertaking. For example: the recipient consented to release, a specified condition failed, or a pre-existing cancellation option was exercised. Those changes can end a claim even while everyone agrees that it was originally generated.

Now consider an explanation consisting only in the speaker's denial. If acknowledging the earlier undertaking removes the alleged ground of discharge, the proposed power is constitutively denial-dependent. This does not make it invalid. It identifies the very power that needs support.

The comparison is made by the theorist, not by asking the speaker actually to utter an acknowledgment. Actual words might renew a commitment. That additional act must be held fixed. Nor is the comparison suitable for an evidential correction: replacing evidence of non-formation with acknowledgment would destroy the correction's basis.

A hypothetical practice illustrates the limit. Suppose its rules expressly make force-open assurances binding until the speaker issues an admissible denial. Acknowledgment would remove the trigger, so the comparison would fail, yet the stipulated rule could still validly terminate the assurance. The comparison exposes dependence on denial; it cannot decide whether ordinary practice contains that rule.

Together, the diagnostics organize the burden of explanation. Neither is an independent decision procedure for settling contested speech acts.

## 7. What the objection does not exclude

A defender may answer that LDS and the constitutive-score assumption are precisely the independently proposed norms the argument demands. That is correct. Merely naming a bridge does not make its support adequate, but demanding one does not refute one already proposed.

Clapp motivates his assumption through the conception of speech acts as changes in normative status achieved by intersubjective agreement. This supports collaboration in producing statuses. It does not yet select among the temporal bridges. A jointly produced status can persist, remain defeasible through an authorized move, or depend on continued agreement. The normative character of its product does not alone tell us which arrangement it is. [Clapp 2025, §4.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

Nor does the objection prohibit later force change. Cousens argues that later conversations can give an earlier utterance further local forces, with a score that is not simply unanimous psychological acceptance. His §4 also considers re-scoring between related types, including predictions, pledges and promises, and allows some conversationally enacted obligations to be undone or suspended. This is substantive counterpressure, not merely a theory of adding force without removing anything. The present objection leaves such transitions possible; it questions whether the pretzel exchange establishes a particular transition terminating Leo's previously generated claim. [Cousens 2023, §4.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04395-w)

The objection is descriptive rather than a demand for morally better conversational rules. A community could give speakers an unattractive denial power. If that power is established for the relevant class of undertakings, it would answer the objection. But observing that people let denials pass does not establish it. Their behavior can also reflect practical abandonment, uncertainty, suspension of demands or consensual release. An explanatory theory must discriminate these possibilities before using the observation as evidence of claim extinction.

A history-sensitive account must equally avoid giving hearers final authority. An audience may have misunderstood, misremembered, or imposed a task the speaker never accepted. Later clarification can expose defective formation. In that case there is no actual claim to terminate. Preserving a possible claim during disagreement is not manufacturing one out of a hearer's confidence.

Finally, no complete survey supports a priority claim for these diagnostics. Wanderer's abstract distinguishes clarification during an encounter from subsequent clarification; Labinaz's repository abstract describes the negotiation and stabilization of normative effects; Buchanan and Schiller's abstract offers a responsibility-based approach to linguistic commitment. Full comparison with these relevant accounts remains unavailable here. Their abstracts do not establish either duplication or its absence. [Wanderer 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0020174X.2023.2255633); [Labinaz 2026](https://arts.units.it/handle/11368/3142998); [Buchanan and Schiller 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/mila.12422).

## 8. The remaining claim

The pretzel case shows a change in shared attribution and an apparent conversational benefit. Even granting its admissible denial, it does not establish that the performance claim initially created by the promise has been unilaterally terminated. Leo's challenge excludes neither selective non-pursuit nor subsequent negotiation. Clapp's explicit constitutive assumption offers a stronger explanation, but its temporal scope and the evidence distinguishing it from these alternatives remain unsettled.

The resulting constraint is an explanatory one: specify the claim, the time and the transition rule before moving from loss of shared attribution to freedom from responsibility. Correction, prospective discharge, historical reclassification and restricted accountability cannot substitute for one another without argument.

This leaves a substantive task unresolved: determining whether the relevant force-open promises actually grant their speakers a cancellation power. No independent counterexample meeting all of Clapp's restricted conditions has been established here. It also limits what the case establishes about competing theories of force. An accountability benefit need not decide whether an earlier act's force was speaker-determined, audience-constituted or collaboratively formed. A successful argument for collaboration must show more than that denial disrupts present shared acceptance.

Agreement has ended. What else has ended remains the question.
