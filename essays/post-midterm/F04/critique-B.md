**Result: major revision of the contribution boundary and the confrontation with the target, not rejection for a demonstrated false citation.** The draft identifies a real inferential gap: removing a jointly accepted description does not automatically discharge a previously generated claim. ## 1. Load-bearing attribution audit

### Clapp: supported target, but narrow its formulation

The attribution at draft line 19 is supported by the supplied publisher-page extraction:

- §2, lines 146–147: a promise initially creates obligations and rights; the subsequent denial replaces the shared promise attribution with a shared description of disagreement, from which Clapp infers escape from the illocutionary obligation.
- §2, lines 148–157: LDS applies only where the alternative interpretation remains available; FIG excludes impermissible reinterpretations. Line 153 imports Camp's restriction by commitments already undertaken, not just sentence meaning.
- §3, line 165: Clapp expressly moves from the disappearance of the scoreboard attribution of constitutive normative statuses to escape from accountability.
- §4, lines 167–175: the claim is restricted to sufficiently interpretively open utterances; line 169 makes the equivalence between performing an act and its presence on the scoreboard a fundamental assumption.
- Note 24, lines 255–256: acquiring responsibility requires mutual acceptance, whereas escape requires its absence. The note mixes the language of appropriate accountability with that of freedom from responsibility.
- §4, line 176: Clapp distinguishes descriptive explanation from ethical approval.

Thus the draft has not invented an unrestricted cancellation thesis and simply assigned it to Clapp. But it must consistently distinguish **his actual conditional rule** from the stronger rule used in draft §2, under which any publicly recognized disagreement suffices. Clapp's rule requires an admissible force reinterpretation, not mere expressed dissent. Also, Clapp does explicitly supply a connecting assumption in §4. The objection should be that this assumption and its application need independent justification, not that he never states a bridge between score and normative status.

There is a further interpretive issue, not an established attribution error: some passages can be read as governing when an audience may appropriately hold a speaker accountable, rather than erasing every claim or historical event. The stronger ontological reading is well motivated by §4 and note 24, but the reviewable disagreement should identify that reading and explain why the weaker one is insufficient for Clapp's stated explanation. Removal of an entry is not itself addition of its negation; Clapp explicitly recognizes this at lines 147 and 150. Draft line 41's retrospective-erasure consequence therefore needs the constitutive equivalence as an additional premise.

### McDonald: fair distinction from a continuing veto

Draft line 17 accurately describes the three-stage collaboration account and its qualifications. Independently consulted primary passages support the conditions in §III (web lines 552–670), delayed and nonverbal signaling in §IV.1 (1131–1304), overlapping negotiations in §IV.2 (1306–1361), and the bad-faith-veto discussion in §IV.3 (1363–1532). The latter preserves responsibility for misleading use and perlocutionary effects and allows tacit agreement despite an overt denial.

Crucially, the gaslighting example concerns a failure to reach agreement, not an explicit demonstration that a completed, ratified undertaking is later annulled. The draft is right not to collapse McDonald into Clapp. Keep that separation; the general collaboration literature is not refuted by refuting continuing-consensus cancellation.

### Camp: accurate citation, underdescribed antecedent

Draft line 81 is supported at the stated printed pages. Page 50 restricts admissible reinterpretation by prior commitments. Pages 58–61 separate record, changing score, and common ground; page 60 permits on-record commitments without direct articulation.

But **page 59 is more directly anticipatory than the draft admits**. Camp distinguishes actual defensive/executive liability from attributed attitudes and currently shared assumptions, requires tracking commitments across contexts, and uses transcription or video footage to expose liabilities even where actual hearers or reporters lack evidence. This substantially anticipates draft §2's recording argument and §5's separation of liability from effective enforcement. The provided proof's printed page 59 is PDF page 20; it is not web extraction line 59.

Counterevidence matters too: pages 60–62 allow deniability in seemingly on-record speech and describe blurry boundaries; page 62 says that in a well-formed conversation the record's elements are in common ground. Camp is not a fully worked-out universal persistence theory. Nevertheless, the present manuscript should present its recording/record distinction as development and application of this antecedent, not merely acknowledge Camp as a general reason to reject a verbal criterion.

### Dinges–Zakkou and Peet: supported, not targets

Draft line 93 fairly describes Dinges and Zakkou's epistemic/practical separation and reaction-relative account. Consulted preprint pp. 10–18 cover §§6–8: §7 relativizes deniability to reactions; §8 separates untouchability from epistemic deniability. Their account does not require actual obligations to disappear. Distinguish pragmatic cancellation from their separate notion of cancellability, discussed in notes 22 and 24.

Peet §§1–3 support the high-credence/knowledge contrast. Section 1 also explicitly distinguishes a non-epistemic form of escape through denial, outside his paper's main scope. The draft should avoid making the enforcement/liability distinction appear to be an objection these epistemic accounts overlook. It is principally a resource for challenging Clapp's stronger explanation.

## 2. Closest predecessors and alternatives

### A. Carassa and Colombetti (2009): direct, omitted overlap

*Joint Meaning*, *Journal of Pragmatics* 41(9):1837–1854, DOI 10.1016/j.pragma.2009.03.005, is a crucial missing comparison. In §2.2 (consulted preprint pp. 6–7; web lines 493–557), they reject defining a joint commitment by current epistemic/volitional states: a participant's change of willingness would then dissolve it without any remaining requirement to negotiate cancellation. In §4.2 (preprint pp. 17–18; lines 1484–1553), a speaker who accepts an utterance's invitation construal cannot subsequently fall back on his original intention without violating a joint commitment. That is close to the draft's distinction between collaborative settlement and later repudiation. Their theory retains separate speaker meaning and allows some joint commitments to end through violation (§2.3); it is neither a universal persistence rule nor identical to a general theory of illocutionary force. **Substantial actual overlap, not proof that every proposed argument or test is already theirs.** McDonald herself cites this work in §III. The omissions should be repaired before the contribution is assessed. [Primary author-upload text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/28765436_Joint_Meaning).

### B. Gilbert: an established collaboration-without-unilateral-exit model

Gilbert's *Three Dogmas About Promising* (2013 collection, chapter 13) already opens with an ordinary future-tense assurance, rejects its redescription as a mere prediction, and separates joint creation from unilateral rescission. Her 2015 *Joint Commitment: What It Is and Why It Matters*, pp. 21–22, distinguishes psychological process from normative product and permits special background rescission rules while denying a default unilateral exit. These are substantial predecessors of draft §§1–3. Do not report the collection date as the original date of every argument. [2013 chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/9917/chapter/157264813); [2015 primary paper](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/pam/article/download/7210/7190/7135).

There is also a disagreement to confront: the chapter's selected release discussion questions whether promisees always possess unilateral rescission power, rather than only supplying concurrence when the promisor wants release. The draft's claim about Lin's authority is plausible in its stipulated case, but cannot simply be treated as an uncontested consequence of every collaborative model. Waiving a claim and rescinding the whole joint undertaking need separation.

### C. Camp: closest antecedent of the record/evidence argument

This is more than related literature: printed p. 59 supplies both the liability/attitude distinction and the recording-based evidential point used in the manuscript. The actual remaining difference is the explicit temporal challenge to Clapp's discharge inference and the proposed diagnostic—not the discovery of a history-sensitive public record.

### D. Cousens (2023): relevant counterpressure, not the same thesis

*Tweet acts and quote-tweetable acts*, §4, argues that later conversational re-scoring can give an earlier utterance new local forces. It also distinguishes conversational score from universally shared psychological states and treats the score as objective in a limited sense. Consequently, temporal change and objective normativity can coexist; retrospective force revision cannot be dismissed merely as conceptually impossible. This concerns multiple conversational contexts and force addition rather than cancellation of this promisee's settled claim. A useful contrast, not an established refutation. [Primary article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04395-w).

This is sufficiently close to require comparison. Full-text and abstract-page opens failed; only the primary search-exposed abstract and selected notes were consulted. **Likely temporal overlap; its actual position on discharge is unresolved.** [Publisher record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0020174X.2023.2255633).
- **Labinaz, “Illocutionary force beyond relativism: toward a processual-normative account” (2026).** The university-hosted abstract addresses divergence, shared rights/obligations, and trajectories that stabilize normative effects. Publisher access failed and the repository offers no associated file. **Related processual alternative; not a demonstrated anticipation of this test.** [Primary repository abstract](https://arts.units.it/handle/11368/3142998).
- **Buchanan and Schiller, “Meaning and responsibility” (2023).** Only primary abstract/opening search excerpts were accessible; the work concerns linguistic commitment grounded in action-theoretic responsibility. It remains a responsibility-based lead, not evidence of the present argument's duplication. [Publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/mila.12422).

## 3. Where the crucial inference still needs work

### The main example may satisfy the target's exception

Settlement explicitly confirms the task and stipulates the absence of a special cancellation power (draft line 31). That is strong evidence for the manuscript's conclusion, but it also makes the prediction reinterpretation look inconsistent with commitments already undertaken. Clapp cites precisely such a constraint. Draft §4 recognizes the escape: the completed exchange may be closed to the relevant force reinterpretation. It has not then refuted Clapp's restricted rule.

To confront the actual rule, the manuscript needs either:

1. a case where Clapp's admissibility conditions independently hold, a definite claim was nevertheless generated, and denial does not discharge it; or
2. an argument that his own pretzel case fails to justify the asserted transition even on its stipulated openness.

The latter is promising: the son's dropping a performance demand may reflect release or practical non-pursuit. Rejecting a denial verbally does not establish that further pursuit is practically worthwhile. Clapp's note 18 recognizes the untouchability alternative but rejects it by appealing to the son's challenge; challenge and continued enforcement are different reactions. The draft should directly address this existing exchange of arguments, rather than rely mainly on a clearer substitute case. This is an evidential/interpretive objection, not an established misquotation.

### The uncertainty problem is conditional

The problem follows if the parties know both the continuing-consensus rule and that the denial satisfies its operative conditions. Public disagreement alone need not give knowledge that a denial is admissible, that earlier agreement was defective, or that a surviving tacit agreement has vanished. Ordinary uncertainty could concern those conditions. The strongest present-agreement rule is vulnerable to the draft's argument; the actual restricted view needs these added premises. The two-history comparison correctly says histories differ, but its conclusion establishes that present disagreement is insufficient information, not by itself that every score-based theory ignores history.

### The acknowledgment test does not independently refute a denial power

The draft correctly calls the test diagnostic. It should maintain that modest status. Replacing denial with acknowledgment removes the very trigger of a practice that grants a specific denial/annulment power. Its failure therefore cannot itself establish that the power is unreal. The substantive question is whether that power actually belongs to the practice, and with what scope. Clapp claims such a norm; the criticism must assess his evidence. The no-special-cancellation stipulation makes the test clear for Settlement, but does not settle that dispute.

**Substantial overlap:** Camp's public-record/liability and recorded-evidence argument; Carassa–Colombetti's settled joint meaning and rejection of mental-state dissolution; Gilbert's joint formation without default unilateral rescission.

The viable contribution is an **application and refinement**: make explicit which transition from shared attribution to discharge is justified in Clapp, distinguish historical occurrence from present liability and practical accountability, and examine what the uncertainty argument and acknowledgment diagnostic add. These devices may be useful, but new labels and a finite search do not establish their novelty.
