# F04 — Argument and counterexamples

## Outcome

**Major unresolved issues in the target-specific argument; the narrower continuity constraint is potentially defensible.** I would not set this essay aside. Its central logical distinction is sound: a condition sufficient or necessary for forming a claim need not be a condition for maintaining it. The essay also makes a serious objection to identifying current common ground with every outstanding obligation. What remains insufficiently established is that the relevant *permissible, force-open* denials in Clapp's account extinguish obligations which ordinary practice instead preserves. The strongest example either excludes that account's operative conditions or stipulates away its proposed cancellation norm.

No unconditional fatal objection was found. A universal prohibition on cancellation through loss of agreement would face counterexamples, but the manuscript expressly allows practice-specific withdrawal rules. Conversely, those allowances limit how much its constraint alone can establish against a theory proposing just such a rule.

## Charitable reconstruction

The thesis is not that speech acts are irrevocable, that the audience always wins, or that intentions are irrelevant. It is this conditional constraint:

1. Some completed exchanges generate practice-governed claims and powers, not merely shared beliefs (13–25).
2. Later disagreement removes a proposition from what both parties presently accept. That is distinct from showing that the original formation failed or exercising a power to terminate an existing claim (35–55).
3. The move from loss of shared acceptance to discharge therefore needs an additional constitutive rule. Collaborative formation by itself does not supply it (21–25, 49, 61).
4. Ordinary promises supply defeasible evidence against a blanket ongoing-consensus rule: recipients can release claims; historical evidence can establish a prior undertaking; recognized disputes need not settle whether an obligation remains (23, 39–47, 97–99).
5. The acknowledgment test diagnoses whether a proffered later change explains discharge independently of denying the original act. It expressly exempts correction of a mistaken attribution and demands identification of special annulment powers (63–69).
6. Thus theories should track formation, correction, discharge, evidential access and practical enforcement separately. They may accommodate collaboration and unilateral withdrawal without treating every denial as cancellation (109–139).

The valid result is a **non-entailment plus an explanatory burden**. A stronger conclusion—that ordinary force-open promises never carry the proposed deniability power—requires further premises about that practice.

## Strengths to preserve

- **Formation versus persistence is a real distinction**, not a verbal trick (21–25, 35–37). Interruption usefully separates mistaken attribution from the repudiation of an actual undertaking. The essay explicitly does not pretend that the histories have identical evidence (35).
- **Responsibility versus enforceability is carefully separated** (91–101). A listener's reluctance or inability to press a claim does not entail its extinction. This is especially important when explaining an apparently successful denial.
- **The author does not attack all collaborative theories indiscriminately** (17–19). The consulted McDonald passages support the distinction between failed ratification, residual responsibility for misleading, and tacit agreement despite verbal rejection: §III, supplied web lines 561–674; §IV.3, 1456–1532. Nothing read licenses attributing an unrestricted post-completion veto to McDonald.
- **Repair is not treated as automatic evasion** (57, 109–119, 129). Genuine misunderstanding can defeat formation; retraction can terminate some assertion-related standings without falsifying the historical report that an assertion occurred.
- **The descriptive objection is acknowledged rather than moralized away** (123–127). The manuscript concedes that unattractive cancellation norms might really exist. Camp's distinction between public commitments and current conversational assumptions, acknowledged at 81, provides substantive support for this kind of separation, including the role of recordings (Camp, supplied text 1008–1047, printed pp. 59–60).

## Major concerns

### M1. The decisive example falls outside the most important disputed class

**Locations:** 3, 31–37, 75–87; especially 31, 79 and 87.

Settlement adds explicit uptake and confirmation, then assumes both successful formation and the absence of a special cancellation power. This is a good counterexample to a *universal* rule that every disagreement erases every promise. But Clapp restricts his rule to cases sufficiently open to illocutionary reinterpretation, and to permissible denials. His supplied §2 states both limited deference and a complementary prohibition on impermissible reinterpretation (text 148–157); §4 reiterates the force-openness restriction (167, 176). He also quotes Camp's requirement that reinterpretation respect commitments already undertaken (153).

The manuscript fairly recognizes at 79 that subsequent confirmation may close the relevant option. That concession prevents a straw man, but it also means Settlement need not falsify the actual rule. The remaining example at 85 concerns uncertainty about punctuality, not uncertainty between a promise and a prediction. At 87 the author withholds judgment when force remains unresolved. This does not answer the intermediate case Clapp stipulates: a promise is successfully made and mutually accepted at the earlier time, yet the words retain enough force-openness to license later denial. Successful formation, unresolved *knowledge*, and residual *admissibility of reinterpretation* are not the same property.

**Strongest reply:** Clapp's own pretzel case explicitly grants initial promising and rights/obligations (141–147). The essay can contest the later inference using those concessions; it need not adopt a complete formation theory or provide perfect linguistic disambiguation. That reply is substantial. Nevertheless, it leaves a conflict between the two interpretations of what the initial rights amount to, rather than an example on which Clapp's restricted account demonstrably predicts the wrong outcome.

**What would resolve it:** Identify a completed undertaking that still satisfies the target's force-openness and permissible-denial conditions, and explain why its original practice-governed claim survives. Alternatively, restrict the achieved result to rejecting unrestricted consensus dependence and demanding a temporal explanation. A force-open counterexample with persistent liability would strengthen the specific challenge; evidence that these undertakings are constituted as defeasible by permissible denial would narrow it.

### M2. The target proposes an additional rule; the essay has not yet defeated it

**Locations:** 19–25, 49, 55–69, 125–127, 131, 137.

The essay is correct that collaboration alone does not imply continuing veto power. But Clapp does not offer only that bare inference. He proposes a limited-deference-to-speaker norm (LDS, supplied text 149–150), argues for it from deniability cases, and connects act performance to scoreboard registration through a fundamental assumption (169–175). Those proposals can be disputed, but they are candidates for exactly the additional rule the essay requires. Line 127 permits a practice that builds cancellation through denial into an assurance's original normative effects.

This creates a fork. If the constraint means “denial does not cancel unless this practice gives it cancellation force,” Clapp can agree while arguing that the relevant practice does. If it means “the practice cannot constitute cancellation merely by loss of agreement,” the concession at 127 retracts that stronger claim. The useful burden of independent support remains, but the essay must then assess LDS's support rather than take the absence of such a rule as established.

**Strongest reply:** An asserted rule is not an independently *established* rule. Clapp's inference at 147 and 165 does slide from disputed scoreboard registration to being off the illocutionary hook. Note 24 and §4 make a strong constitutive interpretation genuinely available. The essay rightly exposes that transition and proposes rival explanations of the behavioral evidence. This is not an invented target. Still, exposing underdetermination establishes an explanatory challenge, not that the rival practice description is false.

Clapp's note 18 (supplied text 237) rejects a practical-escape interpretation because Leo openly challenges the denial. An open challenge does not establish that Leo will pursue performance, nor that pursuing it is costless: a child can call the denial false yet stop seeking the pretzel to avoid further conflict. The manuscript's distinction at 95 is relevant here but does not directly examine this strongest primary example and its rejoinder.

**What would resolve it:** Compare the cancellation hypothesis with practical non-enforcement in the same force-open case, using the particular rights and release powers at stake. No automatic demand for an experiment follows: a sharper philosophical case or stronger analysis of ordinary judgments could suffice. Mere undesirable consequences would not defeat the descriptive hypothesis.

### M3. The uncertainty problem overreaches unless the standing and the successful update are specified

**Locations:** 39–47, 55–59, 95, 113–117, 125.

For a blanket rule (O(t)\leftrightarrow A(t)), publicly known lack of agreement entails lack of the corresponding current obligation. That is a legitimate pressure point. But a restricted rule requires more than recognized disagreement: force-openness, a permissible denial, and a successful score update. Participants can dispute whether those conditions hold. They can also dispute whether an obligation existed before denial, whether it was already breached, whether reliance generated another claim, or whether a correction duty survives. The manuscript itself recognizes differentiated surviving standings at 117. Current disagreement need not settle these questions even on a denial-based discharge theory.

Thus “ordinary disputes remain open” (39–41) is insufficient without a case where the parties understand that the disputed *current* claim is the very one the successful permissible denial supposedly terminates. Knowledge of the norm is also a stipulated assumption at 39, not something every actual participant need possess.

The recording extension needs the same precision. A recording can prove a promise existed before discharge without creating any retrospective standing. If denial occurs before Saturday and the practice permits prospective cancellation, the recording's later evidential use does not restore Saturday's performance obligation. If denial occurs only after breach, the target needs a separate account of whether liability for that breach survives. The draft does not specify the denial's timing tightly enough for this contrast (3, 45). Nor does cancellation at a later time logically imply that the act never occurred. That further conclusion requires a genuinely retroactive or time-indexed theory of historical force.

**Strongest reply:** Clapp does speak of retroactive scoreboard changes and assumes that performing an act depends on its being recorded (152, 169). This gives the historical objection a genuine target. But an opponent can narrow the account to prospective normative effects or distinguish historical events from current attribution. The essay's argument then blocks the strongest historical-erasure reading, not every denial-based discharge account.

**What would resolve it:** Fix the time of denial, identify the particular standing under dispute, and distinguish a historical promise, an outstanding performance claim and breach-related liability. No complete theory of all speech acts is needed. This would show whether uncertainty survives where the restricted rival rule actually predicts certainty.

## Minor concerns and qualified counterexamples

### m1. The acknowledgment test cannot adjudicate a constitutively denial-dependent power

**Locations:** 63–69, 127.

Suppose a practice gives an openly interpretable assurance a defeasibility condition: a permissible repudiation of its force ends the corresponding current conversational entitlement. This is a hypothetical countermodel, not an observed ordinary convention. Acknowledging the promise instead removes the triggering condition. The cited change then fails the test even though, by hypothesis, a valid discharge occurred. Similarly, a continuing joint conversational plan could give each participant an implicit power to stop sustaining it, without a separately announced cancellation clause.

Line 69 expressly allows special annulment powers and calls the test diagnostic, so this is **not a fatal counterexample to the qualified thesis**. It shows that test failure establishes dependence on denial, not invalidity of the discharge. The practice evidence must do the remaining work. Also, the acknowledgment “I did undertake it” can itself renew commitment; a careful diagnostic should hold that possible new effect fixed rather than assume the replacement is conversationally inert.

### m2. A recipient's release power is not exclusive cancellation authority

**Locations:** 23–25, 97–101, 133.

An audience's power to waive a claim can coexist with a speaker's limited power to terminate it. The asymmetric ability to release strongly supports a directed claim rather than merely generic wrongdoing, but it does not by itself show that only the recipient can end the claim. Line 25 allows the alternative and line 59 allows unilateral withdrawal; accordingly the point is underdetermination of the offered support, not an internal contradiction. To defeat the alternative, the limits of the speaker's power matter.

### m3. “Normative standing” covers several different kinds of authority

**Locations:** 7–13, 53–55, 99–101, 113–117, 137.

Promises provide the developed argument. Requests, assertions, permissions and jointly maintained conversational agendas need not have identical persistence rules. The draft already qualifies the claim by practice and particular standing, and its assertion discussion is a strength. Nevertheless, historical truth that an assertion occurred does not alone preserve its original demand for defense; a duty to correct may have a different basis. The essay establishes most clearly a constraint on deriving termination without a practice-specific transition rule, not a uniform substantive persistence law for every listed speech act.

## Uncertain issues and dissent

- **Actual ordinary norms remain underdetermined** (125–127). The manuscript's recipient-power and recording judgments are plausible defeasible data, not independently settled measurements of every force-open exchange. I do not infer the truth of Clapp's rival rule merely from the existence of an alternative interpretation.
- **Novel contribution is bounded, not exhausted** (81, 137). The consulted Camp passage already distinguishes actual liability from common ground and invokes recordings. The manuscript acknowledges that antecedent. Whether the uncertainty problem and acknowledgment test add enough distinctive argumentative value requires further comparison; no finite reading justifies an exhaustive novelty verdict.
- **The contextual closure question is genuinely contested** (77–87). Clapp quotes a history-sensitive admissibility standard, so accepting that history limits reinterpretation is not necessarily a new concession by his defender. But his emphasis on conventional wording and his constitutive scoreboard assumption still leave room for the essay's temporal objection. I would preserve this criticism, not dismiss it as merely terminological.
- **Dissent from an overly negative verdict:** The author repeatedly limits the claim, allows valid withdrawal, distinguishes repair from discharge and treats undesirable norms as possible. It would be unfair to reject the paper for omitting an algorithm it explicitly disclaims (137), or for failing to make all responsibility permanent. The restricted account is coherent and worth pursuing.

## What would defeat or narrow the thesis?

1. **Defeat its ordinary-practice application:** Show that, for the relevant successfully formed force-open undertaking, permissible denial is an independently supported constitutive termination condition. That would satisfy the essay's burden and contradict its expectation of survival in that class, not its logical formation/persistence distinction.
2. **Narrow the target:** Show that confirmation closes the relevant reinterpretation option, or that only present publicly claimable status is removed while historical liability survives. The current examples then support continuity but do not refute that restricted account.
3. **Strengthen the objection:** Show a permissible, force-open denial with continued directed liability, recipient release authority and uncertainty about that very current claim, while excluding correction, waiver and pragmatic abandonment. That would directly challenge the proposed score-to-discharge transition.

**Bottom line:** The paper's best result is that collaborative formation and scoreboard deletion cannot *without further support* establish discharge. Its strongest unresolved task is demonstrating that the further support offered by the restricted target fails. Preserve the distinction and its qualifications; do not yet treat the specific deniability theory as refuted.
