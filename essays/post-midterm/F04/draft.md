# After Agreement Ends

Lin needs someone to open a community workshop on Saturday morning. Noor says, “I’ll be there at eight.” Lin replies, “Good. Then opening is your job, and I can stop looking.” Noor answers, “Yes, that settles Saturday.” Later, Noor says she was merely predicting that she would be nearby. Lin rejects this explanation. They now disagree about whether Noor undertook to open the workshop.

What has that disagreement changed? It has changed what they can proceed on as an agreed description of their conversation. It might also affect Lin’s confidence about what happened. But has it removed Noor’s obligation to open the workshop? Has it taken away Lin’s standing to demand that she do so?

These questions must be separated. I defend a constraint on theories of speech acts: where an exchange has already generated a claim against a participant, the subsequent loss of agreement about that exchange does not, by itself, extinguish the claim. A later denial can be evidence that the apparent undertaking never occurred. It can also accompany a valid cancellation. Neither possibility establishes that withdrawing present agreement is itself a general power of cancellation.

The point matters for accounts on which the force of an utterance—its being a promise, assertion, request, or something else—is collaboratively determined. Collaboration can explain how participants acquire rights and obligations. It does not follow that each participant retains a continuing veto over them. I will identify the inference connecting those claims, explain why it fails, and propose a test for distinguishing correction from discharge. The result permits extensive negotiation over speech without making completed undertakings depend on uninterrupted agreement that they exist.

## 1. Agreement and what it produces

A speech act can change more than what its audience believes. A promise can give someone a claim to performance. An assertion can make its speaker answerable for a claim, including by defending or withdrawing it. A request can put an action forward for another person’s consideration without creating the obligation characteristic of an order. Call these changes changes in *normative standing*: changes in what particular people may demand, challenge, withdraw, or release.

The word “normative” does not certify moral legitimacy. An unfair practice can distribute standings. Nor does every disappointing utterance create an obligation to deliver what its audience hoped for. We need an account of which act, if any, was successfully performed before we can identify its consequences.

McDonald’s collaboration theory offers an account of formation. An audience communicates how it interprets an utterance, and the speaker communicates acceptance of that interpretation. Their contributions can be tacit and need not occur in adjacent turns. Her treatment of bad-faith vetoes also distinguishes successful performance from responsibility for misleading an audience; moreover, apparent verbal rejection may conceal continuing tacit agreement. These qualifications matter: collaborative formation need not mean that any uttered denial destroys force. [McDonald 2022, §§III and IV.1–IV.3.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356942768_Reimagining_Illocutionary_Force)

A stronger temporal claim appears in Clapp’s treatment of deniability. For utterances sufficiently open to interpretation, he argues that a permissible denial can remove the original act from a shared conversational scoreboard, even when the audience remains convinced of the original interpretation. The resulting disagreement takes the speaker off the illocutionary hook. His discussion makes mutual acceptance central to both the act and its normative effects; note 24 explicitly contrasts the agreement needed to acquire responsibility with the lack of agreement sufficient to escape it. He restricts permissible reinterpretation and presents an explanatory account of conversational norms, not a moral recommendation. [Clapp 2025, §§2–4 and n. 24.](https://www.scielo.br/j/man/a/cStgH5LzvjZ8DZZYP58PS5y/?format=html&lang=en&ilang=en)

The issue is thus not whether speakers may clarify their words. It is whether the conditions for *creating* a standing are also conditions for its *continuing to exist*. Agreement can figure in the former without figuring in the latter.

To see why, consider what Lin and Noor have accomplished. Their exchange assigns a task to Noor. Lin consequently gains a kind of authority over its release: she can say, “I no longer need you to do that.” Before the exchange, there was no such undertaking for her to release. After it, asking to be released differs from simply announcing a change of plan. These differences are among the things a theory of promising should explain.

If Noor can remove the undertaking just by refusing to agree that it occurred, the authority supposedly transferred to Lin is conditional on Noor continuing to acknowledge it. That would be a possible arrangement. But it is not a consequence of the bare fact that their original arrangement was collaborative. We must establish the continuing condition separately.

## 2. The inference from dispute to freedom

Consider two histories.

In **Settlement**, the workshop exchange occurs as described. Noor understands that Lin is assigning the opening task to her, and freely confirms it. Assume that this is enough, under their ordinary practice, for Noor to undertake that task. There is no hidden qualification, misunderstanding, or special right to cancel at will.

In **Interruption**, Noor again says, “I’ll be there at eight.” Lin asks whether this is an undertaking to open the workshop or merely an expectation of arriving. Their call disconnects before they resolve the question. Neither has grounds for treating the interrupted exchange as an agreed assignment. Later, Lin mistakenly remembers receiving the confirmation that occurred in Settlement.

In both histories, the later dispute can have the same shape. Lin says Noor undertook the task; Noor denies it. What differs is whether the relevant process of formation was completed. The comparison does not hold their entire evidence or conversational histories fixed. Its point is precisely that those histories matter when the pattern of current disagreement is the same.

A rule saying that lack of current agreement removes the undertaking cannot distinguish these histories. A rule saying that lack of agreement *during formation* prevents an undertaking can distinguish them. The difference is not supplied by the current unanimity of their judgments. It is supplied by what they previously did.

There is a deeper difficulty. Suppose both participants understand a rule on which an earlier act has its force only while they presently agree that it does. Once their disagreement is manifest, they have decisive evidence that this condition is not met. They should then know that there is no current standing generated by that act. Yet ordinary disputes often leave precisely that question open: “One of us may still owe the other something; we need to establish what happened.”

Call this the *uncertainty problem*. On the strong present-agreement rule, publicly recognized disagreement settles the negative answer to the very question the participants take themselves to be disputing. It turns uncertainty about an outstanding undertaking into knowledge that none remains. If the rule concerns the earlier act itself, it goes further: disagreement about whether the act occurred supplies a method for ensuring that it did not.

We should not dismiss this merely because it sounds strange. Some social statuses do depend on continuing agreement. Two people can stop sustaining an informal shared game. A conversational proposal may cease to be live once someone rejects it. The question is whether the same structure explains a completed promise, and the standing it gives its recipient. The workshop case provides reason to deny this. Lin’s demand concerns an undertaking already made, not a proposal whose adoption awaits Noor’s present assent.

A recording sharpens the contrast. Suppose Noor does not open the workshop, the dispute continues, and only afterwards they recover a recording of the full original exchange. It reveals the confirmation in Settlement, or the broken call in Interruption. Its natural role is evidential: it helps settle whether Noor had undertaken to open the workshop before the relevant morning. Playing the recording is not making a new promise to perform an action whose appointed time has already passed.

A theory may posit a special power to create retrospective standings. But it then owes an explanation of why ordinary evidence of a prior undertaking operates as an exercise of that power. The simpler explanation distinguishes the existence of a standing from the participants’ present agreement about it. An undertaking can remain in force while the available evidence, and their acceptance of that evidence, are unsettled.

There are therefore two ways to read a scoreboard from which disputed acts disappear. If it records only what participants presently accept, removing an entry accurately records disagreement. It does not yet explain discharge. If it records the standings that actually remain, removing an entry requires more than the fact that acceptance has ceased. The same update cannot do both jobs without an additional rule connecting them.

## 3. A rule of continuity

The needed constraint can be stated without a complete theory of promising or assertion:

> If an exchange successfully generated a standing, a later refusal to agree that it did is insufficient, by itself, to terminate that standing. Termination requires a change recognized by the practice governing it; correction requires grounds for rejecting the original attribution.

The qualification “by itself” is essential. A denial is not merely a refusal of agreement. It may provide new evidence: the speaker may identify a misheard word, an overlooked qualification, an absent authority, or a difference between the task accepted and the task now attributed. If those grounds show that the original standing never arose, the proper description is correction of an attribution. Nothing that actually arose has been erased.

A standing that did arise may also end. The promised act may be performed, its specified condition may fail, its deadline may define a limited permission, or the person holding a claim may release it. Some acts give their authors a unilateral power of withdrawal. These are changes in what participants owe or may do, rather than discoveries that the original exchange was misclassified.

Does the proposed constraint merely say that a standing persists unless it stops persisting? No. It excludes a particular alleged basis of termination: the bare loss of agreement that the standing was generated. It places a burden on someone who takes that loss to discharge an obligation. They must identify a relevant power or condition, rather than derive freedom from the absence of shared endorsement.

An *acknowledgment test* helps expose the difference. Suppose Noor offers a reason why she is now free of her undertaking. Replace her denial with acknowledgment: “I did undertake it, but this later change has released me.” Does the cited change still explain why she is released?

If Lin has said that she no longer needs Noor to open the workshop, the explanation survives. If their arrangement allowed either of them to cancel before Friday, Noor’s timely notice can also survive the test. Her having performed the undertaking is compatible with her subsequently leaving it under its terms.

But “I no longer agree that I undertook it” does not, without an additional norm, explain release in the acknowledged case. It is a change in Noor’s attitude to the history. To make it a change in Lin’s claim requires a power that we have not yet been given reason to recognize.

The test is diagnostic, not a decision procedure. It does not apply to a denial offered as evidence that there was no undertaking; replacing that denial with acknowledgment would remove the very ground of the correction. Nor does it show that no practice could grant speakers a power of retrospective annulment. It makes the theorist identify that power and explain its limits. It blocks an inference from present disagreement alone.

The constraint is also compatible with an objective fact that remains hard to discover. In Settlement, Lin can possess a claim whose existence she cannot establish to a bystander. In Interruption, she can be reasonably mistaken about possessing one. The continuity rule does not turn either person’s reasonable interpretation into the truth. Its antecedent concerns successful formation, not confidence that formation succeeded.

## 4. What openness can and cannot establish

The strongest reply appeals to interpretive openness. Perhaps the original sentence admits both a prediction and an undertaking, and this very openness conventionally preserves a right to deny the latter. An explicit “I promise” would close the option; Noor chose words that leave it open.

This reply must distinguish openness of a sentence from openness of a completed exchange. Noor’s first sentence supports different uses in different settings. In Settlement, subsequent contributions give it a more determinate role. If the speaker can cooperate in resolving an utterance and then recover every option available before that resolution, the audience’s contribution never settles anything beyond a provisional moment. The theory then needs an account of why ordinary participants treat some understandings as completed.

There is an appropriate concession here. A defender may say that Settlement falls outside the deniability rule because the later confirmation closes the relevant interpretive option. That reply accepts the constraint in this case. More significantly, it accepts that a token’s available reinterpretations must be limited by its completed history, not merely by the conventional meanings of its sentence. An original absence of an explicit performative formula cannot by itself preserve a permanent exit.

Camp already gives a reason to resist a purely verbal criterion. Her account distinguishes the public conversational record from local common ground, allows commitments to enter that record without explicit articulation, and constrains an admissible denial by commitments undertaken in the conversation. This is a substantial antecedent to the present argument. The distinction between common ground and liability is not being proposed here for the first time. [Camp 2018, pp. 50, 58–61.](https://www.elisabethcamp.org/Papers/Camp.Insinuation.pdf)

The remaining problem concerns cases that retain some interpretive openness even after uptake. Ambiguity is not always completely eliminated. Does my argument assume it away?

It does not require perfect certainty about an act. Consider an undertaking made in ordinary language, with a small unresolved question about whether “eight” allows a few minutes’ delay. That remaining uncertainty need not unsettle whether the speaker accepted responsibility for opening the workshop. Openness has dimensions. Uncertainty about exact punctuality does not automatically preserve a right to redescribe an undertaking as a forecast.

Where the force itself remains unresolved, the continuity rule cannot establish that a definite standing arose. We should keep that case open. The rule is consequently less convenient than a universal presumption in favour of either speaker or hearer. It requires asking what was settled, with what qualifications, and by which contributions. But such questions are exactly what the present-disagreement rule wrongly threatens to make unnecessary.

## 5. The difference between release and escape

Even if a standing survives, a denial may make it harder to enforce. This fact explains much of the attraction of deniability without showing that denial extinguishes anything.

Dinges and Zakkou distinguish an epistemic barrier to treating a speaker’s communicative intention as a reason from a practical barrier to acting against the speaker. Their account also permits different reactions to have different epistemic requirements. Peet develops a related epistemic problem by separating strong evidential probability from knowledge. These are existing ways of explaining why someone can avoid being held responsible; my argument concerns the further inference to no longer being responsible under the relevant practice. [Dinges and Zakkou 2023, §§6–8](https://juliazakkou.net/Dinges_Zakkou_OnDeniability_Mind.pdf); [Peet 2024, §§1–3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-024-04600-4).

Suppose Noor’s denial introduces a genuine possibility of misunderstanding. Lin may need to postpone an accusation. That is a change in what it is epistemically appropriate for Lin to do. Or suppose Lin remains sure, but pursuing the dispute would wreck an important friendship. She may decide not to press her claim. That is a change in what pursuing it would cost. Neither outcome entails that there is no undertaking left to perform or release.

The difference can be tested without appealing to dramatic harm. Lin can waive a small undertaking even if she has suffered no loss and would never confront Noor. That power belongs to her because she is its recipient. A bystander’s confident announcement that no undertaking occurred is not normally a substitute for her release. Conversely, Lin’s acceptance that Noor made a harmless prediction does not give her a power to waive a promise that was never made.

These asymmetric powers help distinguish a speech-generated claim from a general duty not to mislead. If all that survived denial were a generic responsibility for causing confusion, it would be difficult to explain why this particular audience member possesses a power to release this particular task. We would also lose the difference between “Please let me off” and “Please stop accusing me.” Their practical effects can overlap, but the first recognizes an authority in the hearer that the second contests.

None of this establishes that all illocutionary standings are moral claims of the same strength. It establishes a need to keep their existence separate from the social success of a challenge. A speaker may get away with refusing an undertaking. A theory should explain that success without building the speaker’s preferred description into the criterion for whether there is anything to get away with.

## 6. Correction without permanent captivity

A concern now presses from the other direction. Communication is fallible. If earlier uptake establishes a durable standing, does a speaker become trapped by an audience’s first reasonable interpretation?

No. Three cases deserve different treatment.

First, an interpretation may be corrected while the original act is still being worked out. Had Noor replied to Lin’s assignment, “No—please keep looking; I only expect to be nearby,” the candidate undertaking would not have been completed. The difference from Settlement is not the moral quality of her motive. It is the difference between withholding agreement during formation and repudiating an arrangement already formed.

Second, later information may show that apparent uptake was defective. Perhaps they were discussing different Saturdays, and neither understood the other’s proposed date. Or perhaps Noor’s apparent confirmation was directed at a separate remark she heard in the room. These possibilities are not excuses merely because they favour the speaker. If supported, they can undermine the claim that the required agreement ever occurred. The continuity rule presupposes successful formation; it does not manufacture it out of an observer’s first impression.

Third, a speaker may have a power to withdraw a standing that was successfully created. Suppose someone permits a neighbour to borrow a bicycle until the permission is withdrawn. Withdrawal changes what future use is permitted. It does not show that yesterday’s permitted borrowing was unauthorized. Continuing permission and the historical occurrence of granting permission are different objects of evaluation.

Assertion presents a further case. A speaker who asserted that the workshop opens at eight can discover that it opens at nine and retract the earlier claim. It would be absurd to require continued defence of a proposition she now has good reason to reject. But retraction does not make an accurate report that she asserted it false. Nor must it eliminate every consequence of that assertion. If a listener is about to make a wasted journey on its basis, reasonable correction can remain called for.

The norm must therefore track particular standings rather than preserve an undifferentiated quantity of “responsibility.” Withdrawal may end a current demand to defend a proposition while leaving an obligation to correct an error, or an entitlement to ask why the assertion was made. A release may end a claim to performance while leaving an accurate historical description intact. Completion can be durable without every consequence being permanent.

This also shows why no fixed number of conversational turns can define the boundary. A hurried exchange may remain unsettled after several responses. Another may achieve understanding almost immediately. My proposal does not add a universal deadline after which clarification becomes invalid. It adds a question: is the later move completing or correcting an earlier interaction, or exercising a power over standings that the interaction has already produced? The answer depends on the practice, the parties’ contributions, and their evidence.

## 7. A descriptive constraint, not a moral shortcut

A final objection says that I have confused desirable norms with actual ones. Perhaps ordinary speech does give speakers an unattractive escape. A theory of that practice should describe it, even if we wish things were otherwise.

The objection is right about the method. That a rule allows bad behaviour is insufficient to show that it is not a conversational rule. My argument has not depended on the desirability of punishing Noor. It has depended on distinctions internal to ordinary practice: withholding an undertaking differs from seeking release; evidence of a past exchange differs from making a new one; a recipient can waive a claim that a bystander cannot; and participants can remain uncertain about an outstanding obligation after recognizing their disagreement.

These are defeasible data. A community might explicitly treat all loosely worded assurances as revocable through denial. Within that practice, the speaker’s power would be a real part of what the assurance originally created. The continuity rule would not override it. But the escape would then be explained by the independently established rule of revocation, rather than by the general nature of common ground. Evidence that people reluctantly let a denial pass would not by itself establish that rule. It would leave open whether they had lost confidence, avoided a costly dispute, or released a claim.

Nor does my account give hearers sovereign authority. Lin cannot make Noor responsible simply by refusing to accept clarification. Lin’s confidence may exceed her evidence. A prejudice may have distorted her interpretation. Her demand may concern a task Noor never accepted. The appropriate assessment must examine the grounds of formation and the grounds of repair. Speakers retain authority to explain their intentions, but evidence about an intention and a power to revoke another person’s claim are different resources.

One may also object that a history-sensitive account adds nothing to a sufficiently rich conversational score. That is correct in one sense. A score can track prior acts, competing attributions, evidence of defects, and valid releases. It can thereby implement the distinction defended here. The objection would be decisive only if the dispute were over how many abstract records to posit. It is not. The disputed issue is which transition rules those records obey. A rich score must not silently translate “no longer jointly accepted” into “no longer binding.”

The account accordingly remains compatible with substantial collaboration. An utterance’s force need not be fixed at its first production, and a hearer can contribute to determining what the speaker does. Once their interaction has generated standings, however, later negotiation must respect the distribution of powers it generated, unless there are grounds for treating that distribution as invalid. Collaboration is a way to create a claim; it need not be a requirement that its holder continually secure the debtor’s consent to possessing it.

## 8. What the later denial leaves open

The proposal is deliberately limited. It supplies no general algorithm for settling contested speech acts and no exhaustive account of deniability. Its contribution is a temporal constraint on the move from disagreement to release, supported by the uncertainty problem and made usable by the acknowledgment test.

For an apparent escape through clarification, we should ask what happened to the earlier standing. Was the original attribution undermined? Was an established power of withdrawal exercised? Did the recipient release a claim? Or did the denial merely change what could comfortably be presupposed, proved, or pursued? The last possibility can be socially effective while leaving the underlying undertaking intact.

When Lin and Noor cease to agree, their conversation has changed. What Noor owes depends on more than that change. It depends on what they had already done, and on what their practice allows them to do next.
