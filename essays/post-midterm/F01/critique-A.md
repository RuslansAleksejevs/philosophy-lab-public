# Critique A — F01: *Answerability Without Surveillance*

## Outcome and limits

**Outcome: potentially defensible, with a major unresolved step in the justification of the positive duty.** The narrow claim is persuasive: justified noncollection does not, without more, justify rejecting a claim. The broader claim—that an undertaking and procedural control generate a distinct duty to arrange protection against verification failure—needs a clearer account of what makes that duty obligatory and what satisfies it. I find no fatal contradiction and no basis for setting the essay aside outright.

## 1. Charitable reconstruction

The thesis is not that privacy is undesirable, every complaint is true, or every unresolved claim deserves payment. It is a prospective institutional-design claim, summarized in §3, line 59, and limited again at §7, lines 135–139.

The argument can be reconstructed as follows:

1. **An existing obligation matters before disputes arise.** Undertaking a service obligation gives reasons to prepare for predictable difficulties in fulfilling it (§3, lines 43–47).
2. **The institution controls an important dependency.** It shapes the terms, admissible evidence, and available challenge. A substantively identical promise has different practical significance depending on the procedure attached to it (§3, lines 49–51).
3. **A justified evidential constraint leaves a separate decision.** Excluding recording does not determine how residual error should be allocated. Testimony, review, limited guarantees, and risk sharing can be assessed without reversing the privacy decision (§3, lines 53–55).
4. **Therefore, reasonable preparation is owed.** The institution must assess permissible alternatives and cannot justify a systematically self-favouring default solely by its reason for excluding evidence (§3, lines 59–63).
5. **The design obligation has its own trigger.** An institution can breach it before any substantive misconduct is established. A remedy under an owed uncertainty arrangement therefore need not entail that an adviser committed the alleged wrong (§4, lines 69–79).

The central fictional case supplies unusually strong facts: categorical exclusion of the user's account; a documentary requirement the service cannot ordinarily satisfy; no appeal; and an affordable, privacy-respecting alternative without comparably serious injustice (§1, lines 13–19). Those facts support criticism of that particular arrangement more strongly than they support a general requirement to insure unresolved claims.

## 2. Strengths worth preserving

- **The separation of permission, truth, and practical response is real.** Whether collection was permissible, whether a breach occurred, and what a procedure should do are different questions (opening, lines 5–7; §2, lines 29–37). A payment need not be a factual verdict.
- **The undertaking is not dismissed as logically unreal.** Line 51 expressly recognizes that an unenforceable right can exist. This avoids confusing an objection to institutional conduct with a logical claim about rights.
- **The employee/organization distinction is important.** Lines 75–79 prohibit treating a protective refund as evidence of personal misconduct and address double recovery. This substantially improves the moral proposal.
- **Costs and false claims are not simply ignored.** Lines 89–93, 109, and 117–123 acknowledge mistaken payments, service viability, effects on other participants, and eligibility conditions. A criticism assuming unconditional compensation would miss these concessions.
- **The cited theories are used cautiously.** The essay does not claim Gardner already proves the prospective duty (line 47), does not infer a general requirement to archive everything from evidential damage (lines 27–33), and stipulates rather than purports empirically to establish the case's privacy constraint.

## 3. Concerns, by severity

### C1 — Major: a reason to prepare is not yet an owed procedural obligation

**Draft locations:** §3, lines 43–47, 51, 59–63; §4, line 71; §7, line 139.

Line 45 gives an institution a *reason* to provide reasonable means. Line 59 gives it a *duty*, owed in a way that can support an individual complaint independently of the substantive breach. Line 63 says that undertaking, controlled dependence, and foreseeable difficulty jointly explain this duty. The missing work concerns why their conjunction is sufficient, not whether they are morally relevant.

The loan analogy at line 43 is strongest where one's preparation deliberately makes actual performance unlikely. But declining to guarantee an unresolved allegation is not necessarily arranging to perform only when convenient. The institution might prepare conscientiously to provide good advice, pay whenever it knows the refund condition occurred, and use a genuinely fair procedure, while refusing to pay when that procedure cannot determine whether the condition occurred. Some reason for additional protection remains; it does not follow without argument that further protection is owed.

The supplied Gardner passage makes precisely this distinction. In §5, tool lines 1242–1283, preparatory acts have supporting reasons without each becoming obligatory. At lines 1259–1261 he says, “None of these is itself an obligatory action.” Footnote 53, lines 1294–1298, allows a possible derivative obligation where a means is necessary as well as sufficient; the offered examples deliberately exclude necessity. This does **not** refute an obligation to choose *some* adequate arrangement rather than any particular precaution. It does show why the cited distinction cannot itself supply the duty asserted here.

**Strongest defense:** line 51 is more than an instrumental-efficiency claim. By voluntarily controlling others' access to performance, the institution acquires a relational duty not to make its undertaking practically hollow. It may choose among methods, but cannot choose none. This is a plausible basis for a disjunctive design duty.

**What remains:** the essay should defend that relational premise and distinguish an undertaking made practically hollow by obstruction from an undertaking with a fair but fallible route to enforcement. Mere control and foreseeable uncertainty are also present in many reasonable, inexpensive arrangements. The issue is not a demand for formal deduction or a complete moral theory. It is identifying the additional reason that makes this particular institutional preparation obligatory rather than merely beneficial.

### C2 — Major for a stronger reading: the example does not establish protection beyond fair consideration

**Draft locations:** §1, lines 15–23; §2, lines 33–37; §3, lines 59–61; §5, lines 87–99; §6, lines 119–123.

Two defects are combined in the central case: an institution refuses relevant testimony, and it gives no residual benefit when proof fails. An ordinary duty to assess claims honestly could condemn the first defect without establishing an additional entitlement to an uncertainty guarantee. The contrast at line 21 changes this slightly: both cooperatives listen, but only one refunds unresolved complaints. Line 23 correctly concedes that this does not show the paying policy is better. It shows only that privacy does not settle their difference.

Consider this **counterexample to a stronger interpretation**, not an empirical claim. A member-owned confidential advice service accepts both accounts, uses an impartial reviewer, and pays the factual refund whenever a breach is established. Some disputes remain genuinely balanced. Members have meaningfully chosen, after considering alternatives, a low-cost scheme that leaves these cases unpaid. A modest guarantee is financially possible, but its costs and incentives would substantially burden other low-income members. Confidentiality is justified. The refund obligation still depends on what actually happened, not just on what can be proven.

This institution has an undertaking, substantial procedural control, foreseeable verification failures, and a technically feasible alternative. Yet no clear injustice follows from its reasoned decision not to pay balanced cases. The default benefits whoever currently holds the fee, but that description alone does not show an unfair allocation. The incidence, likelihood, incentives, and accepted terms matter.

**Important concession to the essay:** lines 91, 97, and 121 already allow much of this case. Consequently, it is not a counterexample to the minimal requirement to assess alternatives. Nor does it vindicate the original cooperative's categorical rejection of user accounts. It defeats only a reading on which the features at line 59 normally require a monetary or other residual benefit simply because such a benefit is possible.

**What remains:** the positive duty should have a recognizable completion condition. Can genuinely fair consideration, followed by justified nonpayment of every still-unresolved case, discharge it? If yes, the duty is principally a duty of fair decision and design, not a general duty to share residual losses. If no, the essay needs an argument the counterexample presently resists. The text sometimes speaks as if practical claim protection must survive evidential exhaustion (lines 5, 35, 73, 139), while its concessions permit restrictive outcomes. This is an unresolved strength-of-thesis issue, not a direct contradiction.

### C3 — Minor/uncertain: a design breach does not identify a particular retrospective benefit

**Draft locations:** §4, lines 71–79; §5, lines 89–93.

Line 73 is explicitly conditional: *suppose* a limited guarantee should have existed. Under that supposition, furnishing its omitted benefit is plausible. It would be unfair to accuse the paragraph of directly inferring that every procedural failure requires a refund.

Nevertheless, most of the essay establishes at most an obligation to choose among several arrangements. If either independent review or a guarantee would have been permissible, failing to choose either does not immediately establish that a particular claimant was owed the guarantee rather than review. Likewise, an eligibility rule would still need to be satisfied, and an institution-wide design defect need not affect all participants in the same way.

**Test case:** assume a cooperative should have installed independent review but did not. A later reviewer would have rejected one complaint on strong contrary testimony, without proving the complainant dishonest. The complainant was denied an owed process, but was not thereby owed the payment an alternative guarantee would have delivered.

The essay's own phrasing can accommodate this. The unresolved issue is how an appropriate remedy is selected when the duty specified no unique design. This is minor for the core prospective thesis, but potentially major for an application seeking particular payments from the existence of a procedural breach alone. The careful distinction between procedural and substantive wrongs should be retained rather than stretched into an automatic remedy rule.

### C4 — Minor: the general statement needs its limitations kept visible

**Draft locations:** §3, lines 49, 59; §5, lines 95–97; §6, lines 111, 127–131; §7, line 137.

The boxed requirement names undertaking and substantial control. The closing formulation also names foreseeable difficulty and feasible protection. Other passages allow meaningful risk acceptance, limited resources, independent rights, and emergency exceptions. These qualifications are substantial, not decorative.

A cooperative with genuine member governance still substantially controls its claims procedure as an institution. That control need not create the same unilateral dependence as management's unchallengeable rule in the original case. Likewise, an externally supplied tribunal may be an adequate route without the institution itself creating a replacement scheme. Line 49 points toward practical inability to substitute a procedure, but the general statement does not expressly distinguish these situations.

This is not a request to exempt institutions whenever terms are disclosed; line 131 rightly rejects disclosure as automatically sufficient. It is a request to clarify which combinations of control, dependence, choice, and existing alternatives carry the positive duty. As written, “reasonable” does much of that limiting work without a clear test.

### C5 — Uncertain extension, not a fatal defect: feasible protection cannot simply be monetaryized

**Draft locations:** §4, lines 77–81; §5, lines 89–95; §6, lines 127–129.

The essay expressly warns that refunds are inadequate for bodily security, equal standing, and coercion. That warning is sound and prevents a direct counterexample from severe misconduct. But it also means that the financial example does not yet establish the adequacy of the proposal in those domains.

Even a payment officially marked as agnostic can affect incentives or be informally interpreted as a finding against an adviser. Separating its basis on paper is necessary, not a guarantee that these consequences disappear. The draft already anticipates several such costs. Without empirical information I cannot establish their magnitude, and I do not treat them as defeating the fictional case's stipulated feasibility. They remain limits on generalizing that case, especially where repeated complaints would predictably affect employment or standing.

## 5. What would defeat or narrow the thesis?

**To defeat the narrow central verdict**, an objection must address the stipulated case rather than merely mention possible fraud or expense. It would need to show that categorical rejection of relevant accounts and lack of appeal are justified despite the stipulated available, privacy-respecting alternative and its acceptable burdens; or show why participants owe no relevant claim-consideration duty in that relationship. Neither is established here. Simply vindicating confidentiality does not do that work.

**To defeat the broader positive duty**, a genuine counterexample would preserve undertaking, unilateral procedural dependency, foreseeable verification failures, and a reasonable alternative, yet show that declining all reasonable provision is permissible. The member-choice case above does not preserve that full dependency and is therefore a scope test, not such a complete defeat. The essay deserves that concession.

**To narrow it successfully**, the author could establish that reasonable provision includes fair consideration with a justified claimant-losing default, or identify an additional relational condition that requires residual benefits in some cases. Either route would preserve the main separation between justified ignorance and justified risk allocation. Establishing a remedy for a particular design breach requires a further account of what was individually owed; it does not require pretending the underlying misconduct is proven.

## 6. Balanced judgment and dissent

The strongest sympathetic reading treats the duty as a modest obligation not to make an undertaking practically hollow: assess permissible means, count both sides' costs, and justify the residual default. On that reading, the central example and the reasons in line 51 make the proposal substantially credible. Demanding a universal proof threshold, automatic compensation, or comprehensive institutional theory would exceed its stated ambitions.

The strongest dissent accepts that management's privacy argument is insufficient but denies that the essay has established an independently owed assurance against unresolved performance claims. Obligations to perform, reasons to prepare, and obligations to insure uncertainty are not interchangeable. That dissent survives the present argument insofar as the positive duty is meant to require more than fair assessment and independently justified defaults.

**Final assessment:** preserve the negative principle, the original cooperative verdict, and the distinction between protective benefits and misconduct findings. The positive relational duty is promising but needs the bridge identified in C1 and the scope decision identified in C2. No fatal concern is established. The remedy question and nonfinancial extensions remain limited or uncertain rather than reasons to discard the essay.
