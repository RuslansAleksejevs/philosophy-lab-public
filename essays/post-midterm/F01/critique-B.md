# F01 — Critique B: literature, accuracy, contribution boundary

## Verdict

The draft identifies a real design problem: justified noncollection does not, by itself, justify a claims procedure that always defeats users. The three cited attributions are substantially accurate. I found **no established quotation fabrication or material attribution error** in the manuscript. However, the contribution needs a narrower literature boundary and a stronger argument for its positive duty. **Substantial revision is needed before presenting this as more than a useful constrained synthesis.** This is not a novelty certification.

I read the entire verified manuscript, including its bibliography. Manuscript line numbers below refer to the supplied `author/draft.md`. Source locations and exact consulted extracts are preserved in `sources/consulted_passages.json`; wider captures are not evidence of wider reading.

## 2. Closest predecessors and alternatives

### A. Evidential damage: substantial overlap, remaining difference

**Classification: established nearby framework; likely conceptual overlap, not demonstrated identity.**

The draft's novelty cannot lie simply in recognizing evidential loss, protecting pursuit of claims, planning for uncertainty, or changing proof burdens. Those topics are already inside its principal predecessor. The distinctive candidate is instead this combination: an institution owes a workable uncertainty arrangement **even when no decisive evidential item was owed**, because it undertook an obligation and controls contestation.

That combination is not established by the passages I read, but neither does their absence establish historical novelty. The author must explain why this is not merely an implied professional duty expressed at a more abstract level. The constraint matters only if the new duty can genuinely be met without producing or retaining the prohibited evidence.

Part VI's treatment of ordinary non-negligent gaps is also an objection, not just background. Why does institutional control defeat the reciprocal-risk account when confidentiality benefits both parties? The answer may appeal to deliberately structured dependence rather than causal responsibility for ignorance. It must be argued, not assumed.

### B. Institutional answerability and risk-bearing

**Classification: related but distinct; reduces breadth of the claimed contribution.**

Nissenbaum's *Accountability in a Computerized Society* (1996) links producer control and user exposure to institutional answerability, criticizes liability disclaimers, distinguishes liability from accountability, and considers strict liability without faulty action. Locations in the author-hosted version: pp.11–15, PDF indices 10–14, lines 479–667. Importantly, her strict-liability discussion retains a causal requirement; it is not a payment rule for an unproved failure. [Primary text](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/accountability.pdf).

The essay therefore offers neither the first control-based risk-bearing argument nor a new distinction between compensation and personal blame. Its more specific difference is protecting unresolved contractual claims under independently justified limits on evidence. This predecessor also sharpens a tension: a guarantee can protect a user without itself giving the institution an account of what happened. The essay must clarify which combinations of hearing, review, and assurance satisfy its own conception of answerability.

### C. Epistemic authority and proof rules

**Classification: likely overlap in a general distinction; full comparison unresolved.**

Alex Stein's *On the Epistemic Authority of Courts* (2008), *Episteme* 5(3):402–410, distinguishes epistemic assessments from morally/politically governed allocations of error risk. I consulted the author abstract reproduced in the institutional publication record; full-text attempts failed. [Institutional abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/on-the-epistemic-authority-of-courts/).

This already places limits on presenting the essay's three-way separation of knowledge, assertion, and practical consequences as a newly discovered general separation. The positive institutional duty and privacy case could still add something. **I have not verified the full article's detailed argument**, and the review does not treat an abstract as full-text consultation.

### D. Epistemic reparations

**Classification: related but distinct, not an established duplicate.**

Felix Lambrecht's *Epistemic Reparations and a Hybrid Theory of Reparative Justice* (online 2025; journal volume 2026) addresses compensable epistemic remainders even where a specific epistemic good cannot be restored. Its hybrid theory and epistemic application require a rights-violating wrong: §§3, 6–7, HTML lines 92–106 and 220–258. [Publisher text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02441-6).

The essay's prospective trigger is different. Nevertheless, once its procedural duty has been established and violated, its §4 remedy is itself a response to a wrong. Describe the proposal as independent of **wrongful noncollection or established service misconduct**, not as wholly independent of wrongdoing. The source boundary should distinguish assurance owed at entry from reparation owed after defective design.

### E. Individualized evidence and protection of accused parties

**Classification: relevant alternative pressure, not the closest institutional-design predecessor.**

Thomson's *Liability and Individualized Evidence* (1986), consulted sections II–IV opening, PDF indices 2–10, distinguishes probabilistic support from appropriately individualized evidence and questions moving directly from probability to liability. The focal passages are lines 199–232 and 317–352. [Primary article copy](https://www.marcellodibello.com/mathtrial/resources/ThomsonIndEvidence1986.pdf).

This supplies a reason to preserve the essay's separation of assurance payments from misconduct findings. It does not show that Thomson endorses the proposed guarantee. I did not read the entire article or resolve the wider controversy.

## 3. The crucial inference still needs work

The vulnerable transition is manuscript lines 41–63: undertaking + control + foreseeability yields an **owed adequate arrangement**, rather than merely reasons to prepare or a requirement to defend a default.

A promise to refund if misconduct occurs is not yet a promise to pay whenever misconduct cannot be determined. And a procedure can sensibly consider testimony yet leave a claimant bearing the residual burden. The original cooperative combines several defects—impossible documentary requirements, categorical rejection of testimony, no appeal, and sole reliance on privacy. Condemning that bundle does not isolate the distinct duty concerning the final unresolved case.

Test a harder variant: both parties receive a genuine hearing, neutral review assesses all permissible evidence, the privacy arrangement benefits both, the residual claimant burden is clearly disclosed, and a limited assurance payment remains affordable. **What additional premise makes refusing that payment wrongful?** Feasibility alone does not make every helpful precaution obligatory. The draft needs a criterion for what participants are owed, beyond the fact that management controls a procedure.

This is especially important at line 73. If several permissible schemes satisfy the design duty, failure to select one does not automatically entitle the claimant to the payment from whichever hypothetical scheme is most favorable. Establish either a minimum protection owed across acceptable schemes or a reason that this specific assurance should have been supplied. Otherwise the remedial claim assumes the disputed positive duty in a more demanding form.

The draft already makes useful concessions about fraud, resource limits, waiver, and residual risk. Those are not answers to this objection: they delimit the duty only after its content has been justified.

## 4. What remains and what must change

**What remains:** a clear, restrained argument against using legitimate privacy constraints as a complete defence of systematically unusable claims procedures; a candidate prospective duty grounded in organized dependence; and a useful separation between assurance payments and accusations against individual advisers. This is a plausible contribution in application and combination, not established creation of a new general theory.

**Required revisions, in order:**

1. Confront the same-source alternatives in Porat–Stein Parts V(d) and VI(b), including the reciprocal-risk challenge.
2. Supply the positive-duty premise and test it on the fair-hearing variant, not only the deliberately obstructive procedure.
3. State the minimum content of reasonable provision and the basis of retrospective remedies where multiple compliant designs were possible.
4. Compare the proposal with existing institutional answerability, proof-risk allocation, and epistemic-reparation approaches; distinguish actual full-text consultation from abstract-only evidence.
5. Add exact citation locators and qualify contribution claims as a bounded extension.
