# Answerability Without Surveillance

A service can have a good reason not to record what it does. That does not settle what it owes someone who later says it failed. Yet these questions are easily run together: if keeping the evidence would have been wrong, surely the service cannot be faulted for lacking it; and if the evidence is lacking, surely the complaint may be dismissed. The first inference can be sound while the second leaves something important out.

I defend a limited duty of institutional design. When an institution undertakes obligations to people and controls the practical route for contesting its performance, it must make reasonable provision for foreseeable uncertainty about that performance. Sometimes it should preserve evidence. Sometimes privacy rules that out. In the latter case, it must consider arrangements that protect claims without requiring the missing evidence: different decision rules, independent review of what can permissibly be known, or guarantees that do not depend on a finding of fault. Its reason for remaining ignorant is not, by itself, a reason to place the costs of that ignorance on claimants.

The duty is prospective. It need not arise from wrongdoing in the collection or destruction of information, and its performance need not establish that any particular complaint is true. This separates three questions that deserve different answers: what may be known, what may be asserted, and who must bear the practical consequences of not knowing.

## 1. A service with no record

Consider a fictional cooperative offering confidential advice. Users pay for a session with an adviser. The cooperative promises a refund if the adviser uses the session mainly to sell an unrelated private product instead of providing the requested advice. The refund depends on what happened, not merely on whether the user felt dissatisfied.

The sessions concern sensitive matters. The cooperative reasonably decides against recording their contents. Nor does it require advisers to make detailed notes that would reconstruct the discussion. Suppose, for the case, that less intrusive records cannot reliably distinguish a sales pitch from the promised service. A timestamp establishes that a conversation occurred, but not what it was about. Some users and advisers will consequently disagree, sincerely or otherwise, about whether the refund condition was met.

The cooperative adopts a further rule: a refund requires independent documentary confirmation of the breach. The user's account is never sufficient. The adviser's denial is enough to leave the complaint unconfirmed. There is no appeal. Management answers objections by pointing to its admirable privacy policy.

Something has gone wrong, even if the policy against recording is entirely justified. The cooperative has combined an undertaking that turns on the content of a conversation with a procedure that systematically prevents disputed content from supporting a claim. Privacy explains why a recording is unavailable. It does not explain why the user should therefore always lose.

This is not a claim that every disappointed user deserves a refund. False claims are possible. Advisers have legitimate interests, including an interest in not being falsely accused. The question concerns the arrangement under which those risks are assigned. Could the cooperative consider testimony under a fair procedure? Could it provide a limited guarantee for unresolved complaints while keeping refunds separate from disciplinary findings? Could it alter its pricing and service terms to fund such protection? Suppose some such alternative is affordable, respects privacy, and does not produce comparably serious injustice. Automatic dismissal is then difficult to defend.

A useful contrast makes the issue precise. Imagine two cooperatives with identical confidentiality practices. Both listen to complaints and try to resolve them. One provides a modest, defined refund for eligible disputes that remain unresolved; the other always keeps the fee. The former's additional payments do not require additional knowledge. They express a different allocation of uncertainty. Nothing about the shared reason for confidentiality selects the latter arrangement.

The comparison does not establish that the first cooperative's rule is always best. It establishes that the choice remains to be justified after the privacy question has been settled. Treating the justification of noncollection as the end of the moral discussion hides that choice.

## 2. Beyond wrongdoing against evidence

There is a familiar route from missing evidence to a duty of repair. If I wrongfully destroy information that you needed to establish a claim, I cannot simply regard your resulting difficulty as your misfortune. Ariel Porat and Alex Stein develop a substantial account of evidential damage. They connect the loss of information with impaired pursuit of rights and weakened settlement positions. They also distinguish ordinary bystanders from people whose special relationships require them to protect, sometimes even generate, evidence. Outside such duties, their discussion emphasizes negligence and nonreciprocal risk. These are already resources for resisting an opportunistic use of uncertainty. ([Porat and Stein, 1999 draft, Part IV(a)](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2db3b43m/qt2db3b43m.pdf))

The cooperative case concerns a different gap. No recording was improperly destroyed. On the stipulated facts, making the decisive recording would itself have been objectionable. We cannot explain the duty simply by saying that the institution owes users restoration of information they were entitled to possess. They were not entitled to that recording. The point is also not that every loss of useful evidence is wrongful. An account that reached that conclusion would turn ordinary social life into a demand for continuous documentation.

Privacy gives us positive reasons to resist such a demand. Helen Nissenbaum's account of contextual integrity distinguishes norms governing which information is appropriate in a setting from norms governing its circulation. Information suitable for one relationship is not thereby freely transferable to another. This helps explain why usefulness to an investigator cannot alone establish permission to collect or distribute information. A conversation can be appropriate between participants without being appropriate material for an institution's permanent file. ([Nissenbaum, 2004, Part III(A)–(B)](https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/H.%20Nissenbaum,%20_Privacy%20as%20Contextual%20Integrity.pdf))

The problem therefore cannot be solved by requiring an archive for every possible grievance. It requires separating a duty concerning evidence from a duty concerning answerability. By answerability here I mean a practical arrangement under which a person can challenge whether an institution has met its obligations, with some reasonable prospect of an appropriate response. Merely inviting the person to speak does not suffice if the response is fixed before anything they could permissibly supply is considered.

An institution might satisfy that duty partly through evidence. But an arrangement can also offer protection when evidence runs out. A guarantee is an obvious example: a seller can owe a replacement under a guarantee without anyone knowing that its manufacturing process was defective. Such examples show that practical protection and proof of the underlying failure can come apart. They do not show that every institution owes every conceivable guarantee.

My claim is accordingly narrower than a general right to compensation for uncertainty. In the class of relationships at issue, the institution has already undertaken a substantive obligation and controls the route through which its performance can be challenged. The question is what taking that undertaking seriously requires when permissible methods predictably leave some disputes unresolved.

## 3. Why an undertaking reaches its procedures

The argument has three steps.

First, an undertaking concerns performance in the relevant circumstances, including circumstances in which performance is inconvenient. Suppose I promise to repay a loan on Friday. I do not fulfil that promise by arranging my finances so that I will repay only if doing so becomes effortless. Likewise, a service undertaking should guide the institution's preparation for predictable difficulty in recognizing when performance is due.

This does not mean that a promise logically contains every useful precaution. Some precautions would be excessive; others would violate independent rights. Rather, undertaking an obligation gives the institution a reason to provide reasonable means of meeting it. When an institution organizes a recurring activity, foreseeable disputes about performance belong among those practical difficulties.

John Gardner's discussion of corrective justice supplies a helpful distinction. Obligations are not identical to the reasons supporting them. A particular obligation can become impossible to perform while some of its supporting reasons continue to guide a different action. His discussion also distinguishes an obligation from the preparatory actions that facilitate its performance. I draw on that distinction, without assuming that his account already establishes the institutional duty defended here. The relevant extension is prospective: reasons for honouring an undertaking can guide the arrangement of future disputes before any breach has been established. ([Gardner, 2010 working paper, §5](https://johngardnerathome.info/pdfs/corrective.pdf))

Second, control over the procedure matters. In an ordinary disagreement between strangers, neither may have undertaken to provide the other with a system for resolving it. An institution can occupy a different position. It chooses the service, defines the terms of performance, controls relevant records, decides which evidence its process accepts, and determines whether an independent challenge is available. The participant commonly cannot substitute another procedure while continuing to use that relationship.

Those powers can change the practical content of the undertaking. A refund promise attached to a rule rejecting every available kind of evidence is materially different from the same promise attached to a usable procedure. The difference remains even though, in a strict sense, the cooperative still owes a refund whenever the condition actually occurs. A right can exist without reliable enforcement. The objection is not that unenforceable rights are logically impossible. It is that the party voluntarily organizing this dependence has failed to give an existing obligation an appropriate role in its own conduct.

Third, the justification for removing an evidential option does not automatically justify the distribution of the resulting risk. A privacy duty can exclude recording from the available means. It need not exclude accepting a certain risk of mistaken payment, providing an alternative benefit, or sharing that risk across the institution's activities. These are different actions affecting different interests. They need separate assessment.

The cooperative cannot answer this point merely by repeating that it chose confidentiality for good reasons. Those reasons explain a constraint on its choice of means. Within that constraint, there may still be several feasible arrangements. Selecting the one that makes every unresolved dispute end in its favour requires a further defence.

We can state the resulting requirement as follows:

> When an institution undertakes a substantive obligation and substantially controls the means of contesting its performance, it must make reasonable provision for foreseeable verification failures. If it properly excludes an evidential method, it must assess permissible alternatives, including alternative allocations of uncertainty. It may not justify a systematically self-favouring default solely by the justification for excluding that method.

The requirement has a positive and a negative part. Positively, the institution must prepare an adequate response to a predictable feature of its activity. Negatively, it cannot treat one legitimate constraint as sufficient authority to disregard other feasible forms of protection. Neither part says that the institution must eliminate all uncertainty or that every claimant must prevail.

The positive part is essential. Without it, the conclusion would be only that management had offered a bad argument for its policy. An institution could then replace that argument with silence. The undertaking, controlled dependence, and foreseeable difficulty jointly explain why it owes an arrangement in the first place. The negative part identifies a tempting but inadequate way of claiming to have discharged that duty.

## 4. Two conditions, two obligations

The distinction becomes clearer if we separate the conditions that activate two duties.

The substantive duty in the example is conditional: if the adviser mainly used the session for the prohibited sales pitch, the cooperative owes a refund. A particular user's claim under that rule can remain uncertain.

The procedural duty has a different condition: because the cooperative offers this service under these terms, and predictably controls how contested performance will be assessed, it owes reasonable provision for those disputes. That duty can be present before any adviser has misbehaved. It can be breached even if, by good fortune, every adviser has behaved properly so far.

This is why a remedy for the second failure need not prove the first. Suppose the cooperative should have provided a limited guarantee for eligible unresolved complaints but did not. It may owe the protection that this arrangement would have supplied. We need not first decide that the user was a victim of the sales pitch. The user may instead have been denied the assurance that participants were owed on entering the relationship.

The distinction prevents two mistakes. One is to dismiss every practical demand because the substantive breach is unproved. The other is to treat a protective payment as an admission that the adviser committed that breach. A payment can be required by an uncertainty arrangement while the institution remains agnostic about what occurred. The record should accurately state its basis.

This is particularly important when an adviser and the institution are different parties. An institution's failure to provide an adequate procedure does not establish an individual employee's misconduct. Nor does a refund under a general guarantee provide a reason to publish the employee's name as a wrongdoer. A scheme that protects complainants by manufacturing findings against others would trade one failure of answerability for another.

Where a payment also satisfies the original refund obligation, it should be credited accordingly. If later evidence shows that the session was indeed a sales pitch, a user who has already received the full fee back is not owed a second copy of that fee merely because its basis is now clearer. Conversely, a small assurance payment need not extinguish distinct claims for other established wrongs. What has been paid, what remains uncertain, and what has been released must be kept separate.

This account gives no general permission to purchase freedom to mistreat people. The underlying standards remain in force. A guarantee governs a response to uncertainty; it does not make the conduct that would violate those standards permissible. Nor does every obligation admit a monetary substitute. Where the protected interest is bodily security, equal standing, or freedom from serious coercion, a refund alone may be woefully inadequate. The modest financial example isolates the structure without settling those harder cases.

## 5. What would reasonable provision require?

A complete account of fair procedures would need much more than one principle. Nevertheless, the present requirement excludes identifiable arrangements and directs attention to concrete alternatives.

A procedure should first use relevant information that can permissibly be obtained. Privacy is not a reason to ignore a participant's voluntary account, an adviser's explanation, or nonintrusive evidence that bears on the dispute. Neither side's account must be treated as conclusive. Reasons for finding one more credible than the other should themselves be open to challenge. In recurring institutions, review by someone who does not benefit from rejecting the claim can reduce a particular source of conflict.

Second, the procedure must say what happens when the permitted inquiry ends without resolution. That question should be faced before participants become dependent on a favourable decision. A defined guarantee, a partial payment, or a shared fund may provide protection. Which arrangement is reasonable depends on the seriousness of the interests, the likely frequency of genuine and false complaints, available resources, and the burdens of mistaken decisions on each party.

It does not follow that error must be divided equally by number. One erroneous refusal might threaten a person's basic security while one erroneous payment imposes a minor cost on a well-resourced institution. In another case, a flood of easily fabricated claims might end a valuable service. These differences matter. The principle requires a justification responsive to them, not a mechanically equal split.

Third, institutions should count the costs of uncertainty honestly. A policy of never paying unresolved claims can look inexpensive because the losses of truthful claimants disappear from the institution's accounts. They do not disappear from the moral assessment. Equally, a policy of always paying can conceal costs shifted to other participants. The relevant comparison includes both, together with the costs of permissible investigation and the rights that limit it.

Fourth, choosing an alternative arrangement should not be a disguised demand for a privacy waiver. Suppose the cooperative offers users two options: accept the possibility of an uncompensated breach or permit permanent recording of the most sensitive parts of the session. If a reasonable, less intrusive guarantee is available, this menu omits an option the institution has reason to provide. The mere fact that users choose from the restricted menu does not vindicate its construction.

Still, participants can sometimes validly choose to bear an unusual share of uncertainty. Friends may arrange an informal loan with minimal documentation. Members of a small cooperative may knowingly accept a modest common risk to keep a service affordable. The present argument does not erase those possibilities. Such choices must actually concern the risk being allocated; a general agreement to confidentiality is not automatically agreement to forfeit every disputed entitlement. One consents to different things in consenting not to be recorded and in consenting to lose a claim whenever a recording would have helped.

These considerations make the cooperative's original policy objectionable under the assumptions given. It requires a kind of proof the relationship properly excludes; it rejects other relevant accounts categorically; it offers no independent challenge; and a feasible alternative could materially protect valid claims without a comparably serious cost. That conclusion is firmer than the claim that any particular percentage refund is uniquely required. It should be.

## 6. Three objections

**The institution did nothing wrong by remaining ignorant. Why charge it for doing the right thing?**

This objection treats every duty involving money after uncertainty as a penalty for causing it. But institutions incur costs through permissible choices all the time. A shop that properly decides to provide wheelchair access may incur construction costs without having wronged anyone by choosing an accessible entrance. The moral reason for the choice and responsibility for arranging its costs are different issues.

Likewise, the cooperative is not punished for respecting privacy. It must organize an undertaking so that foreseeable ignorance does not make its own position automatically decisive. The duty arises from the relationship it undertakes and the control it exercises, not from a verdict that confidentiality was negligent.

Nor must the institution ultimately bear every monetary cost. It may fund a reasonable arrangement through transparent prices or contributions, subject to other requirements of fairness. The claim is about who must ensure that an arrangement exists and justify its terms. It is not a claim that every expense must remain with a particular corporate balance sheet. Indeed, spreading a cost across a voluntary activity may be preferable to leaving it with the particular person whose justified claim happens to be unverifiable.

There is an important limit. An organization responding to an unforeseeable emergency may lack both advance control and a feasible alternative. The failure to provide what could not reasonably have been arranged is not the failure described here. A general social duty to assist might still arise, but it would require another argument.

**Doesn't this let claimants turn ignorance into money?**

It can, in the limited sense that an assurance arrangement may provide a benefit without proof of the underlying breach. That is not enough to discredit it. The proper question is whether it is a justified way of protecting people against uncertainty, including the possibility of deception.

A guarantee can have eligibility conditions. A claimant must establish the relevant relationship and identify the disputed performance. An independent assessment can consider contradictions, relevant patterns, and available contrary evidence. The arrangement can limit the benefit and respond to demonstrated deception. None of this requires pretending that an unresolved complaint is a proven one.

There will remain a tradeoff. A system that protects some truthful people whose claims cannot be verified may also pay some mistaken or dishonest claimants. A system that never makes such a payment may systematically fail truthful people. Refusing to choose openly between these risks is itself a choice, usually in favour of whoever already holds the resource.

The possibility of fraud can nevertheless justify rejecting a proposed guarantee. Imagine that an anonymous demand cannot even be linked to a transaction, or that one claimant can repeatedly submit the same demand under fresh identities. That may make the scheme unreasonable. The answerability requirement does not instruct the institution to adopt it regardless. It requires consideration of feasible alternatives and permits a restrictive outcome when independently justified. What fails is the move from 'we properly lack decisive evidence' to 'there is therefore nothing more to arrange.'

This concession leaves a substantive demand. Where some modest eligibility check and limited protection are feasible, mere appeal to possible fraud is no more decisive than mere appeal to privacy. Institutions must compare the actual forms of protection available, not compare an ideal of infallible proof with the worst imaginable guarantee.

**If no fair alternative is feasible, does the principle forbid the service?**

Sometimes an undertaking should not be made on terms that cannot responsibly be supported. If a provider offers a highly consequential guarantee while knowing that every dispute will be practically insoluble and that no alternative protection can be supplied, the value of the service does not automatically vindicate that offer. It may need to promise something narrower, create collective protection, or refrain from that activity.

But inability to provide ideal protection does not always forbid proceeding. A valuable activity can operate with openly acknowledged residual risk. The requirement is reasonable provision, not perfect verification. Participants may have strong reasons to accept a limited scheme. Where their basic interests are at stake, public or collective support may be appropriate. Nothing in the argument makes the individual institution the sole possible bearer of every supporting duty.

The harder question is when openly announced limits become acceptable rather than merely candid. Disclosure alone cannot answer it: people can knowingly enter unfair arrangements because alternatives are worse. Yet the present argument need not resolve every such case. It identifies a prior mistake that survives disagreement about the full standard of fairness. Neither privacy nor disclosure supplies, by itself, an adequate reason for making an institution's obligations ineffective whenever their violation is disputed.

## 7. The moral importance of an unresolved answer

The account changes how we should describe some failures. When an institution properly avoids collecting information, the first question should not always be whether someone damaged a body of evidence. We should also ask what arrangements were owed for living with its predictable absence. A failure can lie in the design of the relationship even when neither wrongful noncollection nor the alleged substantive breach has been established.

That diagnosis is deliberately limited. It does not establish a universal standard of proof, a general entitlement to payment for any uncertainty, or a complete account of the duties of informal relationships. Its central case involves an institutional undertaking, substantial control over contestation, foreseeable difficulty, and feasible protection. Remove those features and the argument may no longer apply.

Within that case, however, an important conclusion follows. The duty to provide a fair response need not wait for proof of the very event whose proof the institution's legitimate design makes difficult. Otherwise the protection would disappear exactly where it is needed. Its basis lies in a different, ascertainable fact: the institution has undertaken to organize this relationship on terms that recognize participants' claims.

More information can improve a procedure. It can also make a relationship worse by turning it into a site of surveillance. Moral seriousness about claims therefore cannot consist simply in maximizing what can later be proved. It must include seriousness about how people will be treated when appropriate limits on knowledge leave them with an unresolved answer.
