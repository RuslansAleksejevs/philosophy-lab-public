# Author response to the two archived reviews

This is one editor response followed by one revised manuscript, not a further review cycle. Both reviews were read in full, as was the complete assigned draft and bibliography. The responses below explain the revision decisions before R1 is written. They do not certify improvement, eligibility, or historical originality.

## Revision decision

I accept the shared central objection. The draft moves too quickly from reasons to prepare for disputes to an individually owed arrangement, and sometimes lets an arrangement slide into a guarantee. I retain a positive design duty but narrow its content. Its basis is a defended relational premise about exercising control over other people's existing claims, not an inference that every useful preparation is obligatory. It requires a usable, conflict-controlled hearing and a substantively defensible residual rule. It does not invariably require payment after a fair inquiry ends without resolution.

The revised argument therefore relinquishes the stronger suggestion that affordable assurance is normally owed simply because it is affordable. That is a substantive narrowing, not a claim that the reviewers merely misunderstood the original. The fair-hearing case can discharge the duty. An assurance can be one necessary component in particular circumstances, but that needs a further comparative argument.

## Review A

### C1: Reasons for preparation do not establish an owed duty — accepted

R1 §3 supplies an additional premise: when an institution makes people practically dependent on its exercise of power over claims they already hold against it, it owes them a non-self-exempting exercise of that power. The premise is defended by distinguishing conditional entitlement from the institution's authority to decide whether it will recognize that entitlement. The latter cannot be treated as unrestricted discretion simply because the former is uncertain. A usable claims route is owed as a class of arrangements; no specific preparation follows automatically.

The review correctly identifies Gardner's limitation. New consultation confirms it in §5, typescript pp.37–38 and n.53. R1 does not use continuity of reasons to derive this duty. The relational premise remains contestable; the manuscript identifies what a dissenter would have to deny instead of presenting it as a theorem about promises.

### C2: The obstructive case does not establish residual assurance — accepted and narrowed

R1 §§4–5 separates hearing from allocation of residual risk. A genuine hearing, an adequate check on conflicts, and an independently justified claimant-losing default can satisfy the duty without an assurance payment. The member-owned, low-cost service is expressly treated as potentially permissible. Financial possibility does not establish proportionality, and mutual confidentiality is a relevant benefit.

I do not infer that every disclosed default is fair. Nor do I retreat to management's duty merely to offer an explanation. Rules that knowingly exclude available relevant testimony fail an operational requirement. A residual rule also fails where it preserves serious avoidable claimant losses for institutional convenience alone despite a privacy-respecting alternative with only minor burdens and no comparable competing claim. R1 offers a qualitative exclusion test, not a uniquely optimal distribution or a universal refund percentage.

### C3: A design failure does not identify an omitted payment — accepted

R1 §6 withdraws any general move from failure to choose a compliant design to entitlement under the most claimant-friendly hypothetical design. Where review was owed, the immediate response is a proper review, if still possible, and correction of the arrangement. Where a determinate assurance was separately owed, its eligibility conditions matter. A minimum cash entitlement would require independent grounds showing that no permissible design could omit it; the essay establishes no universal minimum.

The review's complaint that would have been rejected on strong contrary evidence is an explicit test. Denial of its hearing is a wrong, but not proof that the fee should have been returned. Irretrievably lost procedural opportunities remain a remedial difficulty, not a concealed refund entitlement.

### C4: Scope, governance, and existing alternatives — accepted

R1 §§3–5 makes practical dependence, rather than institutional size or record ownership alone, central. A genuinely accessible external tribunal can supply the needed check. Meaningful member governance can justify a residual rule. A formally available route that is prohibitively costly for an ordinary small claim is not automatically an effective substitute. Emergencies without advance capacity fall outside the stated prospective duty.

The duty is addressed to the institution organizing that dependence. It need not operate every reviewing body itself, and its administrative expenses can be funded through fair prices or contributions. Disclosure, consent to confidentiality, and acceptance of a loss-bearing default remain distinct.

### C5: Nonfinancial interests and effects on advisers — accepted as limits

R1 §§4, 6–7 keeps protective payment separate from a misconduct finding. It adds that labels do not eliminate predictable reputational effects or distorted incentives. Those effects belong in assessing the arrangement. A refund cannot establish sufficient protection for serious coercion, bodily harm, or unequal standing. The fictional financial case is not evidence about actual fraud rates, service viability, employment consequences, or those wider domains.

### Source check and overall dissent

The source checks do not establish a fatal contradiction. I preserve the privacy constraint as a stipulation and the narrow rejection of the cooperative's documentary-only procedure. I accept the dissent against automatic assurance, but reject the further conclusion that failure of that stronger argument leaves only criticism of management's stated reason. Under the relational premise defended in R1 §3, an institution owes an actual claims arrangement even when no service breach has yet occurred.

## Review B

### 1. Attribution and exact locations — accepted, with an additional version correction

The original attributions were largely supported, but the newly consulted Gardner cover identifies the file as an author eprint of the 2011 article, copyright 2010, not simply an unpublished 2010 working paper. The publisher confirms *Law and Philosophy* 30:1–50, published online in 2010. R1 corrects the entry and expressly uses typescript, not journal, pagination. Publisher full text was not available in the inspected preview.

Nissenbaum's privacy discussion remains support for contextual constraints, not proof of the fictional recording prohibition. R1 cites Part III(A)–(C), printed pp.138–145, and distinguishes actual from justified practice. Exact displayed web passages and PDF indices are retained in the new source record; they are not publisher-original bytes.

### 2A. Porat–Stein overlap and counterevidence — accepted

New reading confirms special evidence duties, the fault-independent cost-avoider alternative, the final preference for negligence, and reciprocity of non-negligent gaps (Parts IV–VI). R1 §§2,5 accepts that counterevidence: its duty concerns controlled claims arrangements, not compensation for missing information. A fair reciprocal scheme may leave unresolved claims unpaid. The account may be an institutional extension of special duties; no exclusive contrast or novelty verdict follows.

### 2B–2E. Other predecessors — accepted with bounded reliance

R1 §2 compares Nissenbaum's accountability/control discussion, Stein's separation of epistemic assessment and error allocation, Lambrecht's reparative account, and Thomson's individualized-evidence problem. The distinctions are modest: an uncertainty benefit need not resolve the historical facts; strict liability without fault still differs from protection without an established service failure; prospective duties differ from remedies for their breach.

Stein's author-site full-text attempt failed again. His institutional abstract was consulted directly, and the manuscript labels that boundary. There is no detailed full-text comparison. Lambrecht and Thomson were consulted in selected original-text passages, not cover to cover. Their relevance does not entail their endorsement of this proposal. Scanlon remains an unconsulted comparison lead; R1 attributes no argument to him.

### 3. Positive premise, hard variant, and remedies — accepted

These are the same load-bearing issues addressed in A-C1 through A-C3, not separate problems solved by more citations. R1 adds the premise, allows fair hearing plus justified nonpayment to discharge the duty, and limits retrospective payments to separately established entitlements. Where the hard variant supplies only disclosure, a fair hearing, and an affordable guarantee, the facts do not decide whether refusal is wrongful. Reasons concerning claimant losses, false payments, contributors, and governance are still needed. The revision does not invent a categorical verdict from underspecified facts.

### 4–5. Contribution and limits — accepted

R1 claims a privacy-constrained institutional application and a separation of design, assurance, and misconduct remedies. It does not claim a new general discovery about evidence or accountability. Research was limited to sources identified in the assigned lineage and metadata for those same works. No broad novelty search was conducted in this editor stage.

## Unresolved consequences

1. The relational premise is defended but not derived from a complete theory of promises or institutional justice. A dissenter who permits unrestricted private control of disputed entitlements will reject the positive duty.
2. The adequacy test rules out specified failures but does not rank every reasonable scheme or yield a universal payment floor. In borderline cases, the essay cannot establish that a particular guarantee is owed.
3. There is no general measure of compensation for irretrievably lost fair-adjudication opportunities. A design defect alone cannot establish the claimant's financial loss.
4. Real privacy constraints, affordability, abuse, and effects on accused people remain empirically unassessed. The account does not establish adequate remedies for severe nonfinancial wrongs.
5. Stein full text, the published 1997 Porat–Stein article and later book, and the wider promise literature were not consulted. The contribution's historical extent remains unresolved. All inherited and new retrieval failures are retained with their separate provenance.
