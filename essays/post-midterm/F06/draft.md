# Keeping Refusal Possible

A public authority introduces a policy on trial. Its evidence is incomplete, but it offers a familiar assurance: the policy will be reviewed, and people can reject it later. During the trial, however, the authority reorganizes essential services around the new arrangement. At review, the evidence is disappointing. Rejection remains legally available. Yet rejection would now interrupt care, destroy livelihoods, or strand people who lack alternatives. Officials reasonably refuse to inflict those harms. The policy survives.

Something may have gone wrong even if the review was honest and continuation is now the right decision. The authority secured permission for an experiment partly by reserving a later decision. Its implementation may have consumed the practical conditions for making that decision.

I argue that an appeal to provisional authority can create a duty to preserve a morally usable route to refusal. The duty does not require costless reversal, preservation of every existing institution, or automatic termination after bad results. It requires institutions to keep the protection of important human interests sufficiently separate from the survival of the disputed arrangement. Where that separation is foreseeably impossible or too costly, the constraint must enter the justification for starting. It cannot be introduced afterwards as though it were evidence that the experiment deserved to continue.

The argument concerns a limited class of public decisions. It is not a general prohibition on policies that change the future. Its subject is the extra authority claimed when institutions say, in effect, that uncertainty is acceptable because a consequential decision remains open.

## 1. A trial with no acceptable ending

Imagine a county that tests a single system for allocating publicly funded home care. Previously, local teams and care recipients settled schedules together. The proposed system assigns visits centrally. It promises more reliable coverage, but recipients worry that it will remove their control over everyday life. The county authorizes two years of compulsory participation, followed by a public decision on whether central allocation should continue. The prospect of that decision is a significant reason for accepting the trial despite uncertainty.

During implementation, the county closes local scheduling offices, transfers staff, and abandons records needed for independent allocation. These changes save money and make the trial easier to operate. Nobody intends to defeat review. At the end, central allocation has repeatedly failed to accommodate recipients' work and family commitments. A clear majority, including most recipients, favors ending it. But the old teams cannot restart soon, and no other arrangement is ready. Abrupt termination would leave some people without daily assistance. The county therefore continues central allocation.

The problem is not that recipients have become attached to an unexpected benefit. Assume they receive no more care than before. Their need for care also predates the trial. What has changed is the range of ways in which the county can meet that need. Implementation has made an independently important service depend on retaining an arrangement still awaiting approval.

Nor must the county reconstruct the old offices. It might decentralize authority within the new organization, preserve transferable records, or maintain a temporary alternative provider. The relevant object of refusal is central allocation, not every piece of infrastructure used during the trial. A workable transition could preserve what the trial taught and much of what it built.

Contrast a second county. It runs the same trial and obtains the same disappointing results, but maintains the capacity to transfer scheduling authority to local teams over three months. Transition is inconvenient and has a real cost. Still, essential care continues. Review can now decide whether the benefits of central allocation justify its burdens. In the first county, review must also decide whether those burdens are preferable to a crisis that the county itself has made difficult to avoid.

This difference matters before anyone actually votes to reject the policy. If a trial succeeds, retaining the ability to refuse it may look wasteful. But an institution can owe people a usable power even when they never exercise it. An appeal process is not made adequate by the fortunate absence of grounds for appeal. Similarly, a successful trial does not establish that its conditions of authorization were properly respected.

## 2. What provisional authority buys

There are several reasons for authorizing a temporary policy. A temporary measure might last exactly as long as an emergency. A study might be permitted because the knowledge it produces is independently valuable. A legislature might approve an irreversible construction project while requiring annual reports. None of these arrangements necessarily reserves a decision about whether to undertake the underlying commitment.

My argument applies when a later opportunity to reject an arrangement does justificatory work now. Call this a reserved decision. The reservation may be explicit in a public mandate or central to the reasons offered for accepting a trial. A reporting requirement alone is insufficient. So is the mere fact that every statute can, formally, be repealed.

Why does a reserved decision matter? Suppose the case for imposing an arrangement permanently is not yet adequate. A trial can sometimes be justified because it exposes people to a limited burden while gathering evidence for a later choice. The authority thereby obtains permission to act under weaker evidence than a durable commitment would require. What warrants this difference is partly that the initial decision settles less.

But what a decision settles is not exhausted by its legal form. If implementing a two-year trial predictably leaves successors unable to reject the disputed arrangement without abandoning essential obligations, it settles more than two years of exposure. The existence of a future vote cannot, by itself, supply the missing justification. A limited permission has been used to create a practical commitment of greater scope.

The underlying principle is about the division of public authority. Where a decision has been reserved, those authorized to implement the earlier decision must not, without further justification, remove the conditions that make the reservation effective. They must exercise their present power consistently with the future power that helped make its exercise permissible. Otherwise the authority to conduct a trial becomes authority to settle its disputed outcome through implementation.

This is not an inference from temporary permission to a magical duty to stop time. Even a perfectly managed trial changes skills, beliefs, and relationships. People may learn that the policy works, and this may make rejection unattractive. Such effects are compatible with a reserved decision. They supply reasons for using the arrangement, rather than making an acceptable alternative unavailable independently of those reasons.

The difficult effects are those that undermine the practical role of the reservation. The central system might prove valuable because it coordinates care well. That is a reason to retain it on its merits. Alternatively, it might survive because the county destroyed the means of coordinating care any other way. That is a reason to avoid an immediate collapse. The two reasons can support the same action while giving sharply different verdicts on the experiment's justification.

An authority can sometimes justify taking the second kind of risk. Perhaps maintaining any alternative would consume resources needed to prevent grave present harm. But then the case for proceeding must include that possible commitment. The policy cannot receive the full justificatory advantage of a reversible trial while imposing the foreseeable exposure of something less reversible.

## 3. A duty to support refusal

The resulting duty has three elements. First, institutions must identify the decision genuinely reserved for later review. Second, they must assess how implementation could make an adverse decision unusable. Third, they must take proportionate steps to preserve at least one acceptable way of acting on that decision, or obtain adequate authorization for proceeding without it.

Consider an adverse-review test. Imagine a credible outcome of the inquiry in which the disputed arrangement fails for a reason the review is meant to consider. Would the reviewing body then be able to reject that arrangement while meeting the basic obligations it would still owe affected people? If not, which implementation choices create the obstacle, and could reasonable alternatives avoid it?

The imagined verdict must be a live possibility within the purpose of the trial. It is not enough to invent a bizarre objection to every possible policy. A trial of central care allocation might properly investigate reliability and recipients' control. It need not preserve an exit for a later demand that no public care should exist at all. Independent duties to provide care constrain the review. Reserving authority over one question does not delegate unlimited authority over others.

A route to refusal is morally usable when it does not predictably require serious violations of obligations to affected people, or impose burdens grossly disproportionate to the reasons for rejecting the arrangement. This standard is deliberately different from popularity. A transition can be usable despite vocal opposition, lost profits, administrative inconvenience, and reasonable additional taxation. Conversely, it can be unusable even when a majority is willing to sacrifice the small group bearing its costs.

The standard also depends on time. The ability to withdraw within a tolerable transition period can be enough. An immediate stop may be impossible without making the reserved decision empty. But a promise to search indefinitely for an alternative is not equivalent to a practicable transition. The important questions concern resources, authority, responsibility, and credible timing, not whether a document contains the word review.

Preserving a route may require very little. Records may need to remain usable outside the new organization. Powers can be reassigned without destroying the service. A limited reserve can finance transition. Sometimes a smaller initial trial reduces the dependency created. None of these devices is universally required. They illustrate a single demand: do not make avoidable dependence on the disputed arrangement the price of receiving a service people remain entitled to have.

Notice that this duty is narrower than preserving the full menu available before the trial. Two alternatives may be enough where ten once existed. A newly invented arrangement can make refusal possible even if every old arrangement disappears. What must survive is a relevant practical power, not an untouched historical landscape.

The duty is also stronger than disclosure alone. Telling care recipients that the system may become difficult to reject does not necessarily authorize imposing that difficulty. An announcement gives information; it does not itself provide the missing reason to expose them to extended control. If a legitimate decision-making process, with the relevant interests represented and substantive constraints respected, separately accepts a specified risk, that can change what is owed. Merely placing the risk in an appendix cannot.

There will be disagreements about proportionality. The adverse-review test does not eliminate them. Its point is to put a neglected choice on the agenda: how much power to reject are we giving up, and on what grounds? Without this question, the cost of preserving refusal appears as an optional administrative expense, while the cost of losing it enters consideration only after avoiding that cost has become difficult.

## 4. Protecting people without protecting the arrangement

The principle faces a serious apparent conflict. Public authorities should permit people to organize their lives around available institutions. Yet the more securely people rely on a trial, the harder ending it may become. Must a genuinely provisional policy keep its users insecure?

That conclusion confuses security of the service with security of the disputed means of delivering it. In the care example, people should be able to rely on receiving essential assistance. They need not be assured that central allocation will govern them indefinitely. The county's task is to provide continuity across possible institutional change. When it can do so, stronger protection for users makes refusal more practical, not less.

This is why a warning that the program is temporary is often inadequate. A recipient who understands the warning still needs help getting out of bed tomorrow. Participation may be unavoidable. She can have no expectation that the trial will last and nonetheless have a powerful claim against being abandoned when it ends. Her claim need not arise from mistaken confidence in permanence.

A further distinction concerns who controls the risk. Individual users generally cannot preserve an alternative care system. Asking each to insure privately against the failure of public arrangements misunderstands the problem. Their pooled contributions through taxation may help fund transition, but only coordinated institutional action can keep the relevant option available. Where the authority chooses the trial's structure and can spread its costs, it is normally the appropriate bearer of the duty to prepare.

This does not imply that every loss merits full compensation. Officials should distinguish essential interests from profits expected from continued exclusivity, and reasonable reliance from a deliberate effort to force renewal. A provider who knowingly makes an unnecessary investment after clear notice of review does not acquire a veto merely by making termination expensive for itself. Its employees may nevertheless face serious hardship. Their claims should be assessed on their own grounds, not simply inherited from the provider's demand for protection.

Nor must every transition cost be charged personally to officials who planned the trial. That might be unjust, ineffective, or both. The duty initially belongs to the public institution exercising the relevant authority. Specific misconduct can generate further responsibility, but personal fault is not necessary for an institutional duty to preserve refusal. Good intentions do not enlarge the scope of delegated power.

The strongest objection invokes the people who would fund preparation. Keeping an alternative available uses real resources. A reserve for transition might mean fewer care visits today. These people also have important interests. Why should a future opportunity to reject take priority?

It need not always take priority. The claim is a presumption grounded in the way the trial is justified, not an absolute requirement to purchase political flexibility at any price. What is impermissible is an incomplete comparison that counts the reserve as costly while treating meaningful later review as already secured by a voting rule. If present needs defeat the duty to maintain a particular alternative, the initial authorization must be reconsidered with that fact included.

There are then several possibilities: choose a less ambitious trial, preserve a different route, delay, or justify a commitment with more durable consequences. Sometimes the last choice is right. The theory does not forbid taking risks on behalf of citizens. It denies that one can justify a larger risk by describing the legal instrument that imposes it as temporary.

The comparison must also include the costs of continuing existing policy. Suppose local care is already unreliable and humiliating. Preserving every local provider would then protect an unacceptable baseline. My principle provides no such protection. An alternative counts only if it can meet the institution's independent obligations. The county may replace its old arrangements completely while preserving a route from central allocation to another acceptable system.

## 5. Where the argument sits

The idea that political decisions can become difficult to reverse is familiar. Levinson and Sachs distinguish formal barriers from functional means of entrenchment. They also explain why the category becomes difficult to delimit: almost every political choice constrains successors, and stability can produce valuable commitments. Their challenge rules out a general inference from costly change to illegitimate power. My proposal isolates a narrower ground of criticism: an institution invokes a reserved decision to justify acting, then foreseeably undermines that decision's practical conditions. The difference is the structure of authorization, not the bare existence of downstream costs. ([Levinson and Sachs 2015, §§III.A.1, III.B.2](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/political-entrenchment-and-public-law).)

Brown's account locates legitimate expectations in official responsibility for creating them. It includes cases where warnings about possible change undermine claims that officials caused an expectation of stability. The present argument concerns something that can persist after every warning is understood: material dependence without confidence that policy will continue. A person may correctly expect the trial to end and still be unable to secure essential care outside it. Protecting a reserved public decision cannot therefore depend entirely on proving frustrated expectations. ([Brown 2017, accepted manuscript pp.12–18](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65003/1/Accepted_manuscript.pdf).)

Green argues against making legitimate expectations the general basis of assistance through legislative transitions and offers wellbeing and responsibility as alternative considerations. This supports separating people's claims from the preservation of a particular rule. My additional concern is with an earlier decision: obligations created when the practical possibility of later refusal helps authorize a policy. Assistance can be owed not only to soften a transition once chosen, but to keep the authority to choose it effective. ([Green 2020, §§II.B, III–IV](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jopp.12231).)

Dorf and Sabel connect democratic experimentation with learning, institutional revision, and safeguards against abuses of power. They do not treat experimentation as exempt from institutional constraints. The present proposal specifies one such constraint for provisional authority: the production of evidence should not foreseeably disable an acceptable response to adverse evidence. This is compatible with learning through revision; it asks what material preparation makes a particular reserved decision consequential. ([Dorf and Sabel 1998, pp.283–286, 337–340](https://charlessabel.com/papers/demex.pdf).)

These connections limit the claim to originality. I offer neither a discovery of political dependence nor a complete theory of legitimate transition. The proposed contribution is a reason for connecting the two: a public power reserved as part of a policy's justification generates duties concerning how its implementation reshapes the available choices.

## 6. Difficult cases

One objection denies that later rejection needs protection in this special sense. If circumstances change, a reasonable reviewing body should respond to them. Dependence is simply one of the new facts. There is no reason why the initial choice set must remain available.

The objection is correct about the reviewing body's immediate task. It is mistaken about what follows for the earlier institution. Present obligations do not certify the actions that created them. If an avoidable administrative mistake leaves someone in danger, rescuing her is required; the duty to rescue does not vindicate the mistake. Likewise, continuation can be required by dependence without showing that creating that dependence respected the scope of the trial.

More importantly, this distinction has consequences before the mistake occurs. If officials know that they will later be bound to protect users, they must consider how their design will interact with that obligation. Otherwise the duty to protect becomes a predictable mechanism for converting provisional decisions into durable commitments. Nobody needs to exploit it deliberately. The constraint arises from the combination of the design and the duties that any decent successor will recognize.

A second objection concerns successful social reform. A policy can create new expectations, capacities, and constituencies. These changes may help protect valuable achievements. Should a trial of a new public benefit refrain from improving people's lives because improvement makes withdrawal harder?

No. A distinction is needed between discovering or creating a reason to protect a good and unnecessarily tying that good to the disputed arrangement. Suppose a care trial enables recipients to take paid work. Giving up this new independence would be a serious loss. It may reveal a strong reason to continue the level of care. It does not, without further argument, establish a reason to retain compulsory central scheduling. The relevant issue is whether the good can be protected through an acceptable alternative to what remains under review.

Sometimes it cannot. The benefit and the disputed means may genuinely be inseparable. That possibility should narrow the reservation from the outset. A trial can ask whether to expand the system while committing to support those already enrolled. Or it can authorize a specified risk that a valuable benefit, if created, will generate reasons against withdrawal. Such designs must still be justified, but they do not promise an unrestricted future decision while predictably making it unusable.

The distinction is not perfect. Whether an alternative preserves a benefit adequately can be contested, and a duty to maintain opportunities can itself become a barrier to desirable reform. The argument offers no complete theory of which new benefits become protected claims. It requires that this question be faced independently of whether preserving the particular experimental arrangement is politically convenient.

A third objection says that reserved decisions can themselves be unjust. Suppose a majority approves equal access to a service only on condition that a later majority may withdraw it. Surely administrators should not build machinery that makes renewed exclusion easier.

They should not. A reservation cannot create authority to violate independent rights. The argument concerns decisions that may legitimately remain open. An invalid condition cannot justify an obligation to prepare for its exercise. The duty to support refusal therefore follows the permissible scope of the review, not the preferences of whoever negotiated it. This limitation also explains why opponents cannot turn every beneficial policy into a trial simply by demanding a future veto.

A fourth case concerns emergencies. An authority may need to reorganize services immediately, without enough time to preserve alternatives. The new system might then become indispensable. If the principle condemns it, the principle is too rigid; if it excuses it, officials may manufacture emergencies to escape its demands.

Actual urgency can provide an independent justification for accepting dependence. It does not make dependence disappear. The authority should distinguish a temporary legal mandate from a guarantee of temporary practical exposure, identify what cannot yet be preserved, and develop alternatives as circumstances permit. Claims of urgency remain subject to ordinary scrutiny of their evidence and proportionality. My argument supplies no license to create the emergency one then invokes.

Nor can an authority escape simply by removing the word trial. If the permanent case is independently adequate, it need not rely on provisionality. But if it still uses later rejection to answer the central objection to acting now, the argument applies whatever label it chooses. Its scope tracks the work done by the assurance, not a legislative heading.

Finally, uncertainty may defeat even careful preparation. A county could preserve an alternative which later fails because of an unforeseeable disaster. A duty to take proportionate precautions is not a guarantee of outcomes. We should distinguish an excusable loss of the capacity to reject from avoidably proceeding without it. Both may require repair. Only the second necessarily shows that the initial exercise of authority was defective on the grounds defended here.

## 7. After refusal has become too costly

Return to the first county. Central allocation has failed important tests, but ending it immediately would interrupt essential care. Officials should protect recipients. The argument does not instruct them to disregard actual suffering in order to punish the institution or vindicate a procedural ideal.

It does require them to distinguish three judgments. Is continuation now permissible or required? Has the trial established that central allocation merits ordinary authorization? What obligations remain because the review's practical conditions were lost? A single vote to continue cannot answer all three.

Continuation may be justified as a temporary response to dependence while the policy remains substantively unvindicated. In that case, repairing the capacity to refuse becomes a present institutional task. The county should assess alternative arrangements, assign responsibility for developing a viable one, and provide a credible transition period. The purpose is not to guarantee a future rejection. It is to permit a future decision whose reasons are less distorted by avoidable threats to recipients.

This repair can be expensive. If the original reservation was legitimate and its practical force was avoidably removed, the expense has a justification that an ordinary desire to revisit settled policy lacks. It helps restore a decision-making power whose reservation was part of the original mandate. Competing urgent needs can still limit what repair is reasonable. They should be identified as competing claims, rather than treated as proof that renewal has settled the original dispute.

There need be no perpetual institutional probation. If an acceptable alternative becomes available, reviewers can choose the central system on its merits. Alternatively, a properly authorized decision can openly accept the durable commitment, including the relevant transition constraints, on grounds sufficient for doing so. But an indefinitely renewed appeal to the same avoidable dependence cannot substitute for either route. Calling every renewal temporary reproduces the original problem.

A later, adequately justified authorization also does not rewrite the earlier history. The initial process may have been defective even though its eventual outcome is worth preserving. This is a reason to keep assessments of institutional conduct distinct from judgments about which policy citizens should now live under.

The political value of review lies partly in what can be done when its answer is unfavorable. Institutions that seek authority on the strength of a later decision must attend to the conditions under which that decision can be exercised. Protecting dependent people and protecting the power to reject should, where reasonably possible, be designed together. Otherwise people’s continuing need for a service can become a standing reason to retain the particular form of authority that a trial was supposed to test.
