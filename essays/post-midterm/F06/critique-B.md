# F06 — Role B: literature, accuracy, and contribution boundary

## Result

**Substantial revision of the contribution comparison is needed.** The four cited sources are broadly represented accurately; I found no established false quotation or load-bearing attribution error. But the nearest comparison is not just political entrenchment plus transition assistance. Responsible-social-experimentation literature already connects experimental permission, adverse results, practicable stopping, and proactive avoidance of lock-in. The draft's narrower authorization-based account may remain valuable, but its distinctiveness needs an argument, not merely a change of vocabulary.

This is a targeted review, not a novelty certification. I read the entire assigned manuscript, including its bibliography (draft.md, lines 1–149), verified all 18 assigned artifact hashes, and independently searched and consulted primary literature. Source boundaries and failures are in SOURCES_B.json and sources/. No other review was consulted.

## 1. Load-bearing source checks

### Brown: accurate summary, incomplete contrast

The account and warning example attributed at draft line 89 are supported by accepted-manuscript pp. 12–18: especially pp. 13–14 (responsibility and warning), pp. 16–18 (procedural and substantive expectations). Pagination is correctly identified as manuscript pagination, not journal pagination. Repository metadata confirms the bibliography's title, date, volume, issue, pages, and DOI. [Brown, accepted manuscript](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65003/1/Accepted_manuscript.pdf); [repository record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65003/).

However, Brown does not only protect expectations of policy permanence. His account also covers official assurances of future procedures; pp. 16–18 and 25 expressly discuss them. In F06, people might expect *effective review*, not permanent central scheduling. Removing an expectation of permanence therefore does not establish separation from Brown's whole theory. The draft needs a case where its duty holds without anyone actually expecting effective refusal, or must acknowledge overlap through procedural expectations. This is an incomplete comparison, not an established misattribution.

### Green: supported, but not merely an ex post predecessor

Draft line 91 accurately identifies expectation-independent wellbeing and responsibility considerations. The precise supporting location is §II.B, rendered lines 194–217: especially 195 and 211–212, which allocate **ex ante** risk-management responsibility to the state when individuals cannot manage the risk. §§III–IV support the broader critique of expectation-based analysis, not the most direct statement of the alternative account. Specify that locator. [Green 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jopp.12231).

Thus, “an earlier decision” is not by itself a distinguishing contribution. Nor is distinguishing workers' hardship from corporate claims, which Green already does. What F06 adds, if successful, is a duty concerning the reviewing body's usable authority, beyond duties to affected individuals. Also distinguish Green's critique of characteristic legislation from Brown's administrative context: a county's care-allocation trial is not automatically the former.

### Levinson and Sachs: sound, with a sharper useful difference

Draft line 87 is supported by §§III.A.1 and III.B.2. The latter explicitly includes inadvertent functional entrenchment, so absence of bad intentions is not new. Their working definition in Part I.A treats present preferences as given and emphasizes barriers to present majority will; the later discussion questions categorical boundaries. F06 instead concerns morally acceptable implementation of a reserved verdict, even where no political constituency blocks it. Emphasize this moral-capacity/political-obstruction difference. Their discussion of credible commitment also supplies genuine counterpressure against preserving flexibility. [Levinson and Sachs 2015](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/political-entrenchment-and-public-law).

### Dorf and Sabel: cited passages support the modest attribution

The assigned PDF's starred journal pp. 283–286 and 337–340 support draft line 93: corrigibility, revision, democratic safeguards, and higher-order institutions. In particular, pp. 339–340 distinguish successful service provision from adequate political institutions. These are not merely epistemic learning provisions. The draft wisely does not say their entire theory omits material safeguards. Keep that restraint. [Dorf and Sabel 1998](https://charlessabel.com/papers/demex.pdf).

The cited starred page markers were checked in the local extraction. The bibliography's terminal page 473 is corroborated by references in the assigned citation-check snapshot, but not independently confirmed from a publisher terminal-page image. The author-hosted reprint has no searchable *473 marker; that is not evidence of a wrong range. Treat terminal-page verification as a minor remaining limitation, not a citation error.

## 2. Closest actual predecessors missing from the comparison

### A. Bergen: strongest conceptual overlap

Jan Peter Bergen, “Reversible Experiments: Putting Geological Disposal to the Test,” *Science and Engineering Ethics* 22 (2016): 707–733, asks whether an authorized body could reasonably switch to an alternative if the technology develops problems. This is strikingly close to F06's adverse-review test. His conclusion links reversibility to its stated justification and calls for proactive design, viable alternatives, and limits on institutional and symbolic commitment. He also recognizes competing safety, justice, feasibility, and efficiency considerations. Locations: introduction, rendered lines 65–71; “On the Ability to Stop…,” 131–149; conclusion, 283–300. [Bergen 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11948-015-9697-2).

**Classification: demonstrated close overlap in test and design prescription; not demonstrated duplication of the entire thesis.** F06 does not demand undoing all consequences and specifically separates essential service continuity from disputed governance. Its proposed authority-based trigger is also narrower than a general condition of responsible technological experimentation. Those are possible differences to defend, not grounds for omitting Bergen.

### B. Van de Poel: existing ethical duties, not just strategic advice

Ibo van de Poel, “An Ethical Framework for Evaluating Experimental Technology,” *Science and Engineering Ethics* 22 (2016): 667–686, connects stopping/adaptation, flexibility and avoidance of lock-in, democratic approval, withdrawal, vulnerability protection, and fair risk distribution. He presents the conditions as defeasible moral obligations, not an inflexible checklist. Locations: “Conditions for Responsible Experimentation,” rendered lines 187–196 and 210–227; earlier informed-consent discussion, 113–131. [Van de Poel 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11948-015-9724-3).

**Classification: likely overlap with F06's duty package; related-but-distinct derivation.** F06 must show why an authorization reservation supplies an additional reason beyond non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice. Its compulsory-care example also raises a difference between individual withdrawal and institutional rejection. The separate Table 3 endpoint failed; the article's explanatory prose was accessible and independently supports this comparison.

### C. Listokin: reversibility already changes what uncertainty can justify

Yair Listokin, “Learning Through Policy Variation,” *Yale Law Journal* 118 (2008): 480–553, derives the attractiveness of uncertain policies from their reversibility and adjusts the analysis for switching costs, partial reversibility, and institutional design. His sunset discussion explicitly links increased reversibility to permission to try otherwise unattractive policies. Consulted locations include pp. 480, 483–486, selected contexts at 523 and 527–528, and 534–536. [Listokin 2008, publisher PDF](https://yalelawjournal.org/pdf/752_qxhyobos.pdf).

**Classification: established overlap in the justificatory role of reversibility; different normative framework.** F06's potential addition is a constraint on the scope of authority, not discovery that trial permission depends on real reversibility. Unlike an aggregate option-value calculation, it may explain why a reserved power matters even after success. That difference needs demonstration.

### D. Wallsten: very close contemporary diagnosis, different mechanism

Scott Wallsten's February 16, 2026 policy essay links experimental framing to getting uncertain policies accepted, then to implementation-created obstacles to ending them. It recommends credible decision rules and transition terms; it specifically mentions managed transitions that relocate beneficiaries. Locations: “The Appeal and the Trap,” “Experiment as Coalition Building,” “Scope and Limitations,” and final section; rendered lines 82–100, 120–137. [Wallsten 2026](https://techpolicyinstitute.org/publications/regulation/why-policy-experiments-never-end-constituencies-form-faster-than-evidence/).

**Classification: demonstrated overlap in problem framing and exit preparation; distinct from F06's moral-dependence mechanism.** Political bargaining by organized interests is not the same as successors' independently binding care obligations. This is a policy commentary, not a philosophical proof. Its empirical examples and universal-sounding title are not verified general laws here.

## 3. What remains of the contribution

The defensible candidate contribution is the following combination:

1. Permission to impose a trial can be weaker than permission to impose the durable arrangement because a specific legitimate future decision remains open.
2. Avoidably destroying acceptable alternatives can exceed that permission without altering the formal voting rule.
3. An independently binding duty to protect service users can enforce that practical commitment even without strategic intent, increased benefits, or confidence in permanence.
4. Therefore preservation or repair of refusal may be owed **as a condition of the authority exercised**, not only as good experimentation or compensation for harm.

The merit-versus-emergency-continuation distinction and service-versus-governance distinction make this combination useful. But the literature comparison leaves its **independent normative force unresolved**. Bergen already offers an adverse-result switching test and proactive alternatives; van de Poel already offers defeasible moral obligations. Merely describing F06 as “one such constraint” does not establish an additional reason.

The crucial inference is at draft lines 31–35. A future vote doing justificatory work does not automatically promise every implementation outcome will remain rejectable. It might reserve a decision *taking a specified dependency risk into account*. F06 admits this possibility, but needs to explain when a reservation is practical, rather than a reservation of deliberation alone. The example stipulates that effective rejection matters; the general principle needs criteria for identifying that fact outside the example.

To establish an authority-based contribution, compare otherwise identical trials with the same welfare risks and implementation capacities, differing only in whether effective rejection was a condition of authorization. Explain what extra duty or remedial priority follows in the reserved case. Also test a trial whose welfare-optimal design sacrifices some exit capacity: does the authorization reason alter what may be done, or merely require a more candid description? Without such a contrast, the proposal may be a valuable application of existing experimental ethics rather than a distinct principle.

## 4. Required changes, in priority order

1. **Rebuild §5 around Bergen, van de Poel, and Listokin.** Acknowledge overlap in the adverse-review test, ex ante preparation, and uncertainty/reversibility justification. Address Wallsten's closely matching diagnosis without treating his empirical claims as established.
2. **Correct the contribution boundaries around Brown and Green.** Distinguish procedural expectations from permanence, and an additional public-power reason from Green's already ex ante allocation of risk-management responsibility. Do not claim all relevant anticipation starts here.
3. **Defend the authority bridge.** Identify the reserved power's holder, content, and time horizon; distinguish legally explicit conditions from rhetorical assurances. Explain why impairing it generates positive preparation costs, not only a negative duty against obvious obstruction.
4. **Make the waiver noncircular.** Draft lines 45 and 59 allow adequate authorization to proceed without exit. Say what must be authorized: expected duration and exposure beyond the trial, specified dependency risks, and independent rights. Otherwise “preserve refusal or justify not doing so” risks saying only “act permissibly.”
5. **Clarify the adverse-review range.** Is one route enough for each credible legitimate adverse verdict, or merely for one verdict selected by implementers? Define what makes delay tolerable and which avoidable implementation choices cause the impairment. A route that can end central scheduling but not remedy the trial's relevant failures may not satisfy the reservation.
6. **Keep outcome and conduct judgments separate.** §7 is an important strength. Do not turn preservation into automatic termination, compensation into renewed authorization, or success into retrospective permission.
