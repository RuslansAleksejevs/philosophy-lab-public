# F06 — Argument and counterexamples

## Outcome

**Potentially defensible, with major unresolved questions about the reach and application of the principle.** The narrow argument against avoidably defeating an effective reservation of authority is convincing. The manuscript has not yet established how often an assurance of later review constitutes that reservation, or how to assess mixed cases where valuable experimentation itself makes exit costly. I find no fatal objection to the qualified thesis actually stated. I would not set the essay aside on argument grounds, but the three major concerns below matter to its claimed generality.

This is an independent Role A assessment, not a literature review or rewrite. Locations are the physical line numbers of the supplied `author/draft.md`, counting blank lines. I read all lines 1–149, including the bibliography. All assigned input hashes matched. Only the packet, exact assigned inputs, and local captures derived from those inputs were used. No other review, global context, external search, or subagent was used. Hypothetical counterexamples below are my constructions, not empirical reports.

## Charitable reconstruction

The thesis is conditional, not an assertion that all temporary policies must be reversible. When a morally legitimate later opportunity to reject a disputed arrangement helps justify imposing it now, implementation creates a presumptive institutional duty to preserve a usable route to that rejection. Proportionate preparation can discharge the duty; adequately justified authorization of specified dependence can change it. Neither an adverse vote nor bad results automatically requires immediate termination (lines 7–9, 29–45, 49–59, 79–83).

The inferential structure is:

1. A trial sometimes receives permission on evidence insufficient for a durable commitment because it leaves a consequential choice unsettled (lines 29–31).
2. Settling less is a practical as well as legal matter. Eliminating every acceptable exit can commit successors despite retaining a formal vote (lines 33–39).
3. **Bridge principle:** an implementing institution must exercise its present authority consistently with the effective future authority whose reservation helped justify the present exercise, unless further justification permits changing that division (line 35).
4. Therefore implementation should identify the reserved question, assess foreseeable barriers, and proportionately preserve a relevant exit or secure adequate authorization for the larger commitment (lines 41–61).
5. Independent duties constrain both the reserved decision and available transitions. Protecting care recipients does not entail protecting central scheduling (lines 65–75, 105–115).
6. If an exit was avoidably lost, continuation can be justified now without vindicating either the policy on its merits or the earlier implementation. A qualified repair duty can remain (lines 99–103, 127–137).

Given a genuinely effective reservation in premise 1 and the bridge principle, the core inference is sound. The main work lies in defending and specifying those premises, not in deriving the conclusion. The rescue analogy at line 101 illustrates the separation of present obligation from past justification; it cannot independently prove that creating dependence was a mistake.

## Strengths worth preserving

- **A clean central example.** Lines 13–21 hold care needs and disappointing trial results constant while varying transition capacity. Lines 17–19 remove two distracting explanations: newfound benefits and a demand to rebuild everything exactly as before. That makes the destroyed-option problem unusually clear.
- **No sacrifice of users to procedural purity.** Lines 127–137 distinguish the immediate policy choice, substantive validation, and assessment of institutional conduct. This is the essay's strongest argumentative achievement.
- **Dependence without mistaken belief.** Lines 67–71 explain why someone who understands a temporary-program warning can nevertheless have a strong claim to continuing assistance. Disclosure alone need not discharge an institutional obligation.
- **Real limits, not just disclaimers.** The essay allows costly transitions, replacement of the baseline, emergencies, independently justified commitments, legitimately narrowed reviews, and excusable losses through unforeseeable events (lines 51–59, 79–83, 109–123). Objections based solely on those possibilities do not refute it.
- **Institutional responsibility without personal blame.** Lines 71–75 avoid confusing the duty to prepare with a claim that individual officials must personally pay for every transition.
- **Restrained contribution claim.** Lines 87–95 do not claim discovery of entrenchment or exhaustive novelty. The consulted source passages give no reason to dismiss the stated connections as obviously inaccurate.

## Fatal concerns

**None established.** A successful refutation would need to show that even a legitimate, materially effective reservation can permissibly be avoidably neutralized without additional justification, or that preserving its practical force has no presumptive normative significance. Costly preparation, valuable commitments, and successor obligations alone do not establish either proposition because the manuscript expressly accommodates them.

## Major concerns

### M1. An assurance doing justificatory work is not yet a determinate reservation of authority

**Locations:** lines 9, 27–35, 59, 121; repair implications at 133–135.

Line 29 allows either an explicit public mandate or an assurance central to the reasons for accepting a trial. Line 35 then treats implementation as subject to a division of present and future public powers. Those are not automatically the same thing. A reason for accepting a decision may be politically influential without being an adopted condition of its permissible implementation. Conversely, a duly adopted reservation may bind administrators even if no voter actually relied on it. The draft needs an account of the normative status of the reservation, not merely its causal importance in persuasion.

**Boundary counterexample:** A council authorizes a two-year reorganization after openly approving the closure of local offices and discussing likely reconstruction costs. The reservation guarantees a public reconsideration of the whole package, not withdrawal at a protected cost. Later review is a central reason for accepting the policy: people want new evidence and another decision, even knowing that reversal may remain unattractive. The reviewing body can consider reconstruction, amendment, or continuation. This is more than an annual report, but less than a promise to preserve a rejection-ready alternative.

The essay's best answer is that this is authorization of specified dependence under lines 59 and 109, rather than a counterexample to its qualified thesis. I agree. But that answer reveals the gap: the initial public reasons must be interpreted to decide whether an effective exit was reserved, a risk was validly accepted, or only reconsideration was promised. Line 29's test does not yet distinguish these. Line 121's label-independent formulation is right but broadens the need for that distinction.

**What would resolve or narrow the concern:** Identify what makes a reservation part of the authorization rather than an influential assurance, and what practical power it reserves. Explicit commitments, authoritative public interpretation, and reasonable understandings of a mandate are candidate grounds; the essay need not choose an entire theory of democracy. It must explain why the ambiguous case inherits the stronger duty. If the argument is restricted to unmistakably effective reservations, the core survives, with narrower coverage.

### M2. Policy merit and implementation-created dependence can be jointly produced

**Locations:** lines 37–41, 55, 67, 83, 105–111, 129–135.

The distinction between a reason to retain a good arrangement and a reason to prevent collapse is illuminating in the deliberately wasteful records example. It is not a general causal division between legitimate learning and illegitimate lock-in. A single design choice can simultaneously produce the trial's useful effects and eliminate alternatives.

**Mixed counterexample:** Pooling scarce specialist staff makes countywide care more reliable. Those specialists cannot simultaneously staff a decentralized fallback. The trial reveals that central scheduling seriously burdens recipients' control but also that pooling is responsible for the reliability gain. Recreating local capacity would require diverting staff or substantial new spending. The scarcity is real, and dependence was foreseeable, but no official gratuitously destroyed records or withdrew an otherwise affordable alternative.

Continuation here can be supported both by performance and by a danger created through consolidation. Calling the danger an implementation-created crisis does not determine whether it is an avoidable misuse of limited authority, a justified cost of producing information, or a substantive advantage of the new system. The fact that the county helped create a constraint does not by itself discredit that constraint as a reason. The draft largely recognizes this at lines 41, 79–83, and 109–111, so the counterexample defeats a categorical reading, not the qualified principle.

The unresolved issue is comparative: against which feasible design should avoidability be judged, and what benefits may legitimately be lost in preserving an exit? A fallback that preserves basic assistance while losing the very reliability improvement under investigation may be acceptable, but not obviously worth funding. An institution cannot escape merely by describing any useful consolidation as inseparable; equally, a critic cannot establish wrongdoing merely by imagining a more expensive alternative.

**What would resolve or narrow the concern:** Explain one intermediate case where useful consolidation, adverse evidence, and a feasible but costly fallback coexist. Distinguish an unexplained sacrifice of review capacity from a substantively justified tradeoff. The conclusion can remain a burden of justification and proportionate planning, rather than a diagnostic rule that all dependence-related reasons are extraneous to policy merit.

The consulted Levinson–Sachs passages support the challenge: functional commitments may be valuable, and upstream choices routinely change downstream options (sections III.A.1 and III.B.2; captured web lines 374–385 and 471–488). The essay appropriately accepts that challenge at line 87; its narrower authorization argument still needs the mixed-case application.

### M3. The adverse-review test leaves the relevant risk and comparison unclear

**Locations:** lines 45–53, 59–61, 77–81, 123, 131–135.

The test asks for a credible adverse outcome, while the duty requires proportionate precautions and only one acceptable route. Three uncertainties interact:

1. Is preparation needed for every credible ground of rejection, a representative set, or only sufficiently likely grounds? A remote but credible failure may demand a very expensive contingency. Foreseeability distinguishes it from the disaster in line 123 but does not determine what precaution is reasonable.
2. An acceptable route under one adverse verdict may be unacceptable under another. A decentralized fallback using the same records could address loss of local control but fail a review concerned with the records' insecure handling. One route in the abstract is not necessarily a route responsive to the relevant reason for refusal.
3. Line 51 makes usability partly depend on whether transition burdens are grossly disproportionate to the reasons for rejection. An available, rights-respecting fallback may be too costly to justify after only mildly negative evidence. That can mean rejection is substantively unwarranted, not that the power to reject was disabled.

**Counterexample to an overbroad test:** The county maintains a fully operable fallback but the trial misses a modest scheduling target. Switching would cost a large sum without violating basic duties. Review reasonably retains the trial. If the expense is disproportionate to this weak reason for rejection, line 51 can call refusal unusable even though review is doing precisely its job: weighing alternatives rather than being coerced by threatened abandonment.

The best reading of lines 47 and 49 is that a genuinely adverse verdict already supplies a sufficient reason to reject apart from disabling dependence. That reading avoids the counterexample, but it must not become circular: a verdict is not genuinely adverse merely because there is a usable exit, nor is an exit defective whenever rejection loses the comparison.

**What would resolve or narrow the concern:** State the risk-sensitive scope of preparation, distinguish a fallback for each relevant failure from one fallback regardless of failure, and separate insufficient grounds for rejection from an implementation-induced inability to act on otherwise sufficient grounds. No numerical formula is required. A worked case would establish more than another statement that proportionality is contested.

## Minor concerns

### m1. The contrast needs a more explicit transition time assumption

**Locations:** lines 15, 19–21, 53, 127–131.

The first county cannot restart local teams soon, whereas the second can transfer authority over three months. Yet line 53 allows delayed withdrawal and line 131 proposes developing a transition. If the first county could reliably reconstruct capacity in six months, the difference might be an expensive delay, not an unavailable ending. State what makes its delay intolerable or its route noncredible. The example remains persuasive if that is assumed, but the severity of the difference should not rest solely on immediate termination being harmful.

### m2. The bearer is an institution, but implementation and funding may have different authorities

**Locations:** lines 45, 53, 71–75, 131–133.

An agency can preserve records while lacking power to appropriate reserves or compel an outside provider to remain available. A successor may inherit a duty whose discharge requires cooperation from another body. This does not remove institutional responsibility. It does require distinguishing duties to propose, seek authorization, warn, and refrain from particular implementation choices from duties to deliver a transition the agency cannot fund. Otherwise the account can mislocate the omission it condemns.

### m3. The three judgments are separable but not evidentially isolated

**Locations:** lines 129–137.

A single renewal vote need not answer all three questions, as the draft rightly says. Still, a transparent renewal can both deal with present dependence and authorize a durable arrangement; reasons about reliability, transition costs, and rights may bear on several judgments. Line 135 permits such authorization. The point is that renewal does not *automatically* vindicate everything, not that the judgments require entirely separate decisions or mutually exclusive grounds.

## Uncertain concerns and dissent

### U1. Distinctive normative ground, or an application of familiar duties?

**Locations:** lines 23, 35, 59, 89–95, 133.

The duty might follow from ordinary constraints on delegated power, honest justification, or responsible transition planning. That possibility is not a refutation and does not establish lack of originality. To show a special contribution, compare two otherwise similar policies, only one authorized through an effective reservation, and identify the difference in what preparation or repair is owed. The appeal-process analogy at line 23 gives a plausible explanation: an unexercised power can matter. It does not yet specify its weight relative to equally grave ordinary transition interests.

Green's consulted section II.B explicitly treats ex ante risk allocation and wellbeing as grounds of transition assistance (web lines 194–217), not simply aid after a decision. Thus the contrast at line 91 should not be understood as a claim that existing transition theory has no anticipatory component. Brown's consulted warning discussion supports the narrower contrast with expectations of stability (accepted manuscript pp.14–15; PDF zero-based pages 13–14, web lines 476–529). Neither establishes the full independence or redundancy of the proposed reserved-power duty. I leave that issue open.

### U2. How are disputed independent obligations fixed?

**Locations:** lines 49–51, 73, 83, 111–115.

The rights limitation is necessary, not an objection to the principle. But if the trial investigates whether central allocation itself violates recipients' control, that alleged right cannot simply be assumed or excluded when defining acceptable exits. The duty's application depends on defensible judgments about which obligations are independent of the disputed arrangement. The essay openly lacks a complete theory of protected benefits; that is an acceptable limit, provided borderline cases are not presented as settled by the test alone.

### U3. Reasonable disagreement about how much specification is needed

A sympathetic reader can regard M2 and M3 as normal limits of a defeasible moral principle rather than defects requiring a decision procedure. That dissent has force: the essay is not offering a regulation or algorithm, and vague proportionality does not invalidate a philosophical argument. My reason for retaining the major labels is narrower. The unresolved distinctions determine whether commonplace review arrangements fall within the duty and whether a perfectly functioning review is incorrectly classified as disabled. They affect the argument's scope, not just its administrative implementation.

The consulted Dorf–Sabel passages combine revision with safeguards and discuss reciprocal changes in means and ends (journal markers *284–285; captured web lines 473–512). That gives a reason to consider adaptable reservations rather than demand frozen objectives. The draft already allows narrowed reservations at line 109. I have not established that the larger experimentalist theory conflicts with, anticipates, or fully supplies this essay's proposal.

## What would actually change the verdict?

- **Strengthen it:** establish the authorization bridge in a case lacking an explicit promise of practical reversibility; apply it to one genuinely mixed benefit/dependence case; and distinguish adverse evidence insufficient for rejection from an exit defeated by avoidable design. These are requests for arguments, not a demand to rewrite the essay in my preferred style.
- **Narrow it:** limit the thesis to clearly reserved practical powers and reasonably affordable contingencies for specified grounds of review. This preserves the central county example and most of the repair argument.
- **Defeat the core:** provide a case with a legitimate effective reservation, no independently adequate acceptance of dependence, an affordable rights-respecting exit deliberately or negligently removed, and nevertheless no presumptive wrong in that removal. None of the manuscript's admitted emergency, cost, new-benefit, or unforeseeable-disaster cases does this.
- **Set it aside:** would require more than the current concerns. I have not found a contradiction or a counterexample defeating the qualified principle.
