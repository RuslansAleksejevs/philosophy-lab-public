# Keeping Refusal Possible

A public authority introduces a policy on trial. It lacks a sufficient case for imposing the arrangement indefinitely, but promises a consequential decision after two years. During implementation it reorganizes essential services around the experiment. At review, the evidence is disappointing. Rejection remains legally possible, yet it would now interrupt assistance that people cannot do without. Officials reasonably continue the policy.

Continuation may be right without vindicating the implementation that made it necessary. A public institution can secure limited permission by leaving a decision open, then consume the practical conditions for exercising that decision. People's legitimate need for protection becomes a reason to preserve the disputed form of authority.

I defend a conditional principle: when a legitimate authorization reserves a practical public power to discontinue specified governance, implementers owe proportionate preparation for its exercise. If preparation cannot reasonably be secured, they must seek a different mandate rather than silently enlarge the limited one. This is not a guarantee of cheap reversal or a rule requiring termination after bad results. Its object is a usable public power, not an unchanged world.

The condition matters. Reporting, reconsideration, and effective authority to reject are different. An influential assurance of review does not by itself establish the strongest reservation. The argument applies where that practical reservation is part of what the institution has been authorized to do.

## 1. Care without compulsory central control

Imagine a county testing a single system for allocating publicly funded home care. Local teams previously agreed schedules with recipients. The new system assigns visits centrally. It may improve coverage, but recipients worry about losing control over daily life. A council authorizes compulsory participation for two years. Its adopted mandate leaves the continuation of central allocation to a later council decision, including a funded transition if discontinuation is warranted. Preserving essential assistance is an independent obligation throughout.

The reservation is reasonably achievable when adopted. Transferable records, a modest transition reserve, and retained scheduling skills could support a three-month transfer of allocation authority. The county need not keep every old office open. Nor must it dismantle useful infrastructure if central allocation ends.

Instead, administrators close the local offices, transfer staff, and abandon transferable records. These choices save money. At review, allocation repeatedly disregards recipients' work and family commitments, and the council judges that its genuine benefits do not justify these burdens. Yet there is no staffed, funded transition deliverable within the three-month horizon. Reconstruction would take an uncertain period, during which the same serious burdens would continue. Immediate termination would leave some recipients without assistance. Nobody intended to defeat review, but administrators have removed the practicable way to implement its verdict.

Contrast a county with the same care needs and results that maintains the transfer capacity. Ending central allocation is inconvenient and costly, but assistance continues. Review can choose between institutional arrangements instead of between central control and threatened abandonment.

For this initial example, assume the trial supplies no additional care. Existing needs have been tied to a new governing arrangement, rather than valuable new benefits making withdrawal unattractive. This isolates the problem; it does not establish that every trial is like this.

The relevant refusal concerns compulsory central allocation, not public care, shared facilities, or every innovation introduced during the trial. Security of assistance need not mean security of the disputed means of delivering it. A person may know perfectly well that an experiment is temporary and still have a claim against being left without help getting out of bed. That claim should not become an avoidable institutional obstacle to ending the experiment.

## 2. What has actually been reserved?

A temporary legal instrument can authorize enduring practical consequences. An emergency bridge can be temporary while its construction permanently changes a river. A reporting requirement can accompany a project already accepted as irreversible. Neither arrangement necessarily reserves practical authority to reject the underlying commitment.

Three kinds of mandate should be distinguished. **Reporting** supplies information without requiring another choice. **Reconsideration** assigns a later body authority to reassess a commitment, taking openly accepted reconstruction costs and dependencies as given. **Practical reservation** limits present permission by leaving that body a protected capacity to discontinue specified governance, subject to stated transition constraints and accepted risks. Only the third triggers the special duty defended here.

A reservation has a holder, an object, grounds of review, and a time horizon. In the example, the holder is the future council exercising public authority, not each recipient possessing a personal veto. The object is compulsory central allocation. Reliability and recipients' control are relevant grounds. The horizon allows three months for transfer rather than requiring an overnight stop. The duty protects the public decision-making power on behalf of the people subject to it.

What makes this reservation authoritative? The clearest basis is an adopted condition specifying practical discontinuation or transition protection. A reservation need not use a legal formula. It can also follow from the competent body's authoritative explanation of a mandate: for example, it answers an objection to extended compulsory control by expressly approving only a limited exposure, with a practicable decision about continuation still to come. The explanation must identify what remains unapproved, not merely predict that another meeting will occur. Its interpretation must fit the adopted implementation powers and the treatment of foreseeable dependencies.

These are evidential criteria, not a complete theory of political legitimacy. A speech can influence acceptance without becoming a condition of permissible action. Conversely, an adopted condition can bind administrators even if nobody actually predicts compliance. Where the public record leaves the distinction unsettled, this argument does not establish a practical reservation. The institution should clarify the mandate before taking consequential steps; duties of honesty and responsible planning may apply independently. Ambiguity cannot simply be counted as proof of the stronger duty.

Suppose a council expressly approves closing local offices, discusses reconstruction costs, and reserves a later judgment about the whole package. That can be genuine reconsideration without protected exit capacity. If the specified exposure is independently justified, my principle does not require administrators to undo the accepted commitment. If closure is approved only because an adequately supported alternative remains available, the case is different. The content of the permission, not the prominence of the word review, determines which case it is.

Neither majority preference nor formal adoption alone makes a reservation legitimate. A majority cannot reserve authority to exclude people from assistance in violation of their rights. The argument starts with a morally permissible allocation of public authority. It does not turn every negotiated condition into an obligation.

## 3. Why a power requires material support

The central inference has four steps.

First, public authority to impose burdens can be limited in scope. Permission to impose compulsory control for a trial is not automatically permission to make that control practically unavoidable thereafter. Where practical rejection is reserved, the initial justification leaves the durable imposition unsettled.

Second, implementation can alter the scope of the imposition without changing any voting rule. If administrators foreseeably make essential assistance unavailable except under central control, decent successors cannot simply withdraw that control. The earlier choice has increased the exposure citizens will bear, using successors' independent obligations as the mechanism. It has not merely supplied evidence for their judgment.

Third, administrative discretion within a limited mandate does not include discretion to erase a legitimate condition of that mandate. Otherwise implementers could obtain authority to settle a question expressly left to someone else merely by choosing the conditions under which that person must decide. The limit would constrain paperwork while leaving the disputed practical power unprotected. There is no reason to interpret an adopted practical reservation that way.

Fourth, respecting a material limit can require positive action. An institution permitted to conduct a trial with protected rejection capacity may need to retain records, fund transition preparation, or arrange alternative delivery. Avoiding a deliberate obstruction is insufficient if ordinary implementation foreseeably defeats the same condition. Where reasonable preparation is unavailable, the conclusion is not that unlimited expenditure is owed. The conclusion is that this limited mandate cannot justify proceeding unchanged. The institution must seek changed authorization, reduce the trial, or refrain from the steps that exceed its permission.

This bridge does not infer practical reservation from temporariness. That condition has already been identified. It explains why, once legitimately adopted, a reservation constrains material implementation as well as formal procedure. The duty concerns proportionate precautions against foreseeable impairment, not guaranteed preservation through every disaster. A successful trial does not retrospectively authorize disabling the power: finding no reason to exercise it does not erase the original condition.

Consider two otherwise identical proposals. Both have the same expected benefits, risks, staff, and feasible designs. In one, the authorizing body independently accepts specified durable exposure and asks for later reconsideration. In the other, it legitimately approves only a trial with practical rejection protected. Maintaining a transfer option costs money that could produce a modest service improvement. Assume neither spending choice violates independent rights.

Administrators in the first case may have discretion to prefer the improvement, subject to ordinary duties of experimentation and welfare. In the second, the same welfare calculation does not by itself grant that discretion. They must maintain the option or return to the authorizer with the proposed sacrifice. Even if preserving it is not the welfare-maximizing package, an administrator cannot replace the adopted allocation of authority with its preferred package. The difference is who may settle the tradeoff and under what mandate, not whether money matters.

The authorizing body may legitimately change the second mandate. It must then confront the larger exposure rather than count protected future rejection as already secured. A valid public condition is not an absolute obstacle to revision, but neither is it merely advice to be followed whenever administrators independently find it optimal.

Suppose citizens distrust both administrations and nobody predicts that genuine rejection capacity will survive. That does not erase the second mandate's limit. If it is breached, restoring the power can have a remedial justification absent from an ordinary desire to reopen a settled commitment. An unexercised power matters because of its legitimate place in the authorization, not only because someone believes it will be exercised.

## 4. Available refusal is not mandatory refusal

The preparation question must be separated from the substantive verdict. A maintained fallback may be perfectly usable while rejecting the policy is unwarranted. Missing a modest scheduling target does not require costly transfer if the overall arrangement remains good. Counting ordinary transition costs is part of review, not evidence that review has failed.

A usable route has resources, staff, legal powers, and a credible timetable. It can discontinue the disputed governance without violating independent obligations or imposing transition burdens outside the justified limits of the mandate. Usability does not mean that this route wins the final comparison. Expensive but permissible options can remain rationally unchosen. Conversely, a cheap option that abandons vulnerable recipients is not acceptable merely because most voters prefer it.

An adverse-review inquiry should therefore proceed in stages:

1. Identify the adopted reservation and reasonably foreseeable failure scenarios within it. Consider which findings could warrant discontinuation after genuine benefits and ordinary costs of a prepared transition are counted. An unfavorable statistic alone is insufficient.
2. For each covered ground that could warrant discontinuation, identify a responsive, institutionally deliverable route. A route must address the relevant failure, not simply change the system's name.
3. Compare feasible implementation packages. Ask whether the proposed design adds a foreseeable obstacle to carrying out such a verdict that proportionate preparation could avoid. Distinguish that obstacle from benefits genuinely lost by discontinuation.
4. Specify remaining risks and responsibility for monitoring them. If the mandate's promised protection cannot be provided, return to authorization rather than treating a favorable prognosis as a substitute.

The comparison uses feasible preparations, not an imagined world with no scarcity or sunk costs. Some switching costs are inherent. Others can reasonably be reduced by portable records or flexible allocation of authority. A threatened loss of essential care caused by abandoning affordable transfer capacity is different from the expense of operating a fallback that has actually been maintained.

No rule identifies a legitimate adverse verdict solely by assuming exit is available. Reliability gains, control burdens, permissible transition expenditure, and the reservation's scope are judged first on their own grounds. Design can then be criticized for removing a way to implement the resulting judgment. In hard cases, these evaluations interact. The staged inquiry makes that interaction explicit; it does not produce a mechanically independent answer.

One route may cover several failures, but there must be a responsive route for each ground within the protection actually reserved. Decentralized scheduling might remedy loss of control while using the same dangerously insecure records. It would not answer a review whose authorized concern is that security failure. A clean data-transfer plan, stronger security arrangements, or another route may be needed. Implementers cannot discharge the duty by preparing only for the failure easiest to handle.

The scope of preparation is risk-sensitive. Likelihood, severity, cost, and whether one precaution covers several scenarios matter. A remote possibility of catastrophic abandonment can justify a relatively cheap safeguard. It need not justify maintaining a complete duplicate service at the expense of indispensable care today. A foreseeable risk is not automatically a risk against which every precaution is owed. The authorizer must identify the protection it can reasonably promise and the residual risks it accepts. Unknown probabilities call for cautious scenario analysis, not invented precision.

A transition horizon must also be justified. Three months is an assumption of the example, not a general standard. Continued serious exposure can make a long delay unacceptable even when assistance remains available. A genuinely deliverable six-month transfer may be adequate for a less serious burden. Indefinite promises to develop an alternative are not equivalent to either. Serious new information can require changing a horizon through the appropriate authority; administrative inability to meet it is not itself such authorization.

## 5. When useful consolidation makes exit costly

The clean care example omits a difficult possibility: a valuable design can both create benefits and narrow alternatives. Pooling scarce specialist staff may make visits more reliable while making a decentralized system harder to maintain. The danger of losing care is then partly connected to a genuine achievement, not simply the destruction of records.

Suppose the trial confirms better reliability but also shows serious burdens from central allocation. Shared clinical staffing could continue under a different allocation process. Preparing that process requires training and a reserve; the expense means fewer additional, nonessential visits during the trial. This is a real sacrifice. The appropriate comparison is between the entire packages: central allocation with transition preparation, central allocation without it, and a smaller or differently designed trial.

If the mandate reserves practical authority to reject central allocation, implementers may not silently choose the unprepared package because it delivers more visits. They should preserve the reasonably affordable route or seek permission for the different package. The authorizer must weigh the visits against continuing compulsory exposure after a relevant failure. Protecting review has a claim grounded in the adopted mandate, but not a claim to priority over every competing need.

At review, losing some reliability may still make discontinuation a bad choice. Then retaining central allocation reflects a substantive comparison, not necessarily a disabled power. Alternatively, reviewers may judge that the control burdens warrant transfer despite the reliability loss. If affordable preparation has been omitted, a further threat to basic assistance can prevent executing that judgment. One design feature can therefore supply both a genuine reason for continuation and a reason to criticize earlier implementation. Neither cancels the other automatically.

Now change the case. Every alternative allocation process would require scarce staff whose diversion would leave recipients without indispensable visits. There is no affordable, adequate fallback, and smaller trials cannot answer the important question. The original practical reservation cannot reasonably be fulfilled. The right response is not to deny the value of consolidation or abandon recipients to preserve a formal ideal. The county must decide whether the extended exposure is independently justified, or decline that version of the trial.

Changed authorization has identifiable content. It must address the disputed means, plausible adverse findings despite which dependence could force continuation, the likely duration and distribution of extended burdens, feasible alternative packages, their opportunity costs, and residual uncertainty. The reasons must support imposing that larger risk, with affected interests represented and independent rights respected. The actor deciding must have authority to accept that exposure. A warning supplies information; it does not itself supply these reasons or powers.

This is not just renaming a trial a commitment. If the case for the specified risk is insufficient, candour does not make proceeding permissible. If the case is sufficient, revision of the mandate changes what implementers may do. The principle changes the decision, or its proper decision-maker, rather than requiring an honest label alone.

New benefits raise a related question. A trial might enable recipients to work. Ending assistance would now threaten a valuable capacity created by the policy. There may be strong reasons to preserve that benefit without preserving central allocation. Where benefit and governance are genuinely inseparable, a mandate can reserve only expansion while committing to existing users, or openly accept specified dependence. Whether a new benefit creates a protected claim requires further argument. The exit principle does not settle that question in either direction.

## 6. Institutions, rights, and emergencies

The initial duty belongs to the public institution exercising the relevant authority. Personal wrongdoing is not necessary. Good intentions do not enlarge a mandate, and institutional preparation does not normally require officials to pay personally for transition.

Responsibilities nevertheless follow actual powers. An agency may retain portable records and scheduling skills but lack authority to appropriate a reserve. It should formulate the plan, seek funds, disclose the dependency risk, and refrain from discretionary choices that exceed its mandate. The appropriating body must consider funding, rescoping, or declining the limited project. No agency owes a transition it has no means or legal power to deliver. Divided powers require coordinated authorization rather than making the condition nobody's responsibility.

The costs fall on people too. Taxes for preparation, forgone visits, and staff diverted from other services are morally relevant. Preserving refusal is a presumptive requirement of this authorization, not an unlimited priority. Independent obligations can require rescoping rather than purchasing the preferred exit. The principle also does not protect an unacceptable baseline: local care that is already unreliable or humiliating need not be preserved. An adequate new alternative can replace every old provider.

Nor does every affected interest acquire a veto. A provider's profits from continued exclusivity and employees' severe hardship require separate assessment. Notice of review may matter to reasonable commercial risk-taking without eliminating workers' claims. These claims concern people and permissible transition, not an automatic right to preserve a governing system.

Reservations are bounded by legitimate authority and independent rights. A review cannot rightly choose renewed exclusion from essential care just because a majority reserved that choice. But calling obligations independent does not settle their content. The example assumes a duty to maintain essential assistance; it does not assume an unlimited right to recipient-controlled scheduling. The trial may investigate the strength of that control interest. Where rights or acceptable service levels are disputed, the preservation duty's application remains conditional on a defensible answer. It cannot resolve the dispute by labeling one side an independent obligation.

Urgency can independently justify a design that cannot preserve an exit. The authority should identify the unprotected exposure, justify it under the emergency conditions, and develop alternatives when feasible. It cannot create an avoidable crisis and treat the resulting urgency as permission for its original design. Unforeseeable disasters are different: proportionate preparation can fail without showing prior misconduct. Loss of capacity may then call for rebuilding, but not for a finding that implementers exceeded their permission.

## 7. The contribution and its predecessors

Responsible social experimentation already connects permission to experiment with the ability to act on adverse findings. Bergen asks whether an authorized body can reasonably switch to an alternative if a technology develops problems. He calls for proactive preservation of viable alternatives and avoidance of excessive institutional commitment, while recognizing competing values. The test and design prescription here overlap substantially. His account also considers undoing undesirable consequences; my argument requires neither restoring the entire past nor undoing every experimental effect. Its narrower question is what a specific reservation adds to the limits of public permission. ([Bergen 2016, introduction, “On the Ability to Stop,” and conclusion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11948-015-9697-2).)

Van de Poel offers ethical requirements concerning stopping or adapting experiments, flexibility, avoidance of lock-in, democratic approval, withdrawal, vulnerability, and fair distribution. These are defeasible moral obligations, not merely recommendations for efficient learning. My proposal is compatible with that framework. It specifies how an adopted institutional reservation changes implementers' discretion and the reasons for restoring a public power. It does not discover an ethical duty to prepare for exit. Individual withdrawal and institutional discontinuation are also different powers; a compulsory care trial needs justification beyond having a future collective vote. ([Van de Poel 2016, “Conditions for Responsible Experimentation” and “Status of the Conditions”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11948-015-9724-3).)

Listokin's analysis already explains how reversibility changes the justification for uncertain policies, and how switching costs and institutional design affect that benefit. His sunset discussion directly connects easier reversal with trying policies otherwise unattractive. The contrast is not between foresight and its absence. It is between evaluating flexibility's welfare value and respecting an adopted limit on who may sacrifice it. The paired mandates in §3 aim to show that this limit matters even when administrators favor the less flexible package. ([Listokin 2008, pp. 534–536](https://yalelawjournal.org/pdf/752_qxhyobos.pdf).)

Wallsten's policy commentary likewise diagnoses experimental framing followed by implementation-created barriers to ending policies, and recommends credible decision and transition terms. His emphasis is organized political constituencies. My care example instead concerns an independent moral obligation that binds successors even without strategic bargaining or a constituency blocking repeal. The framing overlap is real; the commentary's empirical examples are not evidence for a general law asserted here. ([Wallsten 2026, “The Appeal and the Trap,” “Scope and Limitations,” and final section](https://techpolicyinstitute.org/publications/regulation/why-policy-experiments-never-end-constituencies-form-faster-than-evidence/).)

Levinson and Sachs distinguish formal and functional entrenchment and challenge the boundary between entrenchment and ordinary consequences of politics. Valuable commitments constrain successors too. Their working account takes present political preferences as given and concerns barriers to majority will. My claim concerns morally acceptable execution of a particular reserved verdict, even where no political opposition prevents repeal. Absence of deliberate intent is not a distinction from their account; they recognize inadvertent constraints. ([Levinson and Sachs 2015, §§I.A, III.B.2](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/political-entrenchment-and-public-law).)

Brown's responsibility-based account includes expectations of procedures, not only policy stability. Citizens may expect effective review, so disclaiming permanence alone does not separate my case from his. The narrower contrast is that a valid allocation of authority remains binding without actual predictive confidence that it will be respected. Brown's account requires expectations with a predictive component. The pessimistic variant in §3 therefore identifies a ground that does not require proving such confidence, while acknowledging overlap where procedural expectations exist. ([Brown 2017, accepted manuscript pp. 1, 12–18, 25](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65003/1/Accepted_manuscript.pdf).)

Green already treats wellbeing and ex ante responsibility for managing legal-change risks as grounds of transition assistance. Anticipation and expectation-independent protection are not new here. The additional question is whether an institution owes preparation or repair because practical public authority was reserved. His critique concerns characteristic legislative transitions; the county's administrative arrangement need not instantiate that category. ([Green 2020, §II.B](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jopp.12231).)

Dorf and Sabel connect revision, democratic safeguards, and adequate political institutions; successful service provision does not settle the constitutional question. My argument specifies one constraint within that broader terrain. It makes no claim that their complete theory neglects material conditions or that legitimate learning must freeze objectives. ([Dorf and Sabel 1998, journal pp. 283–285, 339–340](https://charlessabel.com/papers/demex.pdf).)

The candidate contribution is thus a focused authorization argument, joined to a distinction between protecting services and protecting governance, and to corresponding repair reasons. It may be a useful specification of familiar duties of limited delegation, autonomy, or justice rather than an independent moral principle. The comparisons support neither exhaustive novelty nor a claim that all these broader theories lack the resources to derive it.

## 8. After rejection has become unavailable

Return to the unprepared county. Officials should preserve essential care. They should not sacrifice recipients to punish earlier misconduct or dramatize the value of review.

They must nevertheless distinguish three judgments: whether continuation is now permissible or required; whether central allocation deserves durable authorization; and whether implementation respected the original mandate. The judgments can share evidence. A transparent renewal may protect recipients and independently authorize a durable arrangement at once. What it cannot do merely by continuing the policy is vindicate either its merits or the earlier conduct.

Where continuation responds chiefly to preventable dependence and durable authorization remains unsupported, restoring a responsive route becomes a present institutional task. Officials should identify alternatives, assign responsibility, seek resources, and set a credible timetable. The purpose is not to guarantee eventual rejection. It is to make the reserved judgment usable without the avoidable threat of abandonment.

Repair has a reason beyond ordinary dissatisfaction with settled policy: the institution removed a public power that belonged within its mandate. That reason can justify costs even where nobody previously expected compliance and no vote to reject has yet occurred. It is not a categorical claim against all other needs. If restoring capacity would undermine indispensable care elsewhere, the institution must explain that conflict and seek an authorized resolution rather than pretend that mere renewal has discharged the original condition.

This remedial reason is also conditional. The authority must identify an actual breached reservation and feasible repair, not infer both from regret that a policy proved hard to change. Excusable loss through disaster, accepted dependency risks, and an independently justified later commitment can generate different obligations. A later commitment can end the need to keep that particular decision open, but it does not retrospectively authorize earlier excess.

There need be no perpetual probation. Once a responsive alternative exists, reviewers can choose central allocation on its merits. Alternatively, a competent public decision can openly justify its durable exposure, including relevant transition constraints, on sufficient grounds. Indefinite renewal justified only by the same preventable dependence accomplishes neither.

The argument establishes no general duty to preserve every political possibility. It establishes a narrower constraint on using one public permission to consume another. Where a practical power to reject legitimately limits a trial, material implementation belongs within that limit. Protecting dependent people and protecting their institution's authority to choose should, where reasonably possible, be prepared together.
