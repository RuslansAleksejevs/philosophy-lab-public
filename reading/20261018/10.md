# When Only Believers Count: Faith and the Evidence of Participation

Religious understanding may require participation. Someone who never prays, attends to religious experience, or attempts a life of trust might lack evidence that becomes available to someone who does. This possibility creates a familiar difficulty: refusing commitment until the evidence arrives can prevent the evidence from arriving. But participation creates a second difficulty. A religious practice can influence both what its participants experience and which participants are subsequently considered competent to describe what happened. If those who remain convinced count as successful learners while those who lose conviction count as people who never properly understood, the practice can appear to vindicate itself without having survived a genuine test.

The problem is not that religious evidence must be accessible before faith. Nor is it that every religious commitment must remain provisional in practice. It concerns a particular claim of acquired authority: *we now have more reason to trust this practice because those who have genuinely undergone it find its teaching confirmed*. I argue that this claim requires a distinction between qualifications for entering an inquiry and rules for accepting its results. Competence can depend on participation. Yet when the reliability of participation itself is in question, continued assent cannot by itself determine whose experience counts. A person can cease to believe without thereby making the history of her participation irrelevant. This does not certify her present interpretation or the accuracy of every retrospective report.

This restriction is narrower than a general demand that religion be falsifiable. It allows immediate religious experience, justified trust, prolonged apprenticeship, and rational commitment in uncertainty. Its target is the conversion of those permissions into a retrospectively guaranteed record of success. Here, vindicating a practice means gaining a further reason to regard it as a reliable route to the understanding it promises. The philosophical consequence is an asymmetry: a practice may justifiably require trust as a condition of access while lacking justification for requiring continuing trust as a condition of testimony about that access.

## 1. Access is not vindication

William James supplies a powerful reason to take participation seriously. In sections IX–X of “The Will to Believe,” he considers truths and relationships whose realization or discovery can depend on prior confidence. A person who refuses every trusting advance may exclude herself from relationships that would reward it. James extends this possibility to religious encounter: an exclusively withholding attitude might block access to a truth that requires a willing response. His defense permits a risk; it does not promise that taking the risk succeeds. ([James 1896/1897, IX–X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/26659/26659-h/26659-h.htm).)

There are two distinct claims here. The first concerns opportunity: without undertaking the practice, I may miss something real. The second concerns learning: after undertaking it, I have gained a reason to judge that what I sought is real. The first does not entail the second. Going to an observatory can be necessary to see a distant object; merely completing the journey does not establish that I saw it. Conversely, the journey's causal role does not discredit a genuine observation. What matters is what happened there and how the resulting judgment is supported.

Religious participation is often more demanding than visiting an observatory. The participant may need to acquire habits of attention, abandon defensive expectations, or become responsive to unfamiliar values. A requirement that all relevant evidence be available in advance would therefore beg the question against an important form of religious epistemology. It would confuse the conditions for acquiring a capacity with the evidence available to someone who already has it.

But the opposite confusion is equally serious. From the possibility that one must change in order to learn, it does not follow that every change conducive to continued belief is learning. Acquiring a capacity and acquiring conviction can coincide without being identical. A practice's claim to teach us about reality must leave room for asking which has occurred.

The question becomes especially pressing when a participant seeks more than a report of her own experience. “Prayer seemed to disclose a loving presence to me” and “the experience of accomplished practitioners establishes that prayer reliably discloses such a presence” make different evidential demands. The second claim invokes a record of results. It therefore inherits questions about how that record was constituted.

This distinction has a religious predecessor. William Alston separates the rationality of continuing an established belief-forming practice from the additional support its results can provide. His account also allows internal checks involving doctrine and spiritual development. He should therefore not be identified with a community whose only test is agreement. The present question concerns the use of participant histories within an argument for additional support, not a general rejection of his account. ([Alston 1994, pp. 865–866](https://www.andrewmbailey.com/wpa/PrecisPerceiving.pdf).)

## 2. Two communities and one missing witness

Consider two possible communities teaching the same contemplative practice. They have the same doctrine, teachers, and demanding preparation. Both acknowledge that distracted beginners are poor judges of a discipline they have barely attempted. Both ask students to practice sincerely over a substantial period. Some students report an awareness they interpret as divine presence; others report no such awareness; still others undergo a powerful experience but eventually reject its religious interpretation.

The first community retains all these outcomes in its assessment. It gives additional weight to careful, experienced reports, including reports whose conclusions it disputes. It distinguishes someone who refused to attempt the practice from someone who attempted it and concluded that its apparent revelations had another explanation. The community may still judge its practice reliable. Nothing in this description settles that question. An inclusive record is not sufficient: all participants might share a source of suggestion, pressure, or interpretive error.

The second community initially accepts exactly the same students as sincere and capable. When outcomes arrive, however, it treats continuing religious assent as a necessary sign of accomplished participation. A student who rejects the interpretation is reclassified as having failed to enter the practice properly. The community then points to agreement among accomplished participants as evidence of its success.

The second community has not merely reached a different conclusion about the same evidence. It has altered the conditions under which a result enters the evidence. Its agreement is partly a product of the eligibility rule. The strength of that agreement cannot be assessed as though disagreement had been allowed to appear in the group being counted.

To isolate the point, suppose one hundred equally prepared students begin, eighty affirm the teaching afterward, and twenty reject it. These numbers are a stipulated illustration, not data about religion. The second community's rule produces unanimous affirmation among its eighty certified practitioners. It would also produce unanimity if only twenty affirmed: the eighty dissenters would disappear from the certified group. The reported unanimity therefore fails to distinguish these very different outcomes of the original inquiry.

The target here is the bare unanimity statistic conditional on selection for affirmation. The fact that eighty of the original hundred affirm, rather than twenty, can remain informative if the original cohort is known. The content and evidential relations of their reports can matter as well. This does not make the eighty affirmations worthless. They may contain valuable individual evidence. Nor does it prove that the twenty are correct. It shows why *unanimity among people selected for affirmation* supplies no additional support merely by being unanimity. A record does not become stronger by making its possible counterinstances ineligible under the description used to announce its success.

The point is elementary as a matter of selection. Its religious application is less elementary because the selection criterion has an initially plausible epistemic rationale. Religious understanding might genuinely require faith. The mistake is to carry that rationale unchanged across two questions: who can acquire the relevant experience, and whether the acquisition process has delivered what it promised. A condition that can sensibly govern the first need not independently justify excluding a result from the second.

## 3. Which claim, which participants?

The selection problem has a close philosophical precedent. C. Thi Nguyen distinguishes exclusion by omission from active discrediting of other voices. More directly, he considers a network chosen for agreement about a diet: agreement guaranteed by the selection rule cannot be treated as ordinary corroboration. The elementary argument above is an application of that point, not a discovery of a new defect in testimony. ([Nguyen, “Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles,” author manuscript pp. 2–3, 7–8](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2018.32).)

The further issue is how to identify improper selection when an access condition is legitimate. Not everyone who begins a discipline completes it; not everyone who once mastered it can still exercise it. “Include dissenters” is too crude a rule. The appropriate record depends on the claim for which it is offered.

Suppose a community announces in advance that its contemplative discipline requires ten years of uninterrupted training. It claims that completing this preparation normally produces a capacity to discern divine presence. Someone leaves after five years because she has experienced nothing. Her case is an outcome of entering the program, but it is not a counterexample to a claim restricted to completing ten years. Excluding her from the latter group can be legitimate even when her departure reflects unbelief. Eligibility to begin does not establish competence at the end.

Now suppose the community uses its record of ten-year completers to tell prospective entrants that undertaking the discipline is a dependable way for people like them to gain religious understanding. The early departures become relevant. What entering is likely to accomplish depends partly on whether entrants reach completion. A practice may reliably reward those who finish while rarely bringing those who start to that point. Success among completers alone does not answer the prospective entrant's question.

This difference is conceptual, not a demand for numerical probabilities in every religious decision. “The discipline succeeds when completed” and “starting the discipline is a dependable route to success” concern different transitions. Evidence for the first can contribute to an argument for the second only alongside information or justified assumptions about completion. Attrition is not proof that the discipline's object is unreal. It is information about the route offered to the prospective participant.

There is also a third claim: people currently exercising an acquired capacity experience what the practice describes. Someone who completed the training but stopped exercising the capacity may not be a counterexample to that claim. Nevertheless, her history remains relevant to the broader claim that training yields stable understanding. A record can therefore appropriately exclude her for one purpose and misleadingly exclude her for another. The error need not involve dishonesty or an incoherent definition of competence. It can consist in moving from a narrow conditional success to a broader invitation without restoring the cases excluded by the condition.

The proposed principle is consequently claim-relative: when deriving additional authority from participation, retain outcomes relevant to the transition claimed, or give reasons adequate to the particular exclusion, supported otherwise than entirely by the agreement that exclusion produces. Duration, preparation, completion, and current exercise can be legitimate distinctions. But a reason to doubt one part of a report need not justify discarding the whole trajectory. If the grounds establish only uncertainty about eligibility, the conclusion must preserve that uncertainty. Continuing assent cannot alone settle whichever classification secures the advertised result.

This formulation also distinguishes delayed success from an indefinitely postponed test. A previously specified ten-year requirement can define an intelligible claim about completers. If the required duration is extended whenever someone completes it without affirming the teaching, the original success claim has not survived. A revised claim may be worth investigating, but the disappointing outcomes remain results of the earlier promise. New evidence can justify new standards; relabeling the past does not establish that the old standards delivered.

## 4. What must be retained?

Three questions about a former participant should be separated. Did this person undergo a relevant course of participation, with this reported result and later judgment? Is her account of that history accurate? Is her interpretation of its significance correct? The selection argument principally concerns the first. It gives no automatic affirmative answer to the other two.

The refusal to dismiss former members as a category is also not new. Stephen Kent argues for checking the contents of their accounts against other evidence, while acknowledging possible deception. That methodological point does not establish their religious interpretations. Here the narrower task is to identify what a particular history can tell us about a particular promise of learning. ([Kent, “The History of Credibility Attacks Against Former Cult Members,” conclusion, pp. 15–16](https://fecris.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Kent-EN.pdf).)

Where available, an assessment should preserve the qualifications established, the participation undertaken, the experiences reported, the subsequent judgment, and the reasons offered for changing it. These are relevant parts of a trajectory. They need not all receive equal weight. A former contemplative might accurately remember an experience but now reject its interpretation because of an unrelated historical mistake. Including that trajectory does not make the historical mistake credible or discredit the experience. Equally, discovering the mistake does not make the person's participation cease to have occurred.

The constraint permits ordinary criticism of testimony. A former participant can misremember, deceive, misunderstand, or lose a capacity. The issue is the support for discounting the report. If the only ground is that a properly formed participant would affirm, and the only ground for that claim is the agreement of those still certified, the explanation depends entirely on the result it manufactures.

By contrast, imagine a teacher who evaluates an apprentice's performance of a prescribed attentional exercise before learning the apprentice's religious verdict. The evaluation might provide a reason to distinguish complete from incomplete performance, provided there is a supported connection between the exercise and the capacity being assessed. Or a witness may demonstrably have performed a different exercise from the one under discussion. Neither finding settles the truth of the doctrine. Each can nevertheless be a relevant reason to restrict a particular comparison without deriving its entire warrant from the agreement produced by that restriction.

The principle requires no universal, religiously neutral examination of competence. Expertise commonly depends on disputed background commitments. A criterion may be internal to a tradition and still have support other than the very filtered convergence now offered as its vindication. Conversely, a detailed doctrinal story about why unbelievers are defective does not solve the problem if all its justification derives from that convergence. What matters is the local dependence of the argument, not whether its vocabulary sounds secular.

Former participants thus have a claim to historical inclusion for appropriate purposes, not a general privilege as interpreters. Their loss of endorsement cannot alone erase their trajectory. Their trajectory cannot alone establish their present authority. This limited conclusion restores information without deciding in advance what an adequate assessment will make of it.

Mixed cases require more than finding some reason independent of agreement. The reason must concern the capacity or condition relevant to the inference being made; there must be evidence that it applies to this person; and the resulting discount must not exceed what the reason supports. Sleep during an exercise can undermine a claim to have performed it continuously. It does not, without further argument, undermine a report of what the teacher instructed. These are constraints on an exclusion argument, not a complete theory of competence or a universal numerical threshold.

Nor does fairness require equal evidential weight for positive and negative outcomes. A capacity might manifest itself before the usual training ends, while its absence before that point shows little. What must remain constant is the claimed achievement and the reason for judging a report relevant to it. Conversely, if competence is unsettled, an assessment may report results for clearly qualified participants. It must then identify that restricted group and explain any further inference to everyone who qualifies. Leaving a doubtful case out of a conclusive verdict is different from establishing that it could not be a counterinstance.

## 5. A departure with a real qualification problem

A public account of leaving a Vipassana course makes the mixed problem concrete. The tradition of S. N. Goenka presents its teaching as nonsectarian; the relevant feature here is its practical route to contemplative understanding. Its published code requires a full ten-day course and adherence to the taught technique. These are ascertainable requirements, not simply a demand to endorse whatever conclusion appears. ([Dhamma.org, “Code of Discipline,” Self Discipline & Commitments; Acceptance of the Teacher and the Technique](https://www.dhamma.org/en/about/code).)

Fix one undertaking throughout this assessment: the official FAQ says that the demanding course is manageable for a person in reasonable physical and mental health who is willing to make a sincere effort and can follow the instructions. It also offers chairs for physical limitations and invites inquiries about suitability. This is a claim about access to training, not a promise of enlightenment within ten days. ([Dhamma.org, FAQ, questions on capability, sitting, and physical conditions](https://www.dhamma.org/en/about/qanda).)

In his 2024 first-person account, Vahe Hovhannisyan describes attending an Armenian course and leaving on day five. He reports a recent leg injury, painful sitting, a successful hour of equanimity, then difficulty with the newly introduced exercise. He also describes becoming distrustful and rebellious and acknowledges a wish to defend himself through his account. This is a published participant history, not an independently established medical or attendance record. ([Hovhannisyan 2024, opening account; day four; departure](https://vahehovhannisyan.com/i-left-vipassana-on-day-five).)

A charitable defender has genuine grounds for limiting the testimony. On the account given, he did not undertake the whole course. His experience cannot settle what completing the prescribed training would have yielded. This restriction has support other than his disagreement: the duration requirement and his own reported departure. A critic who treats him as a fully trained assessor of the technique overstates the case.

But that restriction does not dispose of the fixed undertaking about manageability. Completing the course is the outcome whose attainability is at issue. It cannot also be inserted as an entry qualification for assessing that outcome. Otherwise the claim would reduce to the course being manageable for those who managed it. Keeping the original promise fixed prevents this retreat to a different, much narrower claim.

The recent injury is a harder objection. Unlike departure itself, it is evidence of a possible failure to meet an antecedent condition. It can support a real doubt about whether this participant belonged to the promised group. Yet the existence of some injury does not settle whether his health fell below the relevant threshold. That judgment would require information about the limitation and the accommodations appropriate to the course. The published allowance for chairs also prevents treating every difficulty with floor sitting as disqualifying. None of this establishes that the injury was minor, that an accommodation would have worked, or that the course caused harm.

There is a further complication: leaving is not identical to being unable to continue. Distrusting a method or judging it a poor use of time can explain a voluntary exit from a course that remained manageable. Likewise, becoming rebellious does not by itself show that sincere effort was absent from the outset. Whether the willingness condition requires a sustained attempt is a legitimate interpretive question. But it cannot be answered simply by defining every departure as insufficient willingness. That would again remove the outcome being assessed from the possible evidence.

The warranted result is therefore limited. This account does not establish a failure of the manageability undertaking. It also does not establish that the participant was outside its scope. The health objection is partly supported, the outcome itself admits more than one interpretation, and the report needs corroboration. Those three uncertainties should survive the assessment. A possible independent explanation can block a decisive objection without becoming a demonstrated explanation that cleans the record of all difficulty.

Consider two possible uses of the same case. An assessor could say: the participant did not finish; whether he met the health condition and whether he found the course unmanageable remain unsettled; his case therefore does not decide the undertaking. That is defensible. An assessor could instead exclude him as demonstrably unsuitable, then present the absence of failures among suitable entrants as new evidence for the undertaking. The available record would not support that move. The defect is the unwarranted upgrade from possible disqualification to established disqualification, not the use of a health criterion itself.

The needed further evidence is consequently specific: the participant's condition and effort, what was disclosed and assessed at entry, what practical adjustments were available, and why he left. If that evidence establishes a relevant disqualification, exclusion from this particular comparison is justified. If it establishes eligibility but only voluntary departure for other reasons, the case still need not refute manageability. If it establishes eligibility and an inability to undergo the offered training, the promise faces a genuine difficulty. Retaining the history permits these different judgments; assigning competence from the final verdict prevents them.

This is an application of the proposed rule to an incomplete public record, not a finding that the organization actually excluded this testimony from a success count. Nor have I established that today's published wording governed that particular 2024 course. The undertaking is held unchanged within the analysis; its historical continuity remains unverified. The case therefore tests how a mixed objection should be handled, rather than documenting a completed institutional audit. Its lesson is nonetheless substantive: participation can fail to establish mastery while still raising an unresolved question about the offered route to mastery. Neither trust nor dissent settles which explanation is correct.

## 6. Does the restriction undermine faith?

An objection begins from the value of commitment. A religious life conducted under constant review may never become the kind of life through which religious understanding is possible. If each disappointment triggers another test, the participant's apparent experiment may guarantee failure by withholding the perseverance the experiment requires.

Lara Buchak offers a developed account on which faith concerns commitment to action before further evidence rather than an obligatory inflation of confidence. Her 2012 discussion allows that delaying a decision can be costly and distinguishes investigating for other purposes from making the committed action depend on the investigation. The mere fact that someone declines another search thus need not reveal irrational belief. ([Buchak 2012, III–IV, VI, VIII](https://www.theapj.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Buchak_Can_it_be_Rational_to_have_Faith.pdf).)

That insight leaves the present restriction intact. A person need not interview every former believer before praying again. She may reasonably maintain a commitment through uncertainty. The question is what she may infer if she subsequently cites the experience of participants as confirmation of the practice. A permission not to collect information does not transform the information she did collect into an unselected sample. The practical value of staying does not establish that those who left have nothing relevant to say.

The distinction also applies to evidence that arrives without being sought. Buchak's 2017 defense of steadfastness permits ordinary updating of credences—degrees of confidence—even when commitment sustains a project. She allows that the later perspective can be epistemically superior, and that sufficiently decisive evidence can justify breaking the commitment. Her account therefore does not require immunity to every counterinstance. ([Buchak 2017, author manuscript §§4–5](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9x9649v4/qt9x9649v4_noSplash_6a388f192bbe86349cf26bdd2dd41ad0.pdf).)

The distinction needed here is between degrees of confidence and commitment to action, not a general separation of faith from belief. Buchak's §7 connects faith with maintaining belief under two accounts of that attitude. The additional point is institutional: members may acknowledge uncertainty and continue faithfully while their community's public account of mature practitioners suppresses those who ceased to agree. Personal humility does not automatically correct collective selection.

Nor must preserving evidential standing take the form of continuous confrontation. Reports can be retained and acknowledged without every participant repeatedly reopening every decision. The practice may set periods of undistracted engagement. It may postpone assessment until participants have gained sufficient familiarity. But if the eventual assessment excludes every negative result on the sole ground that it ended in unbelief, postponement has become elimination. There was no possible moment at which sincere failure could count as a result.

## 7. Transformation and direct encounter

A harder objection holds that competence can become intelligible only after transformation. A novice cannot fully anticipate a master's standards. Demanding that every standard be fixed at entry would freeze inquiry at the beginner's level.

The claim-relative principle allows transformation and revised criteria. Its demand is that a change in standards not silently convert failure under an earlier claim into confirmation of that claim. A participant may discover unfamiliar forms of attention, and a community may acquire good reasons for treating them as essential. Those reasons can support a new account of competent participation. They must still explain why the resulting record bears on the particular promise being defended.

Suppose rejecting an interpretation destroys the capacity to experience its object. Even granting that possibility, a person's present inability does not erase what she experienced before rejection or explain away her reasons for revising the interpretation. Loss of current access and irrelevance of a past trajectory are distinct. Which matters depends on whether the claim concerns current exercise, successful training, or the prospects of entering the discipline.

Another objection appeals to direct acquaintance with God. Perhaps the believer does not infer God's reality from the practice's success rate. God is present to her in experience, and the experience itself supplies warrant. The selection problem may then appear irrelevant.

For that person's immediate warrant, it might be. The argument does not establish that every religious judgment must be supported by a comparative survey. It leaves open whether some religious experiences provide basic justification, and whether a sufficiently powerful experience can defeat particular objections. But once the believer adds that the experience of mature practitioners confirms the trustworthiness of the practice, she adds an inference subject to the restriction. The possible sufficiency of one ground does not validate another defective ground.

This scope matters in both directions. A critic cannot establish that an experience is illusory merely by exposing a filtered testimonial record. Equally, a defender cannot repair that record merely by insisting that religious experience could be veridical. What needs defending is the actual evidential route being used.

## 8. What faith may ask of a witness

The proposed constraint is not peculiar to religion. Political movements, therapeutic disciplines, and intellectual schools can dismiss dissent as evidence that the dissenter never really understood. As Nguyen’s analysis makes clear, the broad structure is familiar. Religion makes the distinction between access and retrospective assessment especially consequential because the requirement of prior participation can be philosophically respectable rather than obviously manipulative. Rejecting every such requirement impoverishes inquiry; accepting its unrestricted extension makes inquiry incapable of learning from failure.

The resolution is to preserve a distinction within participation itself. Trust may be needed to begin, patience to acquire competence, and commitment to realize a good. None of these facts alone licenses an evidential policy on which only continued assent can certify that the process occurred. A sincere participant’s rejection can be mistaken, or premature relative to a specified training period. Even so, it remains relevant when the practice appeals to the history of people who began as she did. An assessment of current experts and an invitation to future learners cannot simply inherit the same exclusions. Where a qualification objection is partly supported, preserving the history may require a disputed classification rather than a vote against the practice. An unresolved objection has neither refuted the promise nor become evidence of its success.

Faith can ask a person to act before she knows how the story ends. A claim to have learned from that story cannot count only the endings in which faith survives.
