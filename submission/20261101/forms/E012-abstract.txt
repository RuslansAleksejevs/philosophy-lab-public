Compensation for lost evidence can later be mistaken for proof of what happened. This essay asks what an institution still owes when it has paid fairly but its earlier wrongdoing continues to make that mistake harder to challenge. A municipal fund destroys photographs it promised to preserve. Later, another office misrepresents the fund's decision, despite receiving an accurate explanation. Two cases isolate the difference that matters: the photographs would have provided a usable defence in one case, but would not have made correction easier in the other. Drawing on Gardner's account of substitute performance, I argue that the fund may still owe proportionate help when its breach continues to impair a defence it promised to protect. Such help must keep historical evidence, entitlement to compensation and binding procedural decisions distinct. The duty is limited by adequate substitutes, privacy, third-party rights and the claimant's choices. Legal examples illuminate the argument without establishing a general duty after proceedings close.
